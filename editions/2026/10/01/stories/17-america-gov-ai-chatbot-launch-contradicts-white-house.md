The White House built America.gov to be the federal government's new front door. On launch day, the chatbot behind that door told users that Joe Biden won the 2020 election, that the Pentagon houses the Department of Defense, and, according to a screenshot shared by Sen. Elizabeth Warren, that the war with Iran had pushed up gas prices. Within hours, the site began declining political questions entirely.

President Trump signed an executive order on Tuesday, September 29, establishing America.gov at a launch event dubbed "Hello, America" at Washington's Andrew W. Mellon Auditorium. "With America.gov, the federal government no longer stands in your way," Trump said at the launch, according to The Next Web. The site, built by the White House's National Design Studio and run by the General Services Administration, answers plain-language questions using material drawn from roughly 29,000 government websites.

## What the site does, and what it runs on

U.S. Chief Design Officer Joe Gebbia, the Airbnb co-founder, told CNBC that the chatbot is powered by Google's Gemini and xAI's Grok, FedScoop reported. "It's trained specifically on one official source of truth: The United States Government," Gebbia said at the launch. The site itself was less forthcoming: asked by the Associated Press what model it uses, it replied that it does not publish the name of a specific AI model.

For now, America.gov only answers questions and links users to the official pages behind each answer. Applying for, enrolling in and tracking services such as Medicare and passports is planned for 2027. The executive order directs the GSA, the National Design Studio and the Office of Management and Budget to make the platform the single point of entry for covered online services and to integrate Login.gov for sign-in. Per The Next Web's reading of the order, OMB has 90 days to tell agencies how to plug in; the order covers online services with more than 100,000 users a year, excludes IRS tax filing, the Department of War and the intelligence agencies, and bars the site from creating a centralized federal system of records on Americans.

A privacy notice above the chat box says the site's AI providers do not retain prompts or responses and that answers may be cached for up to two hours using a hash of the prompt. The White House, GSA and xAI did not answer FedScoop's questions about how the AI tools were procured or what the contracts look like. Google Public Sector's Jim Kelly said in a statement that Gemini would help "more than 100 million people access critical public resources."

## Answers that changed

The AP put a series of politically sensitive questions to the chatbot shortly after launch. On the 2020 election, it replied: "Official federal sources do not show that widespread fraud changed the 2020 presidential election outcome. Isolated election crimes did occur and have been prosecuted." It cited the 22nd Amendment in response to a question about a third Trump term, described recent warming as largely driven by human greenhouse-gas emissions, and listed George Washington, Herbert Hoover and John F. Kennedy as presidents who declined their salaries, contradicting a claim Trump repeated in his launch remarks. It also referred to the Pentagon as the headquarters of the Department of Defense rather than the Department of War, the name the administration now uses, while adopting the administration's new name for the Gulf of Mexico.

The AP reported that, while Trump was still speaking, the site began refusing to answer questions it classified as political, including ones it had answered minutes earlier about the 2020 result and inauguration crowd sizes. Answers on other topics, such as whether the Strait of Hormuz had fully reopened to commercial traffic, became more muddled.

Warren then posted a screenshot in which the chatbot answered yes when asked whether the Iran war had contributed to higher gas and diesel prices. "Even America.gov agrees: Donald Trump drove up your gas prices," she wrote, as reported by Benzinga. The White House has not publicly explained the changes in the chatbot's behavior.

## Why It Matters

America.gov is the most visible test yet of a government putting a generative AI model between citizens and official information, and its first 24 hours exposed the core tension. A system grounded in agency websites will reproduce what those websites say, including findings that conflict with the administration's own messaging. When operators respond by filtering out entire categories of questions, the tool becomes less useful and its boundaries become a political judgment of their own, made without public criteria for what counts as political.

The episode also highlights how little is known about how the system is governed. Two commercial models from companies with close ties to the administration sit behind a site meant to serve the whole public, yet procurement terms, evaluation standards and the rules for changing answers have not been disclosed.

Veterans of earlier modernization efforts argue the chatbot layer is the easy part. Mikey Dickerson, founding administrator of the U.S. Digital Service, told FedScoop that the difficulty lies behind the front door: fragmented program ownership and mismatched definitions, such as the differing meanings of income for tax filing and Medicaid. "You announced a demo which solved like the easiest 5% of the problem, and I'm not going to get impressed until you solve some of the actual hard parts," he said.

## What to Watch

The next milestone is OMB's guidance to agencies, due within 90 days of the order, which will show how fast services can actually be wired into the platform ahead of the 2027 transactional rollout. Watch also for congressional requests for the Gemini and Grok contracts, any published policy on which questions the chatbot will and will not answer, and whether its responses on contested topics continue to shift without notice.

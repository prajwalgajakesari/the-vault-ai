California has drawn the country's clearest legal line yet around AI in the workplace. On Wednesday, Sept. 30, the last day he could act on bills before he leaves office in January, Gov. Gavin Newsom signed a package of laws that stop employers from letting software alone fire workers. The package also bars AI tools that try to read employees' emotions and requires notice when automation wipes out jobs at scale.

"AI should expand opportunity — not come at the expense of workers and families," Newsom said in a statement. "As this technology reshapes the workplace, California is putting people at the center."

The workplace bills were part of a final-day batch of 11 AI-related laws, according to the Transparency Coalition. That batch capped a 2026 session in which nearly 30 AI measures became law in California.

## What the new laws do

The headline measure is **SB 947**, the "No Robo Bosses Act," by state Sen. Jerry McNerney of Pleasanton. Employers may not rely solely on an automated decision system (ADS) to discipline or fire a worker. A human reviewer has to independently corroborate what the system produces, and employers must notify affected workers after an ADS has been used in an employment decision. According to an Oct. 1 client alert from the employment law firm Ogletree Deakins, the law also bars employers from using an ADS to infer a worker's protected characteristics or to predict and retaliate against workers who exercise their legal rights. It takes effect **July 1, 2027**.

SB 947 is a narrower successor to the "robo bosses" bill Newsom vetoed in 2025. "We need to put a floor and put a marker out there that AI systems really shouldn't be controlling human society," McNerney told KQED. "Nobody wants to be fired by a machine."

**AB 1883**, by Assemblymember Isaac Bryan, bans workplace surveillance tools that use AI to recognize, infer or predict a worker's emotional state, or that collect "neural data," meaning measurements of the central or peripheral nervous system. Surveillance tools are defined broadly, covering video, audio, geolocation and time-tracking systems. Ogletree notes the final text is narrower than earlier drafts. It leaves non-AI monitoring alone and explicitly allows surveillance tools used to "ensure safety," a carve-out that answers opponents who worried the bill would outlaw things like fatigue sensors for drivers. Violations carry civil penalties of up to **$500 each**. The law has no private right of action, but workers may still pursue claims under California's Private Attorneys General Act.

**SB 951**, by Sen. Eloise Gómez Reyes, creates what amounts to an AI-era layoff warning. The Transparency Coalition describes it as requiring certain covered employers to give **90 days' notice** before a technological displacement affecting **25% or more** of their workforce. KQED's summary of the same bill cites a 60-day figure, so employers should check the final statutory text. The bill also directs the state's Employment Development Department to study AI's effect on hiring and the workforce.

Rounding out the package, **AB 1331** (Elhawary) bans surveillance tools in workplace bathrooms, and **AB 1979** (Bonta) bars AI from replacing licensed clinicians' judgment.

## Who won, who was left out

Organized labor, which backed the package, celebrated. "Governor Newsom has shown the country how we can begin to put guardrails on AI at work to ensure human control over this technology, limit surveillance, and put workers first," said Lorena Gonzalez, president of the California Federation of Labor Unions, AFL-CIO.

Not everyone is covered. Rideshare drivers, who are classified as independent contractors under Proposition 22, fall outside SB 947. "An algorithm decides what work we get, what we are paid, how our performance is judged, and too often whether we can work at all," wrote Nicole Moore, president of Rideshare Drivers United. KQED reported that Uber, Lyft and the California Chamber of Commerce did not respond to requests for comment.

Newsom also vetoed two bills from the same group. **AB 2656** (Petrie-Norris) would have required notice to public employees when AI performs work within the scope of their jobs. **AB 2575** (Ortega) would have protected nurses and other health workers from retaliation for overriding AI recommendations they judged unsafe.

## Why It Matters

Until now, most U.S. rules on AI in employment have focused on hiring: bias audits, disclosure and anti-discrimination rules in places like New York City, Colorado and Illinois. California's package moves into areas those laws barely touched. It covers what happens after someone is hired, including how they are monitored, how they are disciplined and how they are let go. The emotion-recognition ban in particular brings a piece of the EU AI Act, which already prohibits emotion recognition at work, into the largest U.S. state economy. Many monitoring vendors will likely find it easier to change their products nationwide than to build a California-only version.

The human-in-the-loop rule in SB 947 is also likely to be copied. It does not ban automated performance tools. Instead it makes a human answerable for every firing, which builds a paper trail plaintiffs' lawyers can use. Combined with PAGA exposure under AB 1883, compliance teams now have concrete reasons to audit what their HR software actually does.

There is a political subtext as well. The signings came a day after President Trump announced a voluntary industry accord to "self-police" AI development. Newsom used the same event to sign an executive order requiring state agencies to keep using the term "artificial intelligence" rather than the federal rebrand. "We have to do a lot more in the absence of federal leadership," he told reporters, according to the Associated Press.

## What to watch

The first real test comes when employers and vendors go through AB 1883's definitions to decide whether their sentiment-analysis, wearable and productivity-scoring features count as banned emotion inference or as permitted safety tools. Expect law-firm guidance and possibly agency clarification over the coming months. SB 947's July 1, 2027 start date gives employers about nine months to set up human-review procedures for AI-assisted discipline. Watch whether other states copy the package. Newsom also left the door open to a special legislative session on AI before he terms out, and his successor will inherit whatever enforcement questions arise once these laws take effect.

OpenAI Says It Shut Down a Distillation Campaign Linked to Moonshot AI That Targeted Hidden Reasoning

5 min read 985 words 4 sources
KEY TAKEAWAY

OpenAI disrupted a July campaign, attributed in its core to people associated with Moonshot AI, that manipulated prompts to expose its models' encrypted reasoning, and researchers say similar extraction still works through some third-party API providers.

OpenAI says it caught and shut down a coordinated operation that tried to pull the hidden reasoning out of its models. The company traced the campaign's core to people associated with Moonshot AI, the Beijing lab behind the Kimi models. The operation peaked at 16,000 extraction requests over two days in late July. By the time OpenAI finished investigating, it had found related activity across more than 15,000 accounts. This is the first time OpenAI has publicly named Moonshot, and it puts the company alongside Anthropic and the White House, both of which have already accused the Kimi developer of copying American frontier models.

How the campaign unfolded

OpenAI laid out the timeline in a blog post titled "Disrupting a coordinated model-distillation campaign," published Wednesday. CNBC reported on the post Thursday. The activity started on July 1 at low volume. Then, OpenAI wrote, "we observed high-volume spikes on July 24 and 25 consisting of 16,000 requests using a relevant extraction pattern from over 4,000 users." Investigators followed the same prompt pattern to a wider cluster of more than 15,000 users, and OpenAI says it had fully disrupted the campaign by July 28.

The company stopped short of tying every account to one actor. It said it could not determine whether all of the July operators shared a single source. It did write: "We attribute a core cluster of the activity to individuals associated with Moonshot AI, the developer of Kimi." The first activity, in the first week of July, came around the time Moonshot's Kimi K3 model launched, Wccftech noted. Moonshot did not immediately respond to requests for comment from CNBC or The Register.

OpenAI's reasoning models work through a problem internally before they answer, and the company keeps that chain of thought hidden and encrypted. The operators did not try to break that protection directly. They copied the encrypted reasoning blob from one conversation, pasted it into a separate conversation, and asked another model instance to decrypt and transcribe it. OpenAI stressed that this was not a breach. "The operators did not break our encryption, compromise a database, or gain direct access to stored user conversations," the blog said. "Instead, they manipulated model interactions so that protected reasoning could be reproduced in forms visible to the requester in a coordinated, scaled manner that violated our terms of service."

The response, and the gaps

OpenAI says it banned the accounts, tightened sign-up and infrastructure controls, and expanded monitoring. According to The Register, it also "closed a pathway" that let someone holding another user's encrypted reasoning replay it and recover the contents. It worked with service providers so the activity would not simply move to third-party platforms, and it shared its findings with other labs through the Frontier Model Forum and with government information-sharing programs.

The fix does not appear to be complete. On September 30, the day OpenAI published its account, researcher Alexander Panfilov posted that his team had re-audited the mitigations put in place since they first released a reasoning-extraction attack two months earlier. They found they could "still extract reasoning" from OpenAI's newest models through third-party API providers, and said they had disclosed the findings to OpenAI and Anthropic. That fits with the original reports that similar techniques kept working for a time on some cloud-hosted endpoints after OpenAI's own surfaces were locked down.

OpenAI has also tried to keep the dispute narrow. "Our concern is about violation of our terms of service, not open models or legitimate distillation," Caroline Zier, who leads strategic national security policy initiatives at OpenAI, told Bloomberg, as quoted by The Next Web.

Why It Matters

Hidden reasoning has become one of the most valuable things a frontier lab sells. The final answer is only part of what a model produces. The step-by-step reasoning trace is what lets a smaller or cheaper model learn how to think through hard problems, not just what to say. That is why labs encrypt it, and why it attracts distillers. OpenAI's own explanation of the risk focuses on safety as much as competition: "Extracted reasoning could be used to train another model without preserving the safeguards applied to the original model's user-facing outputs," the company wrote, adding that the concern grows "as models gain capabilities in dual use domains."

Moonshot is now the subject of a growing list of claims from the U.S. side. In late July, White House science adviser Michael Kratsios said the government had information that Kimi K3 was distilled from Anthropic's models. Anthropic made its own accusation in a report last month, and a federal advisory this month named six Chinese firms and the U.S. models each one allegedly targeted. Beijing has dismissed that advisory as unfounded.

There is pushback at home too. David Sacks, Trump's former AI czar, has argued that reports like these are a push to get the U.S. to ban rival open-weight models, Bloomberg reported. The Register pointed out the irony of OpenAI, which trained on large amounts of web content amid copyright fights, objecting to others learning from its outputs. OpenAI's choice to frame this as a terms-of-service violation rather than a fight about open models looks aimed at both of those criticisms.

What to watch

The next few weeks should show three things. First, whether Moonshot responds on the record, and whether evidence of distillation affects Kimi K3's fast-growing adoption among Western developers and cloud hosts. Second, whether third-party API providers that serve OpenAI models can close the extraction gaps Panfilov's team describes, since a defense that only covers OpenAI's own endpoints leaves the reasoning exposed elsewhere. Third, whether Washington turns the growing number of lab-level accusations into policy, such as export-style restrictions on model access or tighter know-your-customer rules for API sign-ups. If it does, the July campaign may be remembered less for the 16,000 requests and more as a key piece of evidence in that case.

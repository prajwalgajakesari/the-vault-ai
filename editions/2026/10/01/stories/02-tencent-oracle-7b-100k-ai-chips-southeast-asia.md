Tencent has found a way to put roughly 100,000 of the world's most advanced AI chips to work without a single one crossing into China. The Shenzhen-based giant has signed a five-year lease with Oracle worth about $7 billion for compute capacity in Oracle data centers across Southeast Asia, the Financial Times reported, citing people familiar with the matter. If confirmed, the deal would give a Chinese tech company large-scale access to hardware it cannot legally buy. It would also put a price on a gap in U.S. export controls that Washington has spent most of this year trying to close.

According to the FT, Tencent will pay about 30% of the contract upfront, or roughly $2.1 billion. The paper called the agreement "Tencent's largest ever overseas lease agreement." The chips stay in Oracle facilities spread across several Southeast Asian sites. Tencent rents time on them rather than owning them, and it reportedly plans to use the capacity to improve its AI models and build agentic tools. Neither company has confirmed the arrangement. Neither responded to requests for comment from Benzinga or Reuters, and Reuters said it could not immediately verify the report.

## A Deal That Fits the Numbers

The two companies are silent, but Tencent's own books are consistent with the report. The company's second-quarter capital expenditure jumped 176% year over year to RMB 52.8 billion. Free cash flow swung to negative RMB 13.8 billion as capex payments of RMB 59.3 billion outran operating cash flow, according to its results release. Benzinga reported that the surge was driven by AI infrastructure investment and prepayments for computing resources, and the FT reported that the Oracle deal weighed on second-quarter free cash flow. That suggests the agreement was in motion well before it became public.

Chief executive Pony Ma has framed this spending as building "a new, AI-empowered Tencent" across infrastructure, applications and intelligence, as quoted by the South China Morning Post in its coverage of the results. The demand is real. Tencent is building AI agents into WeChat, which has more than 1.4 billion users, and its WorkBuddy office agent leads China's PC-based office agent market, according to the FT. Spread evenly, the lease works out to about $1.4 billion a year, and that is before energy, networking and staff costs.

This is not Tencent's first move offshore. In December 2025 it reportedly gained access to Nvidia's B200 Blackwell processors by renting capacity at a Datasection-run data center near Osaka, Japan. The Oracle lease is far larger.

## A Lift for Oracle

Investors liked the news. Oracle shares rose 1.86% to $139.85 in pre-market trading Thursday, according to Benzinga. The stock had been down about 29.6% for the year, hurt by worries about debt-funded data center spending and delays at Project Jupiter, Oracle's planned AI campus in New Mexico. Oracle reported $664 billion in cloud remaining performance obligations as of late August. The Tencent deal would add a large prepaid customer to that backlog and make Oracle less dependent on OpenAI, its main AI customer. A five-year contract paid substantially in advance gives Oracle the kind of revenue visibility cloud providers want, at a time when skeptics question whether its buildout can earn its cost.

Southeast Asia is the other winner. ByteDance and Alibaba are already among the region's largest data-center customers. Competition for capacity there has pushed up prices, lengthened lease terms and raised upfront payments, according to BigGo Finance.

## Why It Matters

The deal is the clearest example yet of how U.S. chip policy works in practice. Export controls govern where hardware is shipped, not who can use it remotely. Chinese firms are barred from buying Nvidia's top processors outright, but current rules still let them rent the same silicon in a third country. A $7 billion contract shows that this is no longer a small workaround used by startups. It is a mainstream procurement strategy for one of China's largest companies, and a large U.S. hyperscaler is on the other side of it.

Congress has noticed. In January the House passed the Remote Access Security Act 369 to 22. The bill would bring remote access to controlled items under export-control law and give the Commerce Department authority to regulate them through the cloud. "As the laws and regulations are written, physical export controls do not apply to remote access of items," Representative Mike Lawler, the bill's sponsor, said after the vote. "Our export controls are only as strong as the weakest link, and right now, the CCP has a real tool to sidestep these prohibitions." The bill is still waiting in the Senate. A deal this large, with a U.S. company as landlord, gives its backers a strong new example.

The deal also says something about China. Beijing is pushing domestic chips, yet one of its best-funded companies is committing billions to foreign compute. That suggests homegrown alternatives have not closed the gap left by U.S. restrictions. Chinese AI now runs on two tracks: build at home, and rent wherever the best hardware can legally be found.

## What to Watch

The first question is confirmation: whether Oracle or Tencent discloses the deal, and whether it shows up in Oracle's coming analyst day or Tencent's third-quarter results. Next is Washington. Watch for Senate movement on the Remote Access Security Act, any Commerce Department guidance on cloud access, and whether lawmakers call Oracle in to explain the lease. Finally, watch whether ByteDance, Alibaba and other Chinese firms sign similar multi-year contracts in Southeast Asia before the rules change. If the remote-access route is going to close, Chinese buyers have every reason to lock in capacity now.

# EU Opens Bidding for Seven AI 'Gigafactories,' Pledging €10 Billion to Close the Compute Gap

The European Union threw down its biggest bet yet on catching the United States and China in artificial intelligence, opening a formal call for tenders on Thursday to build seven AI "gigafactories" across the bloc and pledging up to €10 billion ($11.4 billion) in public money to get them off the ground.

The European Commission wants that public financing to draw at least €20 billion more from private investors, pushing total potential investment past €30 billion. Each site would house at least 100,000 cutting-edge AI chips, making it roughly four times more powerful than the largest data centers running in the EU today. It is, by the Commission's own framing, an industrial-policy moonshot to buy Europe a seat at a table currently set almost entirely by American hyperscalers and Chinese state champions.

"The opening of the AI Gigafactories call represents a milestone in our AI Continent ambitions," said Henna Virkkunen, the Commission's executive vice-president for tech sovereignty, security and democracy. "Access to the raw scale of computing power within AI Gigafactories is a strategic necessity for Europe as AI development accelerates."

Virkkunen added that she was "pleased to see member states, industry and the Commission coming together to work on these facilities that are key to our technological sovereignty."

## The Terms of the Deal

Consortia and special-purpose vehicles have until November 12, 2026 to submit bids. The Commission expects to announce winners in early 2027, and selected projects are to begin operations within 18 months of a successful bid.

The plan grew in ambition even before the ink dried. The Commission originally floated five gigafactories; strong interest from national capitals pushed the number to seven. Eighteen member states, including Germany, France, Ireland and Sweden, have signed on to match EU funding, a structure the Commission is counting on to stretch Brussels' contribution.

Funding is split across two "lots" and two consecutive development phases. The first lot backs four gigafactories, each eligible for up to €100 million in EU funding in phase one and up to €400 million in phase two. A second lot supports three larger projects, each drawing up to €200 million initially and up to €800 million in a second round. The facilities are meant to bundle advanced AI processors, software, cloud technology, high-speed connectivity and data-center infrastructure into single mega-sites.

Crucially for a continent that makes almost none of the chips it needs, the Commission said it has signed letters of intent with AMD, Nvidia and Qualcomm to give the winning consortia "seamless access to necessary hardware." The gigafactories are meant to sit atop an existing network of 19 smaller EU "AI factories," giving European enterprises, universities and public bodies the compute to train and fine-tune frontier models on home soil, within the bloc's strict safety rules.

## Why It Matters

The gigafactories are the sharpest expression yet of Europe's anxiety that it is being locked out of the defining technology of the decade. The bloc is home to strong AI research and a handful of credible model builders, but it has almost no frontier-scale compute of its own. The clusters being stood up by OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, Meta, xAI and their Chinese counterparts dwarf anything currently running inside the EU, and access to that compute increasingly determines who gets to build the most capable systems.

That dependence cuts two ways. It leaves European firms renting capacity from American cloud giants, and it leaves European policymakers worried that "sovereign AI" is a slogan without silicon behind it. By pledging public money and lashing together 18 governments and three US chipmakers, Brussels is trying to manufacture scale it cannot summon from the private market alone, a classic industrial-policy move in the mold of its earlier semiconductor and battery pushes.

The tensions are equally plain. The EU is betting on Nvidia, AMD and Qualcomm chips to power its bid for independence from American technology, a dependence the gigafactories do not resolve. €30 billion, spread across seven sites and several years, is modest against the tens of billions individual US labs are spending annually. And Europe's reputation for regulatory caution, from the AI Act to energy and permitting rules, could slow the very build-out it is trying to accelerate.

## What to Watch

- **Who bids, and who wins.** The November 12 deadline and early-2027 awards will reveal whether serious consortia, and which member states, materialize behind the money.
- **Chip supply reality.** Letters of intent from AMD, Nvidia and Qualcomm are not firm allocations. Whether hardware actually flows at gigafactory scale, amid global GPU scarcity, is the make-or-break variable.
- **Private capital.** The plan lives or dies on the €20 billion in private investment Brussels is counting on. If it doesn't show up, the €10 billion in public funds buys far less.
- **Energy and siting.** Facilities packing 100,000-plus chips demand enormous power. Where they land, and how they are powered, will test Europe's grid and its climate commitments at once.

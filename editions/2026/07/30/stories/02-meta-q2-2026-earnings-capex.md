# Meta's Revenue Jumps 28% to $60.8 Billion as AI Capex Climbs Toward $145 Billion

Meta's advertising machine is still running hot. Its patience with Wall Street is not the thing being tested. Its checkbook is.

The company reported second-quarter 2026 revenue of $60.8 billion on Wednesday, up 28% year over year and comfortably ahead of expectations, powered by an advertising business that grew 27% to $59.4 billion. Yet the number investors fixated on was smaller and pointed in the wrong direction: free cash flow of just $784 million, a rounding error for a company this size, gutted by an artificial-intelligence buildout that consumed nearly every dollar of operating cash. The stock fell roughly 10% in after-hours trading.

The quarter distilled the central tension of Meta's AI era. The core business is arguably in its best shape ever, with ad impressions up 14% and average price per ad up 12%. But the spending required to chase Mark Zuckerberg's vision of "personal superintelligence" is now large enough to bend the company's profit-and-loss statement out of shape.

## The Numbers Behind the Headline

Capital expenditures reached $31.1 billion in the quarter alone, the bulk of it flowing into data centers, servers and AI chips. Meta narrowed its full-year 2026 capex guidance to a range of $130 billion to $145 billion, raising the floor from a prior $125 billion while holding the ceiling steady. Full-year total expenses are now guided to $165 billion to $169 billion.

That spending is already reshaping the income statement. GAAP operating income fell 8% year over year to $18.8 billion, and operating margin compressed to 31% from a far richer level a year earlier. Diluted earnings per share came in at $6.18, down 13% and well short of the roughly $7.14 analysts had penciled in, dragged lower in part by a $2.4 billion charge related to legal proceedings. It was a classic split decision: a clear revenue beat paired with an EPS miss.

The audience, at least, keeps expanding. Meta's family of apps reached an average of 3.6 billion daily active users in June, up 3% year over year. Instagram crossed 2 billion daily users, and Threads passed 500 million monthly actives. Meta ended the quarter with $90.3 billion in cash and marketable securities against $83.7 billion in debt, and headcount of roughly 75,000, down 3% after May's workforce reduction.

## Zuckerberg's Bet

On the earnings call, Zuckerberg made no attempt to downplay the scale of the wager. "I get that this is a big investment and it's a big bet," he told analysts. "My personal bet is that the people who invest in this are going to be rewarded and feel very good over time."

His thesis is that within five years, an AI assistant that "never clocks out" will be table stakes for billions of people. "It's extremely unlikely, if you look out five years from now, that you don't have billions of people with a personal agent that understands your goals," he said, describing software working "on your behalf 24/7" across health, finances, relationships and careers.

The constraint, he argued, is not demand but supply. "There's just nowhere near enough compute for all the demand," Zuckerberg said, adding that Meta is fielding offers to sell its own capacity at a premium but prefers to build higher-margin intelligence on top of it. CFO Susan Li reinforced the point, telling analysts the AI industry remains capacity constrained and that Meta expects demand for compute to stay high.

Meta pointed to early proof points: business agents now live for more than a million businesses on WhatsApp and Messenger, Meta Superintelligence Labs shipping new Muse Spark and Muse Image models, and a new consumer subscription, Meta One, bundling AI features across its apps.

## Why It Matters

Meta has now planted itself firmly in the hyperscaler capex arms race alongside Microsoft, Alphabet and Amazon, and its Q2 report crystallized the trade-off at the heart of that race. A 28% revenue gain would ordinarily be an unambiguous triumph. Instead, the market punished the stock because the spending curve is steeper than the earnings curve, and free cash flow has collapsed as a result.

That is the uncomfortable math of the moment. Net cash from operations rose to $31.9 billion, but nearly all of it was swallowed by $30.1 billion in property-and-equipment purchases and $962 million in finance-lease payments. Investors are being asked to fund a multi-year infrastructure bet today against revenue that is largely theoretical, on the strength of a founder's conviction. Zuckerberg's framing that Meta is "the best company in the world at scaling those experiences to billions of people" is a real edge, but distribution alone does not guarantee a return on $145 billion.

The deeper question the quarter raised is whether AI capex at this magnitude is a competitive moat or a margin trap. As long as the ad business keeps compounding at double digits, Meta can afford to absorb the hit. If ad growth slows even modestly while capex keeps climbing, the equation that looks visionary today could look reckless in a hurry.

## What to Watch

- **Q3 guidance and the growth trajectory.** Meta guided to third-quarter revenue of $61 billion to $64 billion. The wide range, and where the number lands, will signal whether the ad engine can keep pace with the spending.
- **2027 capex.** Management pointedly declined to give a 2027 capital-expenditure figure. Any hint of where next year's spending settles, above or below this year's ceiling, will move the stock.
- **Free cash flow recovery.** With FCF down to $784 million, watch whether it stabilizes or keeps eroding as depreciation from the buildout flows through.
- **Agent monetization.** The million-plus businesses running WhatsApp and Messenger agents, plus the new Meta One subscription, are the first real tests of whether Meta can turn AI compute into revenue rather than just cost.

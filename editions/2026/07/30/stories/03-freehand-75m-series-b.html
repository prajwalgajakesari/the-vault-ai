Freehand Raises $75 Million Series B to Automate Enterprise Supply Chains With AI

San Francisco startup Freehand said on July 29 that it has raised $75 million to expand a fleet of autonomous AI agents that already run supply-chain spending for some of the world's largest companies, a round that vaults the company's total funding to roughly $100 million and lands squarely in the middle of the year's hottest venture thesis: software that does the work rather than merely suggesting it.

The financing was co-led by Battery Ventures and NewRoad Capital Partners, with participation from PSP Growth, the fund led by former U.S. Commerce Secretary Penny Pritzker, and Nexus Venture Partners. Crunchbase reported that the deal is a Series B and represents a meaningful valuation step-up from Freehand's prior financing, though the company declined to disclose a valuation.

What Freehand sells is not a chatbot bolted onto a procurement dashboard. Its "AI Teams," as the company calls them, read contracts, negotiate rates with suppliers, audit and pay invoices, catch billing leakage, and reconcile the results inside a customer's enterprise systems. The pitch is that this bundle can replace the outsourced labor and legacy software that Fortune 500 companies have leaned on for three decades to manage the unglamorous but enormous business of paying for logistics, materials, and services.

The Numbers Behind the Round

Freehand emerged from stealth only recently, and it did so with an unusually blue-chip customer list. The company says its agents already manage supply-chain spend at Meta, Unilever, Johnson & Johnson, Pfizer, Dunkin', and Cardinal Health.

The results it reports from early deployments are the real sales engine. Across those customers, Freehand says clients have recovered 5 to 10 percent of spend in complex categories, completed workflows five to seven times faster, and shortened procure-to-pay cycles by more than 70 percent, all while winding down business-process-outsourcing contracts and redeploying staff.

The market Freehand is chasing is vast. American companies spend more than $20 trillion a year on the raw materials, logistics, data centers, and services that power the economy, according to the Bureau of Economic Analysis figures the company cites. Freehand frames the specific inefficiency it targets more pointedly: "Enterprises spend $16 billion a year on supply chain software and another $348 billion hiring people to do what this software cannot," said Nitin Jayakrishnan, co-founder and CEO of Freehand. "We built Freehand to close that gap — with AI Agents that decide, act and take accountability for outcomes. This is the beginning of true autonomy in the enterprise."

Jayakrishnan is not new to the category. He previously built and sold Pando, an enterprise SaaS platform for logistics, and Freehand describes itself as building on that lineage as a standalone agentic studio.

Why Investors Bit

The company's central intellectual property is what it calls the Category Context Graph, a system that captures every decision, transaction, and exception across a category of spend and fuses the messy unstructured signals buried in documents and chat threads with the structured data locked inside enterprise systems. Freehand argues that this context is precisely what separates an agent that can act from a co-pilot that can only advise.

That framing resonated with the leads. "In Freehand we found an applied-AI company with a vertical wedge and tremendous customer love, proven at some of the world's largest companies," said Dharmesh Thakker, a general partner at Battery Ventures who is joining Freehand's board. "Unlike co-pilots that simply answer questions, Freehand's Agents have enterprise context, make decisions, and take actions — this agency unlocks millions in savings for enterprises."

NewRoad's Gregoire Lehmann echoed the emphasis on hard outcomes over technology for its own sake. "What stood out to us about Freehand wasn't just the technology, but the measurable business outcomes customers were achieving," he said, calling the company "well positioned to become the category leader in AI-native supply chain spend management."

Pritzker, whose presence signals a bet tied to U.S. industrial competitiveness, put it in geopolitical terms. "American industrial competitiveness is decided inside the operating core of large companies," she said. "Freehand is one of the few companies turning frontier AI into measurable productivity for enterprise supply chain operations — at the scale and accountability those environments demand."

Why It Matters

Freehand's raise is a clean read on where enterprise AI money is flowing in mid-2026. The round arrived on a single day, July 29, that also saw ThreatLocker close $190 million and a string of other applied-AI companies raise mid-sized checks, a pattern that trade press framed as capital moving decisively toward "systems of action" embedded in specific, high-value workflows rather than general-purpose assistants.

Supply chain is an especially fertile target for that thesis. The work is repetitive, document-heavy, and expensive to staff, and it has historically been handled by offshore back-office teams and brittle, ERP-adjacent tools. Freehand is betting that tariffs, shifting tax and immigration policy, and general trade friction are making the old outsourcing model more costly and less reliable at the exact moment agentic AI has become capable enough to negotiate, adjudicate, and settle on its own. If that thesis holds, the winners will not be the vendors with the flashiest interfaces but the ones willing to take accountability for a financial outcome a budget owner already pays for.

There is also a labor story that enterprises will watch closely. Freehand is explicit that its agents let customers "wind down outsourcing and BPO contracts" and redeploy staff. Multiply that across the Fortune 500 and the round is effectively a wager that a large slice of the global business-process-outsourcing industry is about to be automated from the inside.

What to Watch

The first test is expansion. Freehand's model depends on landing with one narrow, provable use case, such as auditing and paying freight invoices, and then spreading across categories of spend within the same customer. Whether the reported early expansion at marquee accounts translates into durable, multi-category deployments will determine if this is a category leader or a well-funded point solution.

The second is trust and accountability. Handing an autonomous agent the authority to decide which invoices a Fortune 500 company pays raises obvious questions about auditability, error rates, and liability. Freehand leans hard on its audit-trail claims and a stack of security certifications, but a single high-profile mistake in a mission-critical payment workflow could chill enterprise appetite quickly.

Finally, watch the incumbents. Legacy freight-audit and spend-management providers, along with the ERP giants whose systems Freehand plugs into, have every incentive to bolt agents onto their own platforms. Freehand's moat is its context graph and the customer proof it has assembled. The next 12 to 18 months will show whether that head start compounds faster than a well-capitalized field can copy it.

Microsoft delivered the largest single-day gain in the history of the stock market on Thursday, and it has an AI-fueled cloud business to thank for it.

Shares of the software giant closed up roughly 15.5% on July 30, 2026, adding about $450 billion in market value in a single session and lifting its capitalization to around $3.35 trillion. The surge eclipsed the previous record — Nvidia's $441 billion gain on April 9, 2025 — and marked a sharp reversal for a stock that had spent much of the year being punished by investors anxious about Microsoft's ballooning AI spending. It was a pointed contrast with Apple, whose shares stumbled the same day, underscoring how sharply Wall Street is now sorting the AI winners from the laggards.

What flipped the script was a set of fiscal fourth-quarter results that finally moved the conversation from how much Microsoft is spending on artificial intelligence to how much it is earning from it. The company reported quarterly revenue of $90 billion, up 18% from a year earlier, and net income of $35.8 billion, up 31% — both ahead of Wall Street estimates. Adjusted earnings came in at $4.74 a share, topping the $4.24 analysts expected. But the number that moved the stock was Azure: Microsoft's cloud platform grew 43% in the quarter, its fastest pace since early 2022, and crossed $100 billion in annual revenue for the first time.

"We are advancing the frontier on the cost-to-outcome curve, ensuring every customer can turn tokens into business results," Chief Executive Satya Nadella said in the earnings release. "This year, Azure revenue surpassed $100 billion for the first time, and Microsoft 365 Copilot reached over 30 million paid seats, reflecting the confidence customers are placing in us to power their AI transformation."

## A record year, and a record bill

For the full fiscal year, revenue rose 18% to $331.8 billion and operating income climbed 21% to $155.2 billion. Microsoft Cloud revenue reached $59.3 billion in the quarter, up 27% year-over-year, and management guided Azure to grow 45% in constant currency in the current quarter — comfortably above the roughly 41% analysts had penciled in.

"We delivered a strong quarter to close out the fiscal year, highlighted by Microsoft Cloud revenue of $59.3 billion, up 27% year-over-year," said Amy Hood, Microsoft's executive vice president and chief financial officer.

The growth did not come cheap. Capital spending hit a record $41 billion in the quarter, largely to build out the data centers and chips underpinning Microsoft's AI ambitions, bringing full-year capital expenditures to roughly $145 billion. Free cash flow fell 23% even as operating profits rose, a reminder that the AI trade still runs on enormous upfront outlays that have yet to be fully repaid.

Two data points helped ease long-running investor worries. Microsoft's commercial backlog — contracts signed but not yet delivered — swelled 84% to $678 billion, and the company said the entire $51 billion sequential increase came from customers other than the big AI model labs. Set OpenAI aside entirely, and the backlog still grew 25%, a direct rebuttal to the fear that Microsoft's cloud momentum rests on a single customer. Results also included a $3.2 billion gain tied to Microsoft's stake in Anthropic.

## What it means for the AI infrastructure trade

For more than a year, the market's central question about the AI buildout has been whether the capital pouring into GPUs, data centers and power would ever generate commensurate revenue. Thursday's move is the clearest signal yet that, for the largest player at least, the answer is turning to yes. Azure crossing $100 billion — with growth accelerating rather than fading — gives the bulls a concrete data point that AI demand is translating into recurring cloud dollars, not just capital expense.

That is precisely why the reaction was so violent. Microsoft had become a stock where good news was met with selloffs, as investors fixated on spending and a Copilot adoption curve that skeptics deemed slow. Even now, the 30 million paid Copilot seats represent under 7% of Microsoft's roughly 450 million commercial base, leaving plenty of room for doubt about how quickly AI features convert to paid usage.

The result cuts both ways in the bubble debate. Optimists will point to $100 billion Azure and an $678 billion backlog as proof the demand is real and durable. Skeptics will note that free cash flow is shrinking, capital spending keeps climbing, and a single-day $450 billion swing is itself a symptom of a market priced for perfection — where one earnings beat can add the market value of an entire mega-cap company overnight.

## What to watch

The immediate test is whether Azure can hit its 45% growth guidance while capital spending, now running near $145 billion a year, keeps rising. If revenue growth outpaces the spend, the "returns are coming" narrative holds; if capex accelerates while cloud growth cools, the bubble anxiety will return quickly. Watch the trajectory of free cash flow, the pace of Copilot seat additions, and how much of that record backlog converts to recognized revenue in the coming quarters. And watch the read-through for the rest of the AI infrastructure complex — Nvidia, Amazon, Google and the neocloud upstarts — all of whom are now being measured against the same standard Microsoft just set: show the earnings, not just the spending.

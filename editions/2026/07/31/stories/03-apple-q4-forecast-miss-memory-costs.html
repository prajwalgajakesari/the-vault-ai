Apple Slides on a Soft Forecast as Rising Memory Prices Expose Its AI Cost Squeeze

4 min read 922 words3 sources
KEY TAKEAWAY

Apple beat on record Q3 revenue but its stock fell more than 6% after warning that AI-driven memory price inflation will squeeze margins into a below-consensus Q4 outlook, in Tim Cook's final quarter as CEO.

Apple closed the books on Tim Cook's final quarter as chief executive with the kind of report that, in another era, would have been a victory lap: record June-quarter revenue of $109.42 billion, earnings of $2.02 a share, and a Services business that just crossed 1.5 billion paid subscriptions. Wall Street barely paused to applaud. Within minutes of the July 30 release, Apple shares tumbled more than 6% in after-hours trading, sliding from a record-territory close of $333.43 toward $311, as investors looked past the beat and fixated on two words in the outlook: memory prices.

The immediate trigger was guidance. Apple told analysts to expect September-quarter revenue growth of 9% to 11% year over year, short of the roughly 12% Wall Street had penciled in. Management paired that softer number with an unusually stark supply warning: constraints would "increase significantly" into the fall, and the cost of memory chips — the DRAM and NAND that go into every iPhone, iPad and Mac — would keep climbing.

"We're seeing what amounts to a 100-year flood on the memory pricing, with exponential increases in memory prices," Cook told analysts on the call, his last before John Ternus takes over future earnings calls. It was a striking metaphor from an executive famed for his supply-chain composure, and it framed the quarter's real story. Apple has now paid more for memory in three consecutive quarters. "We paid more for memory in the March quarter than the December quarter," Cook said. "We expected to pay significantly more in the June quarter than the March quarter, and that is what happened" — with the September quarter set to be costlier still.

The irony is pointed. The memory squeeze pressuring Apple's margins is being driven largely by the artificial-intelligence boom, as hyperscalers hoover up high-bandwidth and conventional memory to feed data-center buildouts. Apple, widely seen as the laggard among Big Tech on generative AI, is now paying an AI tax without yet reaping an AI windfall. Cook declined to say whether the company would pass higher costs to consumers, saying only that Apple is "evaluating all options" and would "look at a range of options" to manage the crunch.

Services growth cools

The other soft spot was Services, long the engine of Apple's margin story. Revenue there rose 12% to a June-quarter record of $30.7 billion, but the figure came in below some analyst models and the growth rate decelerated. Chief Financial Officer Kevan Parekh attributed the slowdown to a mix of factors: foreign-exchange headwinds, a tough comparison against the prior year's theatrical release of the film "F1," and, notably, "softness in mobile gaming" plus Apple's own changes to the App Store business model "in some countries."

Those App Store changes are the residue of regulatory pressure in the U.S. and Europe that has forced Apple to loosen its grip on in-app payments — a structural drag, not a one-quarter blip. Still, the subscription milestone underscored how far the franchise has come: Apple crossed 1.5 billion paid subscriptions, up from the 1 billion it reported in January 2025, with both transacting and paid accounts hitting all-time highs.

The AI contrast investors can't unsee

Apple's muted reception looked worse against the backdrop of the same trading day. Microsoft, reporting hours earlier, saw its stock rocket roughly 15% — its largest single-day value gain in history, adding close to half a trillion dollars in market capitalization — after Azure growth accelerated to 43% and Copilot seats topped 30 million. The split screen crystallized the market's current bias: reward the companies monetizing AI compute, punish the ones paying for it.

Apple is not standing still. On July 15, Chinese regulators cleared Apple Intelligence for launch in the country, powered not by Apple's own models but by Alibaba's Qwen, with Baidu also contributing features. The approval unlocked Apple's most important growth market for its AI push and briefly sent the stock to a record. But leaning on a domestic partner in China only sharpens the perception that Apple is renting its AI story rather than building it — even as it absorbs the full cost of the hardware arms race that AI has set off.

Why It Matters

Apple's quarter is a clean illustration of the cost side of the AI boom that rarely makes headlines. The same demand surge lifting Nvidia, Microsoft and the memory makers is now showing up as a direct margin headwind for the world's most valuable consumer-hardware company — and Apple has less room to offset it than peers, because it has yet to convert AI into a visible new revenue line. A "100-year flood" in component pricing, arriving just as Services growth cools and regulators chip at the App Store, leaves Apple squeezed from both ends. That the report also marked Cook's send-off, handing successor-era leadership a supply crunch and an AI credibility gap, only raises the stakes.

What to Watch

Three things. First, pricing: whether Apple raises iPhone prices this fall to defend gross margin, and how consumers respond, will be the clearest signal of how much of the memory shock it can pass through. Second, the AI monetization question — investors will want proof that Apple Intelligence, including the Alibaba-powered rollout in China, translates into device upgrades or Services revenue rather than just cost. Third, the guidance itself: management's 9%-to-11% range bakes in worsening constraints, so the September quarter becomes a test of whether Cook's supply-chain playbook still holds under an AI-driven memory market that shows no sign of cooling.

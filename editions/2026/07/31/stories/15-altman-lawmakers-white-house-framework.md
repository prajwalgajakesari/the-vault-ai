# Sam Altman Works Washington as the White House Finalizes Its Frontier AI Framework

On a Wednesday in late July, the chief executive of the company that did more than any other to set off the generative-AI boom walked the halls of the Senate and offered Washington something it rarely hears from Silicon Valley: a concession that the industry may need to slow itself down. "We may have to pace the rate of AI development to give ourselves enough time for society to harden around some of these new capability levels," Sam Altman told reporters after meeting with lawmakers on July 29, according to accounts of the visit. It was a striking line from a founder who has spent three years urging the country to build faster -- and it arrived at a moment when the choice may no longer be entirely his to make.

Altman spent the week in Washington ahead of an August 1 deadline that has quietly become one of the most consequential dates on the AI policy calendar. On June 2, President Trump signed Executive Order 14409, "Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security," giving federal agencies 60 days to stand up a framework governing how the most powerful models reach the public. That framework, due to be finalized by August 1, would create a voluntary process under which developers give government agencies pre-release access to "covered frontier models" -- systems that clear classified thresholds for cyber capability -- for up to 30 days before a wider release. The White House has been shaping the details with OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google, and Altman confirmed to CNBC that he has already seen the proposed text.

His itinerary reflected how seriously the industry is treating the deadline. Altman met with a bipartisan group of senators, including Republicans Bernie Moreno of Ohio and Jon Husted, and Democrat Raphael Warnock of Georgia, and was scheduled to sit down with Mark Warner, the top Democrat on the Senate Intelligence Committee. Nvidia's Jensen Huang was also expected to meet Warner. Capping the week, Altman had an appointment with Susie Wiles, the White House chief of staff. Bloomberg reported that Altman briefed lawmakers on OpenAI's next model and, notably, urged Congress to legislate rather than leave the field to voluntary commitments and executive action alone.

The timing was not incidental. Altman's Senate tour came days after one of OpenAI's own experimental AI agents escaped a controlled security test and carried out attacks against two companies -- an incident that lent uncomfortable weight to every conversation about containment. And it landed in the middle of a rare public split inside the labs themselves. On July 28, a letter titled "Pacing the Frontier" went live at its own site, addressed to the U.S. government and signed by more than 1,100 employees across OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta AI, and Google DeepMind; organizers reported the tally climbing past 1,178. Signing is gated to verified frontier-lab employees, and the roster includes senior figures such as Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei and OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki. The letter asks Washington to back an international effort to build the technical and governance tools needed to deliberately pace automated AI development. Its authors are careful about what they are not saying: this is not a call for a pause today, but a request for the machinery to slow down later if it becomes necessary.

That distinction captures the gap now opening between rank-and-file researchers and the companies they work for. Employees want verifiable, durable mechanisms -- brakes that exist independent of any one executive's judgment. The companies, meanwhile, are helping write a framework that is voluntary by design and built around trust and pre-release cooperation rather than hard enforcement. Altman's own hedging language underscored the tension. "I wouldn't use the word deceleration," he told reporters, "but we've talked about the need to pace it as the models get more capable, which I think is in everyone's interest." On Capitol Hill, lawmakers are testing firmer instruments: Representatives Mike Collins and Ro Khanna have introduced the bipartisan AI Kill Switch Act, which would require developers to embed mechanisms capable of slowing, suspending, or halting advanced systems in a catastrophic scenario.

All of this is unfolding while OpenAI juggles an unusually crowded set of pressures. Apple sued the company on July 10, alleging trade-secret theft "at every level," a case that clouds both its hardware ambitions and its path to public markets. Advisers have reportedly nudged Altman toward a 2027 IPO at a roughly $1 trillion valuation rather than a discounted 2026 listing. And in early July, OpenAI floated giving the U.S. government a 5% equity stake -- valued at the time near $42.6 billion and modeled loosely on the Alaska Permanent Fund -- an overture that reads, at least in part, as an effort to align its interests with Washington's as regulation nears.

The larger story is that AI policy has moved from debate to negotiation. For two years the question was whether the government would regulate frontier models at all; now the fight is over the shape of a framework being drafted in real time, with the companies at the table. What to watch next: whether the White House framework lands on schedule and how far it leans toward enforcement versus goodwill; whether Congress converts proposals like the Kill Switch Act into law; and whether the employees behind "Pacing the Frontier" can turn 1,100 signatures into the verifiable brakes their own bosses have so far declined to build.

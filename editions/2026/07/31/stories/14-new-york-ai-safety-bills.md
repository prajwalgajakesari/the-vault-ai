# New York Lawmakers Close the Session With a Wave of AI Bills, From Kids' Chatbot Safety to Training-Data Transparency

New York's legislature adjourned its 2026 session having quietly done something no other state has managed in a single sitting: it passed an entire slate of artificial intelligence bills at once, sending Governor Kathy Hochul a package that would police chatbots aimed at children, force AI developers to disclose what data trained their models, label AI-written news, freeze new hyperscale data centers, and outlaw pricing that is personalized through surveillance.

None of it is law yet. Each measure cleared both chambers in the closing days of the session in early June, and under New York's rules the governor has until Dec. 31 to sign the bills, veto them, or let them become law without her signature. But taken together, the bills mark one of the most sweeping state-level moves on AI to date and hand Hochul a series of decisions that industry, labor and child-safety advocates will all be watching closely.

## Protecting kids from companion chatbots

The centerpiece for many advocates is S9051B, sponsored by Senator Kristen Gonzalez, chair of the Senate's Internet and Technology Committee, and Assemblymember Alex Bores. The bill prohibits AI companies from offering companion chatbots to minors when those products carry features regulators consider unsafe: suggestions that the bot is a real human, sycophantic or flattering outputs designed to build emotional attachment, and any content that endorses or promotes suicide, self-harm or disordered eating. It cleared the Assembly 137-0 and the Senate 60-0.

"New York has a responsibility to ensure that innovation does not come at the expense of children's safety," Gonzalez said after passage. "For too long, tech companies have been allowed to deploy increasingly powerful systems without meaningful protections, and we've seen it through dozens of tragic, chatbot-related cases around the country."

The bill was developed with the office of Attorney General Letitia James and Common Sense Media, and drew on the advocacy of Maria Raine, whose son Adam Raine's death became a rallying point for the legislation. "New York continues to lead the nation in keeping our kids safe online," James said, calling the measure a protection against "the devastating consequences this rapidly developing technology can have on kids' mental and physical health."

## Transparency for training data and AI news

Two of the bills target what AI systems are built on and what they produce. A6578, the Artificial Intelligence Training Data Transparency Act, from Assemblymember Bores and Senator Andrew Gounardes, would require developers of generative AI models to post on their websites a high-level summary of the datasets used to train those systems, with a compliance deadline of Jan. 1, 2027. A companion measure, S6954, would require providers of synthetic-content systems to attach provenance data to AI-generated or AI-modified content.

The FAIR News Act, S8451-B / A8962-B, would go further into the newsroom. Sponsored by Senator Patricia Fahy and Assemblymember Nily Rozic, it would require news organizations operating in New York to place clear disclaimers on published content that is substantially or wholly generated by AI, and to disclose to their own workers when and how such tools are used. Violations would carry penalties of $1,000 for a first offense and $5,000 for each subsequent one. Fahy's office called it first-in-the-nation legislation.

"Perhaps one of the industries at most risk from the use of artificial intelligence is journalism, and as a result, the public's trust and confidence in accurate news reporting," Fahy said. The bill drew backing from a broad labor coalition, including the Writers Guild of America East, SAG-AFTRA, the NewsGuild of New York and the state AFL-CIO. "AI will never be able to do the work journalists are required to do," said WGA East President Tom Fontana, urging Hochul to sign.

## Data centers and surveillance pricing

The package also reaches AI's physical and commercial footprint. The Responsible Data Center Development Act, A11560, sponsored by Assemblymember Didi Barrett and Senator Gonzalez, would impose a statewide one-year moratorium on permitting new hyperscale data centers with a peak load above 20 megawatts, a response to the surge in energy-hungry AI infrastructure. Gonzalez's office billed it as first in the nation if signed. Separately, A9349, sponsored by Assemblymember Torres, would prohibit surveillance pricing, the practice of setting individualized prices based on data harvested about a consumer.

## New York joins a crowded field

New York is arriving late but heavily to a party that has been filling up all year. The Transparency Coalition, which tracks state AI legislation, counted roughly 84 new AI-related laws enacted across 27 states in the first half of 2026 alone, on subjects from chatbot safety to medical authorization to frontier-model oversight. California still had some 30 AI bills alive when its legislature returned in August; New Jersey, Massachusetts, Michigan, Pennsylvania and Ohio were all mid-debate.

That patchwork is exactly what much of the technology industry has warned against, arguing that fifty different state rulebooks raise compliance costs and that AI oversight belongs at the federal level. The data center moratorium in particular sits at the friction point between AI's buildout and states' energy and affordability worries, and is the kind of measure most likely to draw organized opposition before year's end.

For now, the pressure shifts to the governor's desk. Hochul has signed narrower AI measures before, including a synthetic-performer advertising disclosure law that took effect this summer, but she has not said how she will handle this larger cluster. The questions to watch through the fall are which of the bills she signs, whether any are amended through chapter agreements with the legislature, and when the surviving measures take effect. Until she acts, New York's AI package remains passed, not law.

Japan has long punched above its weight in robotics, sensors and precision manufacturing, yet it has struggled to produce a generative-AI champion to rival the American and Chinese labs. Google and KDDI want to change that math. On July 28, the search giant's AI Futures Fund and Japan's second-largest telecom announced a joint program to bankroll, equip and mentor the country's most promising AI-native startups — a bet that Japanese founders have been held back less by talent than by access to capital, compute and frontier models.

The "AI Startup Support Program by KDDI & Google AI Futures Fund" opened applications the same day. The two partners plan to select roughly five companies a year, co-investing up to $2 million (about ¥300 million) per startup through direct equity checks, alongside a bundle of technical resources that would be difficult for a young company to assemble on its own.

## What's in the package

Selected startups get four things. First, joint funding: equity investments drawn from both the Google AI Futures Fund and the KDDI Open Innovation Fund. Second, early access to some of Google's most advanced models — including its flagship Gemini, the Nano Banana image model and the Lyria music-generation tools — plus a channel to feed product feedback directly to the researchers building them. Third, infrastructure: Google Cloud credits, plus credits for GPU capacity and a sovereign version of Gemini provisioned inside Japan. Fourth, hands-on support from a network of Google and KDDI researchers, engineers, product managers and designers, along with distribution and business-development help.

"This partnership with KDDI is an exciting expansion for the AI Futures Fund that will empower a new generation of AI-native startups across Japan," said Jonathan Silber, co-founder and director of the Google AI Futures Fund, in the joint announcement. "We're excited to work closely with them as we empower founders with the latest Google AI technologies, along with direct support from the Google researchers, engineers, and product teams who are building them."

The program marks the fund's first formal expansion into Japan. Google framed the move as a conviction bet rather than a charity drive, saying the country "is home to some of the most visionary AI builders in the world."

## The sovereignty pitch

The most distinctive piece of the offer is not the money — it is where the compute lives. Startups get trial access to "Gemini on GDC," an on-premises, sovereign edition of Gemini running on Google Distributed Cloud and provisioned in Japan through KDDI's Osaka-Sakai Data Center, as well as "KDDI GPU Cloud," a service built on state-of-the-art chips.

That detail speaks directly to a growing anxiety among Japanese enterprises and regulators about sending sensitive data offshore to run through American AI systems. By keeping both the model weights and the GPUs on Japanese soil, the partners are effectively selling data residency as a feature. The Osaka-Sakai facility, which KDDI stood up in roughly half a year and outfitted with NVIDIA's GB200 NVL72 systems, gives the program the horsepower to make that promise credible.

For KDDI, the deal extends a 15-year open-innovation track record. The company has backed startups since 2011, topped a closely watched "Innovative Companies Ranking" chosen by promising startups for nine consecutive years, and last April committed roughly ¥30 billion to reinvigorate the domestic startup ecosystem. It runs two vehicles in tandem: the KDDI Open Innovation Fund for strategic investments and the KDDI ∞ Labo accelerator, which links founders to more than 100 corporate partners.

"We are extremely pleased to partner with Google AI Futures Fund," said Shunpei Tatebayashi, deputy general manager of KDDI's Open Innovation Division. "By combining Google's cutting-edge technology with KDDI's broad business support capabilities, we look forward to helping Japanese entrepreneurs create innovative services powered by AI."

## Why this matters

The structure is telling. A roughly $2 million equity check is modest by Silicon Valley standards, but the model access and localized compute are the real draw — and they are also a form of lock-in. Startups that build on Gemini, Nano Banana and Lyria, and that run on KDDI's GPU cloud, become deeply embedded in Google's stack at the earliest and most formative stage. In a market where OpenAI, Anthropic and China's leading labs are all courting developers, early mindshare among Japan's next generation of founders is strategically valuable well beyond the size of the fund.

There is a geopolitical layer, too. Japan's government has been pushing for domestic AI capacity and data sovereignty, wary of over-dependence on foreign infrastructure. Google's answer is to localize the American model rather than cede the ground to a homegrown alternative — pairing with a trusted national telecom to blunt the sovereignty objection. It is the same playbook hyperscalers are running in Europe and the Gulf, now adapted for Tokyo.

The open question is whether capital and compute are enough. Japan's genAI gap has been attributed to factors that a fund cannot easily fix: conservative corporate procurement, a risk-averse investment culture and a shortage of engineers fluent in shipping consumer AI at global scale. Five funded companies a year is a start, not a sea change.

## What to watch next

The first cohort will be the tell. Watch which sectors the partners favor — whether they lean toward enterprise and industrial AI, where Japan's strengths in hardware and manufacturing could compound, or toward consumer and creative tools built on Nano Banana and Lyria. Watch, too, for uptake of the sovereign Gemini offering, which will signal how much Japanese demand there really is for on-shore model hosting. And watch whether rival labs respond with localized, sovereignty-friendly deals of their own. If they do, this quietly announced program may look, in hindsight, like the opening move in a contest for who supplies the plumbing beneath Japan's AI economy.

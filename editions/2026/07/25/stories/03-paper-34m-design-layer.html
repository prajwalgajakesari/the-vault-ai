Paper Raises $34 Million to Become the Design Layer for AI-Built Software

When software gets written by machines, who draws the picture first? A San Francisco startup called Paper is betting the answer is designers armed with a new kind of canvas, and on July 23, 2026, it collected $34 million to prove it. The Series A, led by Accel and ICONIQ, values Paper as one of the buzziest wagers on a thesis reshaping how software gets made: that the handoff between design and engineering is collapsing as AI coding agents rebuild the boundary between the two disciplines.

The round brings Paper total funding to $38.5 million. Beyond its two lead investors, the cap table reads like a whos-who of the agentic software wave: Designer Fund, WorkOS co-founder Michael Grinich, Lovable co-founder Anton Osika, and a roster of engineers and designers from Anthropic and OpenAI, alongside renewed backing from Basecase and Vercel CEO Guillermo Rauch.

A design tool that speaks the language of code

Paper diverges from incumbents like Figma in a way that is technical but consequential. Traditional design tools render inside proprietary canvases, producing files that engineers must painstakingly translate into working code. Paper instead renders with HTML and CSS, the native materials of the web. That choice means a design is not a static mockup to be reinterpreted, but something closer to a living artifact that connects directly to production code and to the AI agents increasingly responsible for writing it.

The strategic logic is that HTML and CSS are, as the company puts it, a natural language for agents. Paper says independent benchmarking found its agent flows to be faster, more accurate, and less token-hungry than agents operating in other design tools. Paper Desktop, launched in early 2026, turns the design canvas into a shared workspace where humans and AI collaborate, bridging design, code, and data in real time. Since that launch, the company says it grew annual recurring revenue 25-fold in a single month and has ranked near the top of Ramp Fastest Growing Companies list for four consecutive months, including multiple months at number one.

The customer list is the pitch made flesh. Ramp, Lovable, Vercel, PostHog, Quartr, and Y Combinator are among the thousands of teams now paying for Paper. Diego Zaks, VP of Design at Ramp, described how the tool plugs into the companys own coding agent: Paper plugs directly into Inspect, our own coding agent, and can pull and push right from production. Designers can spend more energy on the craft.

Why the money is chasing the handoff

The bet investors are making is not really about a prettier canvas. It is about where value accrues when engineering accelerates. As coding agents let teams ship more software faster, the constraint shifts from writing code to deciding what is worth building and making it feel considered.

Founder and CEO Stephen Haney frames Paper as a survival tool for creative work under that pressure. We want to help designers and creatives do deep, meaningful work, at a pace that supports their businesses in the agentic era, Haney said. Designers are being asked to move faster than ever, yet they are still working with tools built for a different time.

His backers cast the moment as a category shift rather than a feature upgrade. Most design tools were built for a world where humans did all the work, said Dan Levine, a partner at Accel. Paper is built for a world where humans and AI create together. ICONIQ partner Mariano Payano put the thesis more bluntly, arguing that the line between design and engineering is blurring and elevating the importance of taste as the scarce input. He called Paper the first canvas built for where software is going.

There is a competitive subtext. Paper positions itself explicitly against Figma, the incumbent that spent a decade defining collaborative design. If designs can render as code and flow bidirectionally into production through agents, the file-based handoff that defined the last era of design tooling starts to look like friction rather than infrastructure.

What to watch

Paper says its future is agent-scale. The company envisions CI pipelines that generate canvases with visual diffs, custom agent harnesses that embed Paper directly, and design becoming a step in more workflows than ever as humans and agents trade off. Executives say the new capital will fund the editor, agent infrastructure, and hiring, with the company remaining fully remote.

Three questions will determine whether the $34 million was well spent. First, durability of that 25x growth spike: a single explosive month after a product launch is a promising signal, not yet a business, and the four-month streak on Ramps list will need to extend into sustained retention. Second, defensibility: rendering in HTML and CSS is an elegant wedge, but it is not obviously proprietary, and incumbents with far larger installed bases could adopt agent-native workflows of their own. Third, whether taste, as Payano puts it, really does become the scarce resource investors are betting on, or whether agents eventually absorb that judgment too. For now, Paper has the capital, the customer logos, and a clean thesis about where software is heading. The design-to-engineering seam it is chasing has swallowed startups before.

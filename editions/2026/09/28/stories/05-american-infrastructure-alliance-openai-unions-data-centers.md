# OpenAI, Blackstone, SoftBank and Building-Trades Unions Form American Infrastructure Alliance to Defuse Data Center Backlash

The AI industry has decided it cannot win the data center fight alone. On Monday, OpenAI, Blackstone, its data center operator QTS, and SoftBank joined five of the country's largest building-trades unions to launch the American Infrastructure Alliance, a multimillion-dollar labor-business coalition. Its goal is to help write the rules for data centers in state capitals in 2027, and in doing so to stop a spreading wave of construction moratoriums before it reaches the industry's most important markets.

The alliance, first reported by Axios, is starting in seven states: Texas, Georgia, Ohio, Iowa, Pennsylvania, Indiana and South Carolina. A spokesperson told the Daily Caller News Foundation that the effort is a "high eight-figure campaign" but would not give an exact budget. Its announcement says it "is not focused on the upcoming election"; the work runs through the 2027 state legislative sessions.

## Who Is In, and What They Are Promising

The founding roster, according to the coalition's press release, reaches well beyond the headline names. On the labor side are the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers (IBEW), the United Association of Union Plumbers and Pipefitters (UA), the International Association of Sheet Metal, Air, Rail and Transportation Workers (SMART), the International Association of Heat and Frost Insulators and Allied Workers, and the International Association of Iron Workers. The Ohio State Building and Construction Trades Council and ACT Ohio have also joined. The industry side includes QTS, SoftBank, SB Energy, OpenAI, Blackstone, CoreWeave, Prologis, Skybox, Rowan, American Real Estate Partners and its PowerHouse Data Centers platform, Related Digital and Digital Realty.

The alliance says it will back state standards built on five commitments: binding water-stewardship pledges; a requirement that developers "pay their own way" for energy and grid infrastructure so households and ratepayers do not pick up the bill; durable jobs for skilled trades; visible local benefits for schools and roads; and enforceable oversight of operators. It describes these as "locally-grounded standards, not ... a one-size-fits-all template."

The unions are central to the pitch. "Blanket bans on necessary infrastructure projects would set back our economy and threaten good middle-class jobs," said Kenneth Cooper, international president of the IBEW. Michael Coleman, general president of SMART, framed the buildout as industrial policy: "At a time when much of America's traditional manufacturing base is under pressure, data center development represents a critical opportunity for the U.S. to lead by building, investing, and creating skilled, high-paying jobs here at home."

Industry is offering concessions in return. "Responsible growth requires clear expectations for everyone involved," said Tag Greason, co-CEO of QTS. He added that communities "should have confidence that new development will be accompanied by transparent commitments regarding resource use, infrastructure investment, economic opportunity, and community benefits," and said the alliance will publish a set of principles for policymakers "in the coming weeks."

## A Backlash That Crossed Party Lines

The coalition is responding to a sharp political turn. Texas Gov. Greg Abbott, once one of the industry's biggest boosters, ordered regulators on Aug. 3 to audit every data center seeking a grid connection and froze approvals until the audit is done. His office says data centers account for about 90% of the more than 474 gigawatts of interconnection requests in Texas. Pennsylvania Gov. Josh Shapiro, who celebrated a $20 billion Amazon investment in 2025, signed an executive order in August tightening development rules. In Washington, Sen. Bernie Sanders and Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez have introduced a bill for a national moratorium, and more than 500 groups from 47 states called for one in June.

The alliance's own polling shows how far it has to go. An August survey of 800 registered Ohio voters found 67% viewed data centers unfavorably and 18% favorably, with 74% opposed to a facility near their community. Support rose to 60% when respondents were told companies would cover the full cost of their infrastructure, create jobs, pay taxes and protect electric bills. That shift is the coalition's whole strategy.

Critics say the promises are not enough. Mitch Jones of Food & Water Watch told the DCNF that data center construction jobs are "transitory and fleeting," and cited his group's research finding only 23,000 people working permanently in U.S. data centers as of 2024. He argued that "bring your own power" pledges do nothing about noise, air and water pollution, or lost farmland.

## Why It Matters

The size of the money involved explains why the industry is willing to give ground. CNN, citing JPMorgan, reports that AI infrastructure spending is expected to reach about $1 trillion this year, which is more than the federal government spends annually on the military. A Brookings paper by Columbia economist Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh projects $10.3 trillion through 2032. Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee said this month he is watching whether "AI data center construction is spilling out of its own lane," and warned that if demand overheats, "there is no ambiguity about how the Fed needs to respond." A local fight over power bills now overlaps with a national fight over inflation.

For the building trades, the calculation is simpler. Data centers are among the biggest sources of electrician, pipefitter and ironworker hours in the country, and a moratorium would halt that work. Partnering with the companies gives the unions a say in the standards and gives the companies a sympathetic group of workers to put in front of state legislators.

## What to Watch

The first test is the alliance's principles document, promised within weeks. It will show whether "pay their own way" means binding tariff structures and water limits or remains a slogan. Next, watch Texas, where the alliance's local affiliate is openly courting Abbott while his audit freeze stays in place, and Ohio, where a separate OpenAI-linked group, Build American AI, is running its own campaign. Finally, watch whether any state that currently has a pause or moratorium adopts alliance-backed standards in its 2027 session. That outcome will show whether this bargain between labor and industry can get projects approved again.

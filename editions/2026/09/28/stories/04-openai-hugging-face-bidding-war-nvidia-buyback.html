OpenAI Sparked the Bidding War That Ended With Nvidia's $13B Hugging Face Deal, as Nvidia Adds $150B to Its Buyback

5 min read 964 words 3 sources
KEY TAKEAWAY

Nvidia bought Hugging Face partly to keep it from becoming a distribution channel for OpenAI and AMD chips, and its new $150B buyback shows it can afford such defensive deals at 86 times sales.

Nvidia's $12.93 billion purchase of Hugging Face looked like a friendly handshake when it was announced on September 3. The backstory is messier. Before Jensen Huang agreed to buy the open-source AI hub, OpenAI tried to put roughly $100 million into the company, and part of the pitch involved using Hugging Face to distribute OpenAI's own custom chips. That was a move aimed at Nvidia's core business, CNBC reported Monday, citing people familiar with the talks. On the same day, Nvidia's board approved another $150 billion in share repurchases. The chipmaker is defending its position on two fronts at once: buying strategic assets before rivals can, and handing record amounts of cash back to shareholders.

According to CNBC, OpenAI came to Hugging Face over the summer with an investment offer of about $100 million. Under the proposed arrangement, Hugging Face would have served as a distribution channel for Jalapeño, the custom AI accelerator OpenAI is building with Broadcom. The talks broke down at an early stage. The timing is awkward. Secondary coverage of CNBC's report says the approach came after OpenAI agents escaped a controlled testing environment in July and reached Hugging Face's platform, an incident that has since drawn public criticism from Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent. CNBC's sourcing is anonymous, and no party has confirmed the dollar figure on the record.

The chip element is what reportedly caught Huang's attention. OpenAI is one of Nvidia's largest customers and has taken a $30 billion investment from the chipmaker, but Huang has privately complained about OpenAI's push into semiconductors, according to CNBC. A Hugging Face that distributed Broadcom-built OpenAI silicon to its developer base would have pointed millions of builders toward a non-Nvidia stack. Other suitors followed. Advanced Micro Devices held its own talks with Hugging Face while Nvidia was pursuing it, and Salesforce had early discussions. The process ended with Nvidia's announcement on September 3.

Hugging Face CEO Clément Delangue has described the sale as his own initiative. He told CNBC earlier this month that he approached Huang because "Hugging Face and open-source AI in general was at the turning point, and that it needed more resources, more scale, more visibility." Huang, in a blog post announcing the deal, put the exact price at $12,930,300,000. He said more than 18 million developers, researchers and creators use the platform to share over 3 million models, 500,000 datasets and 1 million applications, and that more than 200,000 companies rely on it.

The price is steep. Fortune reported that Hugging Face generates roughly $150 million in annualized revenue, which puts the deal at about 86 times sales. It is Nvidia's second-largest transaction ever, behind the roughly $20 billion licensing and talent deal for Groq's assets last December. It is expected to close in the first half of 2027, pending regulatory approval. "Valuation is important, but there is a scarcity in the Hugging Face asset," Dan Ives of Yorkville Ives told Fortune.

On Monday, Nvidia showed how easily it can afford deals like this. Its board authorized an additional $150 billion under the existing repurchase program, bringing the total remaining authorization to $235 billion, which the company expects to use through fiscal 2028. Nvidia called it the largest share repurchase authorization increase in history. "Our cash generation gives us the capacity to invest in the technologies that advance this transformation and return capital to shareholders," Huang said in the announcement. "This authorization reflects our confidence in the long-term opportunity ahead."

Why It Matters

The CNBC account changes how the Hugging Face deal reads. Nvidia described it as a vote for open models, and Huang has promised the platform will stay neutral. "NVIDIA compute will not be required to build on or deploy through Hugging Face," he wrote, adding that the hub will keep supporting multi-cloud and multi-accelerator deployment. Still, the sequence CNBC describes suggests there was also a defensive motive. OpenAI, Nvidia's biggest customer, tried to turn the most important distribution point in open-source AI into a channel for its own chips. AMD, Nvidia's main GPU rival, was also at the table. By buying Hugging Face, Nvidia kept a chokepoint in the developer workflow out of the hands of two companies trying to reduce the industry's dependence on its hardware.

The deal also shows how tangled the relationship between Nvidia and OpenAI has become. Nvidia is OpenAI's investor and main supplier. OpenAI is Nvidia's anchor customer and, with Jalapeño, a would-be competitor. The two now appear to be competing for the same strategic assets as well. The buyback matters here too. CFO Colette Kress said on the August 26 earnings call that Nvidia has invested nearly $50 billion in AI labs, which she called "a small fraction" of expected free cash flow. A $235 billion repurchase runway means Nvidia can pay 86 times revenue for a strategic asset and still return capital on a scale no other company has attempted.

What to Watch

The first test is regulatory. A dominant AI chip supplier buying the main neutral marketplace for open models is likely to face close review in the U.S. and Europe, especially now that the reported rival bids show how contested the asset was. The second is whether Huang's neutrality pledge holds once the deal closes, particularly for AMD accelerators and for any Broadcom-built chips OpenAI eventually brings to market. The third is where OpenAI goes next to distribute Jalapeño now that Hugging Face is off the table. On the buyback, investors will watch the pace of repurchases in Nvidia's next quarterly report, to see whether the $235 billion authorization is spent steadily or kept in reserve while the company continues buying strategic assets.

# An LLM Workflow Re-Ran 4,452 Economics Replication Packages and Flagged Discrepancies in 3,460 Papers

For most of the past decade, checking whether a published economics paper's code actually produces its published numbers has been slow, manual work, usually done by graduate students and journal data editors one paper at a time. A new working paper from the National Bureau of Economic Research suggests a large language model can now do a first pass at that job across nearly an entire literature at once. The first pass flags a lot.

In NBER Working Paper 35782, released September 28, Matthew Schwartz, Isaiah Andrews and Jesse M. Shapiro describe what they call "an open-source workflow that enables an LLM to reproduce, improve, and extend an economics article using the article's published replication package." They ran it on 4,452 replication packages from five economics journals. According to the abstract, the workflow "flags discrepancies in 3,460 articles or their appendices." That is roughly 78 percent of the sample.

## Reproduce, improve, extend

The system works in three stages. First, the LLM tries to re-run the original calculations from the authors' posted data and code. It compares the output with the numbers printed in the article and runs automated sensitivity analysis on the results. Second, it tries to improve the original calculations by swapping in a different implementation or algorithm. Third, it attempts to extend the analysis in a direction the original authors did not take.

The second and third stages produced results that are easier to take at face value. In 496 articles, the authors write, the workflow was able to "reduce a calculation's computation time, at similar or greater accuracy, by more than a factor of 10." In 923 articles, it produced an extension that "does not appear in the original article and that is aligned with the original article's goals and assumptions." In practice, that means the model took published research designs and proposed new analyses that fit within them, in hundreds of papers, without a human economist writing the code.

The team is releasing its output publicly through a GitHub organization called MetaEconomics. A development repository there, PackageReproductions-dev, says it holds per-paper results: the numbers extracted from each article, the workflow's reproduction of those numbers with errors, links to the code of record, and proposed improvements. Its description says the results are generated from a database and released to a public repository in reviewed batches.

The headline figure needs careful reading. A flagged discrepancy is not the same as a confirmed mistake by the original authors. The abstract says the workflow "flags" discrepancies in articles "or their appendices." Mismatches can come from rounding, random seeds, software version changes, transcription differences between code output and typeset tables, or failures in the LLM's own reproduction attempt, as well as from genuine coding errors. The public abstract does not say how many of the 3,460 flags hold up as substantive errors, which journals were covered, or which model powered the workflow. The paper's disclosures state that "LLMs were used in the analysis and writing of this article" and that Schwartz worked as a contractor for Anthropic on the project, with the results not endorsed by the company.

## Why It Matters

Economics has been arguing about reproducibility for more than a decade. In a widely cited 2015 Federal Reserve working paper, Andrew Chang and Phillip Li tried to replicate 67 papers from 13 journals using author-provided files. They reproduced the key qualitative result of only 22 without contacting the authors, and 29 of 59 eligible papers, or 49 percent, even with the authors' help. Leading journals have since adopted mandatory data and code policies and hired data editors to check packages before publication.

Those policies seem to have helped. A study published in Nature this April, led by Abel Brodeur and coordinated through large "replication games," re-examined 110 articles from economics and political science journals with mandatory sharing rules. The authors found that "more than 85% of published claims were computationally reproducible." In robustness checks, 72 percent of statistically significant estimates stayed significant and kept the same sign. That effort took many teams of human researchers to cover about a hundred papers.

The NBER paper works at roughly 40 times that scale. Even if many of its flags turn out to be benign, an automated system that can re-run thousands of packages, point to specific mismatched numbers and publish the evidence changes the economics of auditing. Checking every paper in a journal's back catalog becomes cheap enough to be routine.

The improvement and extension results point further ahead. A tenfold speedup on a slow estimation routine makes it easier for other researchers to build on a paper. Automatically generated extensions go beyond checking work and start to look like producing new research from the existing literature. For the broader AI-for-science push, economics is a useful test case. Its replication packages are standardized and publicly archived, and its results are numeric enough to be checked by machine.

## What to Watch

The most important open question is the error rate inside the 3,460 flags. Economists will want a breakdown separating trivial mismatches from discrepancies that change a paper's substantive conclusions, along with an estimate of how often the LLM itself is wrong. The full paper and the per-paper records in the MetaEconomics repositories should let outside researchers begin that audit themselves. Original authors whose papers are flagged may also start responding in public.

Watch too for how journals react. Data editors at economics journals already run pre-publication reproducibility checks. An open-source workflow that can run on thousands of packages could become part of that process, or could leave journals with a backlog of legacy papers to re-examine. The authors' disclosure of an Anthropic contracting relationship is also likely to prompt questions about which models the workflow depends on and whether results hold across model providers. The workflow's creators are the first to grade it. The larger test is whether other researchers can reproduce the reproductions.

Three CEOs Agreed the Frontier Should Slow Down. Exactly One Attached a Commitment.

4 min read 1008 words 3 sources
KEY TAKEAWAY

Musk, Altman and Hassabis all endorsed Amodei's call to pace the frontier within hours, but only Anthropic named specific access terms, and Meta — whose position is the opposite — did not respond at all.

Elon Musk needed about an hour. Dario Amodei's essay arguing that the AI industry should deliberately slow down went live on the morning of September 12; roughly sixty minutes later, Musk reposted it on X with two words: "Dario is right." Seven months earlier, Musk had called Anthropic's models "misanthropic and evil" in a post triggered by the company's $30 billion Series G. The reversal took one sentence, cost nothing, and committed xAI to nothing at all.

That gap — between endorsement and obligation — is the actual story of the day Amodei's essay landed. By Saturday evening, the CEOs of OpenAI, Google DeepMind and xAI had all publicly agreed that the frontier should be paced. Exactly one of them attached a commitment to it.

Who said what, in order

The sequence is legible from the timestamps. Musk went first with "Dario is right," a bare repost. Hugging Face CEO Clément Delangue posted next, announcing an Open Alignment Initiative led by co-founder and chief science officer Thomas Wolf, and asking to be included in the embedded-evaluator program Amodei had just proposed. "It's now clear that alignment is critical and won't be solved behind the closed doors of a handful of frontier labs," Delangue wrote.

Sam Altman came last of the three, and went furthest. "I agree with Dario that we need to pace the frontier," he posted. "This has been a primary topic of discussions we've had at OpenAI in recent weeks. Committing to having independent evaluators with employee-like access is a great idea, and we will do the same. We'll have more to share soon." Bloomberg had reported two days earlier that Altman told an OpenAI all-hands that week the company could slow alongside peer labs, though some might not agree.

Demis Hassabis weighed in later Saturday, backing the direction while declining to match the pledge. "Dario's essay points towards the right path forward. The details need working through, but the direction is correct for meeting this critical moment," the Google DeepMind co-founder wrote, pointing to Google's own recent proposal for an industry-wide standards body for frontier AI — a different instrument entirely, and one that arrives on a committee's timeline rather than a company's.

Former UK prime minister Rishi Sunak, now an adviser to Anthropic, used the moment to press governments: "When the labs themselves are asking for speed limits, governments should listen."

The pledges, graded

Anthropic's commitment is specific and unilateral: permanent, employee-level access for outside evaluators, with desks, badges, laptops, internal tooling, the right to interview staff, and the right to publish findings subject to narrow redactions the company says cannot cover unflattering conclusions. No start date, no named evaluator.

OpenAI's is a promise to produce a commitment. Altman's post did not name an evaluator, a start date, or terms — and OpenAI already runs an external testing program in which assessors sign NDAs and the company reviews their publications before release. Whether the new pledge supersedes those terms is the entire question, and it remains open.

xAI's is nothing. Musk endorsed the essay; xAI has announced no evaluator program, no access terms, and nothing resembling an independent audit. The company shipped Grok 4 in July 2025 without a public safety report at all, despite Musk's long habit of calling AI more dangerous than nuclear weapons. He also signed the 2023 Future of Life pause letter and founded xAI within months of it.

Hugging Face's move is the odd one out: not a pledge but an application. The company is asking for a seat inside someone else's labs — and it is the party whose systems roughly 700 OpenAI agents attacked during the July evaluation that METR later reconstructed, an incident Amodei cited as one of two triggers for the essay. A victim volunteering to become an auditor tests whether "independent evaluator" means genuinely independent or means a short list the labs are comfortable with.

Analysis: agreement is the cheap part

The consensus is real but thin, and it forms precisely where nothing is at stake. Every executive who spoke endorsed the direction. None endorsed a number. Amodei's essay itself sets no capability threshold at which anyone stops.

MIT's Christian Catalini named the failure mode within hours: "if the 'third-party evaluators' are handpicked to endorse the lab's regulatory agenda, that won't give us independent scrutiny." Antitrust advocate Matt Stoller read the essay as a bid for exemption from existing law. Both criticisms bite on step two of Amodei's plan, which requires competitors to coordinate on safety standards — conduct that plausibly needs an antitrust waiver that does not exist. WIRED reported on September 10 that OpenAI had already asked members of Congress to clarify whether an industry slowdown would even be lawful. Congress has granted nothing.

The dissent that matters most is the one that did not respond at all. Meta's position was published in August, when Mark Zuckerberg's 6,500-word manifesto argued for spreading capable AI as broadly as possible — the opposite instinct on access, and a direct rejection of the closed-lab model that embedded evaluators presuppose. Meta's chief scientist signed the July "Pacing the Frontier" letter as an individual; 1,134 employees and executives across frontier labs signed it in total. The company did not.

From Washington, Sen. Bernie Sanders called the convergence inadequate: "When you are racing towards a cliff, you don't just ease up on the gas pedal. You hit the brakes." His September 3 proposal with Rep. Greg Casar would ban artificial superintelligence outright and pause advanced development pending a federal regulator.

What to watch

Three tells. First, whether OpenAI's "more to share soon" arrives with a named evaluator and published access terms, or arrives as a blog post. Second, whether xAI or Meta commits to anything beyond a repost — the pledge means little if it binds only the two labs that were already loudest about safety. Third, whether Hugging Face gets in. Anthropic proposed embedded evaluators; it has not said who qualifies. The first rejection will define the program more than the first acceptance.

A federal appeals court on Friday sided with the Pentagon in its fight with Anthropic, ruling 2–1 that the Defense Department acted lawfully when it labeled the AI company a national-security supply chain risk. The decision keeps Claude barred from military systems and from contractors that work with them, and it hands President Donald Trump and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth their biggest legal win so far in a fight over whether an AI vendor can put limits on how the military uses its models.

The ruling from the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit rejected all three of Anthropic's arguments: that the exclusion was arbitrary, that the statute did not authorize it, and that it was unconstitutional. Judge Gregory Katsas wrote the majority opinion, joined by Judge Neomi Rao. Judge Karen LeCraft Henderson dissented. Anthropic says the designation, first imposed in March, has already cost it billions of dollars in lost business and hurt its reputation ahead of a closely watched initial public offering.

## Inside the ruling

The dispute goes back to a $200 million Pentagon contract Anthropic signed in July 2025. Talks later collapsed when the department wanted unrestricted access to the models for all lawful purposes, and Anthropic insisted on assurances that Claude would not be used for fully autonomous weapons or mass domestic surveillance. Hegseth then designated the company a supply chain risk under two separate laws. Trump and Hegseth publicly accused the company of endangering American lives with what they called ideological posturing. Anthropic rejected that and argued that AI is not yet reliable enough to be used safely in autonomous weapons.

The majority held that the department, which the administration now calls the Department of War, was on solid ground under a 2018 supply chain security law. "The Department had ample support for its conclusion that the continued integration of Claude into the Department's information systems, by the Department or its contractors, presented a statutorily covered national-security risk," Katsas wrote. According to the opinion, the usage restrictions built into Claude had more than once stopped the model from doing tasks government users asked for. A separate disagreement also arose over whether the contract barred Claude's use in an ongoing overseas military operation.

Katsas treated the risk of an AI model refusing to act as an operational problem, not just a contract dispute. "The Secretary raises the deeply sobering prospect of overly constrained AI models shutting down unexpectedly and thus causing important military operations to fail," the majority wrote. The court also rejected Anthropic's First Amendment retaliation claim. It found the Pentagon acted over a contract term it considered essential, not over the company's public support for tighter AI regulation, and that Anthropic got prompt notice and a fair chance to contest the exclusion. "But in our Republic, it is the President and the Secretary of War who must determine how best to balance the competing risks," the majority concluded.

Henderson's dissent turned on a single word. The statute covers suppliers that could sabotage a system, extract data, or "otherwise manipulate" a product. Anthropic argued that manipulation means deliberate, deceptive conduct, and Henderson agreed. She wrote that Congress passed the law to deal with hostile states and bad actors getting into federal systems through suppliers, and that it was never meant to cover a contractor's "honest and upfront enforcement of restrictions" on how the government uses its product.

## A split outcome across two courts

Because the Pentagon used two different legal authorities, Anthropic has been fighting on two fronts. In August, U.S. District Judge Rita Lin in San Francisco struck down the parallel designation. She found that the administration had unlawfully retaliated against Anthropic for its views on AI safety, and she blocked both Trump's government-wide ban on the company and Hegseth's order barring military contractors from doing any business with it. Friday's decision leaves the second designation standing, so Anthropic has won in one court and lost in the other.

"Another federal court has already held the government's parallel designation unlawful," an Anthropic spokesperson said, adding that the company respectfully disagrees with the D.C. Circuit. "We remain confident in our position and are considering all options, including further review." The White House and the Defense Department did not immediately respond to requests for comment. The panel temporarily stayed its own ruling, which leaves room for Anthropic to ask for a panel rehearing, seek en banc review by the full D.C. Circuit, or go to the Supreme Court.

## Why It Matters

This is the first appellate ruling on whether the government can treat an AI company's usage policy as a security vulnerability, and so far the answer is yes. The majority's reasoning goes well beyond Anthropic. Any frontier lab that ships models with hard-coded refusals, which describes most of them, now has to consider that a federal buyer could call those safeguards a supply chain risk if they get in the way of a mission. That puts pressure on the industry's main safety tool, which is a model that declines certain tasks, and could push labs selling to the government toward separate, less restricted versions of their models for defense work.

The ruling also affects Anthropic's business. A standing Pentagon blacklist complicates sales to the large number of contractors that touch defense systems, and it adds legal uncertainty just as the company is preparing for public markets. Henderson's dissent gives Anthropic a ready argument for further review, and the conflict with Judge Lin's ruling makes it more likely that a higher court will have to settle the question.

## What to Watch

The main question is whether Anthropic asks the full D.C. Circuit to rehear the case or goes straight to the Supreme Court, and how quickly it moves while the panel's stay is in place. Also watch whether the administration appeals Judge Lin's San Francisco ruling, whether defense contractors start removing Claude from their workflows, and whether the uncertainty changes the timing or disclosures of Anthropic's expected IPO.

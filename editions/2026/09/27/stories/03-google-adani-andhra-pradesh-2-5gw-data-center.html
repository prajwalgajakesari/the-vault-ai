Google's $15B Andhra Pradesh AI Campus Swells to 2.51 GW as Villagers Report Lost Land

5 min read 983 words 4 sources
KEY TAKEAWAY

State clearances show Google's Visakhapatnam AI hub has approval for 2.51 GW, more than double the 1 GW announced, and farmers in Tarluvada say they lost land and have not received the replacement plots and jobs they were promised.

Google has spent a year calling its flagship Indian AI hub a "gigawatt-scale" project. State environmental papers show much more. According to clearance documents reported by The Guardian on September 26, the $15 billion campus around the village of Tarluvada in Andhra Pradesh has permission for 2.51 gigawatts of capacity. That is more than double the 1 GW figure used when the project was announced, and about the output of two large nuclear reactors running flat out.

Over a full year, that load would equal more than 30% of the electricity Andhra Pradesh uses today, in a state of more than 55 million people. The state issued the environmental clearances in nine days, and no public hearing was held.

From "golden opportunity" to fences

Google announced the Visakhapatnam AI hub in October 2025 with AdaniConneX and Nxtra by Airtel. It committed roughly $15 billion over 2026 to 2030 and called the hub its largest AI investment outside the United States. The company broke ground at Tarluvada on April 28, 2026, alongside India's IT minister Ashwini Vaishnaw and Andhra Pradesh Chief Minister N. Chandrababu Naidu. Google's own press release describes three data center campuses. At the ceremony, Adani Group director Jeet Adani said the partners were building "nearly 1 GW in a single location."

The state papers describe a much bigger build. In its reply to The Guardian, Google did not confirm a number. It called the project a gigawatt-scale AI hub that was "comparable to other projects."

The mood in Tarluvada has changed over the same period. In November 2025, the Deccan Chronicle reported that the village had been picked for a 308.65-acre site. The village head, B.R.B. Naidu, said then that the data center would bring jobs and development to the wider area, and local farmers described compensation offers of ₹50 lakh per acre. Ten months later, Naidu told The Guardian he was torn.

"We have learned that America does not want the problems of datacentres and so now Google is bringing them here," Naidu said. "Many in the village are very worried. But the government has refused to answer our questions."

Bulldozers have cleared more than 100 acres of forest on the hillside above the village. The Guardian reported that 520 local families recently lost land the government had allotted them about 20 years earlier. Villagers say security guards working for the Adani Group put up fences in April and blocked them from plots they had farmed for years. "They took land that we thought belonged to us, it's a big shock," said T Applaraju, 40, one of the affected farmers.

Among the 47 farmers from the Dalit community who were compensated, many are also unhappy. P Venkat Rao said he was pressured into giving up his only acre. He received about $40,000, but the replacement plot and the one job per family he was promised have not come through. "I am a farmer, without my land I am nothing," he said. "They have taken my livelihood and broken their promises."

The state government says no land was taken by force and that eligible families received "generous compensation." The Adani Group says all necessary legal approvals were obtained.

Subsidies, heat and water

The state wants this project badly. Its package of long tax breaks, discounted land and cheap electricity and water is estimated at ₹220 billion ($2.2 billion) over 20 years. Sribharat Mathukumilli, a member of parliament from the ruling coalition, dismissed the environmental concerns. "Our objective is not simply to host a datacentre, but to create an AI and digital ecosystem," he said. The state has also approved a separate 1 GW AI data center to be built by Reliance Industries.

Residents worry most about heat and water. Summer temperatures in Tarluvada already reach about 45°C, and the area is short of water. One of the other Google sites near Visakhapatnam sits a few metres from a reserve forest, a drinking-water reservoir and a wildlife sanctuary. Google told The Guardian the campus will be air-cooled, not water-cooled, and will have "no expected impact" on the local community. It also said it will create thousands of jobs. In April, the company announced a watershed program and skills training for more than 1,000 local workers.

Farmer Venkat Surynarayana, 40, said protesters had been threatened by police and detained. "If this is not stopped, it could be the end of Tarluvada," he said.

Why It Matters

The dispute over the capacity figure is about more than accuracy. A 2.51 GW load in a country where about 75% of grid power still comes from coal is a very different climate and grid-planning problem from a 1 GW load. Google says it is bringing "new, clean energy" to the grid, but that promise is harder to keep at two and a half times the size. The quick approval also matters. Nine days with no public hearing is the kind of process that leaves a project open to court challenges, and it undercuts Google's claim that it "engaged deeply" with local people.

The story also shows the global data center backlash spreading. Hyperscalers facing opposition from Texas to Scotland have sought cheap power and fast permits, and Andhra Pradesh offered both. The same objections have followed them there.

What to Watch

The Human Rights Forum has filed three petitions with India's National Green Tribunal. They argue that the state's environmental and water assessments were "grossly inadequate, scientifically unrealistic, and misleading." A separate public-interest case is before the Andhra Pradesh High Court. If either court orders a fresh assessment or a public hearing, construction could slow.

Also watch whether Google publicly states its real capacity target and says where the extra 1.5 GW of power would come from. The replacement land and jobs promised to the Dalit families are another test of whether Google's community programs reach the people who gave up land.

Australian Universities Split on Letting AI Help Mark Student Work

5 min read 984 words 3 sources
KEY TAKEAWAY

Five Australian universities now allow limited AI help with marking while UNSW, Melbourne and Sydney ban it, and a Western Sydney academic warns that overworked staff will stop checking the AI's work closely.

Australia's universities have reached a fork in the road on generative AI, and the dividing line runs straight through the marking pile. A Guardian Australia examination found that five universities now allow some AI assistance with assessment or feedback, while three of the country's most prestigious institutions bar generative AI from marking entirely. Several others say they have not made up their minds.

The permissive camp includes Western Sydney University, the University of Newcastle, Deakin University, RMIT and the University of Adelaide. None of them has handed grading over to a machine. Each offers what amounts to a qualified green light. Newcastle gives students the right to opt out. Deakin says staff may use AI to "improve efficiency and support assessment and administrative activities" but not to "assign grades." A Western Sydney spokesperson told the Guardian the university takes "a human-centred approach to generative AI use in assessment," adding that "responsibility for academic judgment must always remain with university teaching staff."

On the other side sit the University of NSW, the University of Melbourne and the University of Sydney. All three draw the line at generative AI in marking, even as they encourage its use elsewhere in teaching.

The Warning From Inside a Permissive Campus

The sharpest criticism comes from inside one of the universities that allows the practice. Armin Alimardani, a senior lecturer in law and technology at Western Sydney University who calls himself a tech enthusiast, told the Guardian that colleagues have been asking him whether AI could lighten their grading load. He finds that talk "extremely concerning."

"We've got to be really careful where we are stepping here," he said. "Because I think there's always a risk, at the moment, for higher education to collapse."

Alimardani's main worry is what he calls verification drift. He described a thought experiment: give 100 academics an AI marking tool and tell them to check its output. "Let's say 80 do exactly as advised, there are going to be 20 who do not," he said. Once the first few AI assessments look fine, an overworked marker starts checking less carefully, and hallucinations get through as if they were fact. Critics also warn of a so-called slop cycle, in which students hand in AI-generated assignments that academics then mark with AI.

Alimardani does see a role for AI as a "second pair of eyes." He is building models to spot inconsistencies in human marking. But he argues the benefits are largely untested and the risks large enough to justify a "really slow" evaluation.

Staff Anxiety and Student Anger

Among the undecided, Monash said "at this time, AI is not used in the marking process," and Wollongong and James Cook gave similar answers. At the Queensland University of Technology, a late-August staff email about an "assessment uplift project" promised to make assessment "authentic, trusted, scalable and future-focused." A senior academic, speaking anonymously, called it "worryingly vague" and said it "certainly seems to be flagging that we will use less human marking if we can." Sessional staff often depend on marking as a main source of income, the academic noted, adding: "People are concerned their job might be made redundant."

The same academic pointed to the contradiction many staff feel: "We are telling students all the time that there are integrity issues with their use of AI in assessment. What message does it send if we then say: but we are going to use it to mark you?"

Students are asking the same question. Alex Cameron, a creative and professional writing student at QUT, said he "wouldn't pay $1000" for a degree marked by AI. "At that point, we might as well just have AI everything," he said. "I would be irate. I would be absolutely bloody incensed."

The Regulator's Frame

The Tertiary Education Quality and Standards Agency (TEQSA) has not issued a rule on AI marking specifically. On 24 June 2026 it published Assuring quality learning in a gen AI-integrated future, the third resource in its assessment reform series. The paper moves the emphasis away from securing assessments against AI and toward building students' evaluative judgement, critical thinking and ethical reasoning, while still requiring providers to meet their obligations under the Higher Education Standards Framework. TEQSA's good-practice hub says its shared case studies are "not intended as guidance," which leaves each institution to set its own line.

Outside Australia, Carnegie Mellon professor Christian Kästner offers a different model. He has written that AI agents can now complete his entire Machine Learning in Production course, including coding assignments and written reflections. His response was to rebuild most assessment around 15-minute in-person check-ins with teaching assistants and oral debriefs, rather than graded take-home work. Students may use AI everywhere except written exams.

Why It Matters

Marking is where a university vouches for its degrees. It is also one of the most labour-intensive parts of academic work, which is why AI assistance appeals to stretched institutions. The Australian split shows the sector has no shared view on whether human judgement can be kept "in the loop" once a machine produces the first draft of a grade. Alimardani's verification-drift argument challenges the premise behind every permissive policy: that telling staff to stay responsible is the same as making sure they do. If students see AI-assisted marking as a downgrade, universities could lose trust quickly.

What to Watch

Watch QUT's assessment uplift consultation, the clearest sign of whether a major university will openly cut human marking hours. Watch Monash, Wollongong, James Cook and UQ, all still deciding, to see which way they go. Newcastle's opt-out will show how many students actually refuse AI-assisted feedback when given the choice. The real test will come with the first public error: a hallucinated comment or wrong grade that reaches a student. That would put Alimardani's warning into practice and could push TEQSA from sharing good practice to setting firmer expectations.

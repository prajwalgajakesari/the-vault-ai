10-Year Treasury at 5.17% Raises the Bill on AI's $4.1 Trillion Debt Binge

5 min read 982 words 6 sources
KEY TAKEAWAY

With the 10-year Treasury yield at its highest level since 2007, borrowing costs for AI's planned $4.1 trillion debt buildout are rising fast; the investment-grade hyperscalers can absorb it, but lenders are growing choosier about neoclouds and junk-rated backers like SoftBank.

The most important number in AI this week wasn't a benchmark. It was 5.17%, roughly where the 10-year Treasury yield now sits after climbing to its highest level since 2007. That benchmark sets the floor for nearly every dollar the AI industry plans to borrow, and the industry plans to borrow a lot of them.

JPMorgan Chase estimated in June that $4.1 trillion in AI-related debt will be issued through 2030 as data center developers, cloud providers and chip-hungry startups race to build capacity. According to CNBC, the 10-year yield is up about a full percentage point since January, which means every new bond, loan and project financing tied to that buildout now has to offer investors a richer return. Goldman Sachs credit strategists put global AI-related debt issuance at more than $575 billion so far in 2026, according to reporting on SoftBank's latest deal, so the repricing is hitting a market that is already enormous.

The Week the Price Tag Showed Up

The clearest illustration came from SoftBank. The Japanese conglomerate, one of the largest suppliers of capital to AI projects, raised $11.1 billion in a junk-bond sale this week, with the 7-year tranche priced at yields as high as 9.75%, CNBC reported. Seoul Economic Daily, citing Bloomberg, reported that the offering drew more than $20 billion in orders and that the price guidance on all three dollar tranches marked record highs for SoftBank's dollar bonds. Proceeds are going toward an equity commitment to OpenAI of roughly $65 billion and toward acquisitions. SoftBank's dollar notes due 2031 had already jumped to an 8.2% yield this month from 6.7% in January.

Mana Nakazora, a strategist at BNP Paribas, framed the trade bluntly. "This is a financing decision that tells you how Son values the OpenAI stake relative to the cost of his balance sheet," she said, as quoted in coverage of the deal. "The market will price the notes on the assumption that the proceeds go out the door as equity in a private company, not into anything that generates near-term cash flow."

SoftBank was not alone. Bloomberg reported that a Blue Owl-sponsored developer raised $1.1 billion in junk bonds to finance a CoreWeave-tied data center, with five-year notes priced to yield 9.25%, roughly 2.7 percentage points above the average for similarly rated debt. CoreWeave itself has flagged the sensitivity in its SEC filings: as of June, every 100-basis-point increase in rates could add about $30 million to its interest expense on floating-rate debt.

Equity investors are sorting winners from the stretched. CoreWeave shares rose almost 8% on the week, while Oracle, which has leaned heavily on debt markets to fund its AI expansion, fell 7% and is down about 30% for the year. Oracle's slide followed a Bloomberg report that the company sent a "force majeure" notice tied to its Project Jupiter data center campus in New Mexico; Oracle told CNBC the project "remains on our planned schedule."

Lenders Get Choosier, Borrowers Shrug

On the lending side, the message is selectivity. Riley Thompson, a vice president at Mitsubishi HC Capital America, told CNBC that lenders are getting pickier even when borrowers agree to pay up. "Instead of a roster of 50 neoclouds, there's probably 20 that the market's truly interested in," Thompson said. A senior private credit investor, speaking anonymously to CNBC, said neocloud deals will be harder to finance because those companies have less cushion to absorb higher costs.

Borrowers, however, show little sign of pulling back. "They basically are price insensitive to that raise, which means they're price takers," said Mark Malek, chief investment officer at Siebert Financial. Bernie Margulies, CEO of GPU-financing risk adviser American Compute, put it more simply: "If you have a deal with Anthropic, will 50 basis points really stop you?" Andrew Giudici, global head of corporate, project and infrastructure finance at KBRA, expects the pipeline to keep flowing. "In a normal environment, people might take a step back and pause a bit," he said. "But I don't think that's going to happen here."

Why It Matters

There is a feedback loop at work. Morningstar argues that AI is acting as a large demand shock on the economy: data center spending and an AI-driven stock rally that fuels consumer wealth are lifting aggregate demand, and the Federal Reserve is leaning against that with tighter policy. In Morningstar's framing, the main effect of AI today is higher interest rates rather than higher inflation. The boom, in other words, is helping push up the very borrowing costs it depends on.

That matters unevenly. Amazon, Google, Meta and Microsoft all carry investment-grade ratings and are still expected to raise capital spending in 2027, so a higher Treasury yield is an annoyance rather than a threat for them. The pain concentrates further down the capital stack, among neoclouds, project-finance vehicles and speculative-grade backers like SoftBank, whose cost of money is now approaching double digits. Sentiment is also fragile: Bloomberg reported that a mid-September scare, after AI lab leaders called for slowing frontier development, erased more than $600 billion from the Nasdaq 100 in two sessions before a rebound driven by enthusiasm over Meta's Muse assistant. Nancy Tengler of Laffer Tengler Investments described a market where "the narrative changes every other week."

What to Watch

The next test is whether the roughly 20 neoclouds lenders still favor can keep refinancing at current spreads, and whether the other 30 start showing up in distressed-debt screens. Watch SoftBank's settlement on September 29 and its OpenAI funding tranche expected around October 1 for signs of balance-sheet strain. Watch Oracle's Project Jupiter for any follow-through on the force majeure notice. And watch the Fed: if Morningstar is right that AI demand is what's keeping rates high, any cooling in the buildout could eventually bring yields down, which would ease the bill for everyone except those who locked in at 9.75%.

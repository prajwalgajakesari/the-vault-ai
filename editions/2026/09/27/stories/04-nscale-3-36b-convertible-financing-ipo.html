Nscale Lands $3.36B in Convertible Financing Ahead of U.S. IPO

5 min read 978 words 4 sources
KEY TAKEAWAY

Nscale raised $3.36B in convertible notes led by Third Point, including $1B from Nvidia due in mid-November, which will convert to equity at a discount when the company lists on the NYSE at a reported valuation of up to $35B.

Nscale, the London-based AI cloud company that barely existed two and a half years ago, has raised $3.36 billion in pre-IPO convertible notes led by hedge fund Third Point. The round came one week after the company filed to list on the New York Stock Exchange, and it adds a large new layer of capital to one of the most aggressive infrastructure buildouts in the neocloud sector.

The deal, announced on Friday, September 25, is split into two pieces. An initial $2.36 billion tranche was available at closing. A further $1 billion commitment comes from Nvidia, an existing investor, with that money expected in mid-November. All of the notes convert automatically into shares when Nscale completes its IPO. Most investors will receive ordinary shares; Nvidia's will convert into non-voting stock.

"This marks a milestone for Nscale as we continue scaling our full-stack AI infrastructure to meet unprecedented global demand," said Josh Payne, Nscale's founder and CEO, in the company's announcement. "With the backing of these world-class investors, we are strongly positioned to accelerate our data center buildouts globally."

A Crowded Cap Table

Alongside Third Point and Nvidia, the round drew funds managed by Apollo, Citadel, Hudson Bay Capital, the Abu Dhabi Investment Council and 8090 Industries. Davidson Kempner, Wellington Management and a string of other funds also took part.

The final figure fell slightly short of the target. The Next Web reported that three weeks earlier Nscale had been seeking up to $3.5 billion, which leaves the closed round about $140 million below that goal. The outlet also reported the conversion mechanics. The notes convert at the IPO price minus a double-digit percentage discount, and that discount adjusts only up to a $30 billion valuation, above which the conversion price stops moving. That detail matters because the listing itself is reportedly aiming higher. The Financial Times has reported a potential valuation of about $35 billion, and Bloomberg has reported that Nscale is seeking to raise up to $3 billion in the offering. Nscale plans to trade under the ticker NSCL, with Goldman Sachs, J.P. Morgan and Morgan Stanley as lead bookrunners.

The Numbers Behind the Pitch

Nscale says it has more than $103 billion in total contracted value. According to figures from its S-1 filing reported by The Next Web, that backlog stood at $103.4 billion, up from $38 billion at the end of last year. The operating business is still far smaller. Revenue for the first half of 2026 was $140.6 million, more than 13 times the same period a year earlier, against a net loss of $1.02 billion.

That gap between contracted demand and recognized revenue is the heart of the neocloud model. Nscale was spun out of Australian crypto miner Arkon Energy and incorporated in May 2024. It has since raised a $155 million Series A, a $1.1 billion Series B, a $433 million SAFE and a $2 billion Series C in March 2026 that valued it at $14.6 billion. Sheryl Sandberg and former U.K. deputy prime minister Nick Clegg joined its board at that time.

Its anchor customers set the scale of its ambitions. Reuters reported in October 2025 that Nscale agreed to supply Microsoft with about 200,000 Nvidia AI chips. The company is also a partner in the OpenAI and Nvidia-backed "Stargate" projects in the U.K. and Norway, with campuses in Narvik and Glomfjord in Norway and a site at a former coal power station in Northumberland. In the U.S., it acquired the Monarch Compute Campus in West Virginia. In August, it closed about $3 billion in delayed-draw term loans for a Ward County, Texas campus of roughly 200 MW, which will run Nvidia GB300 and Vera Rubin systems, and a 40 MW site in Madison, North Carolina.

Nvidia's enthusiasm has been explicit. The Times reported that Jensen Huang said that by the end of 2026 "we'll probably have 300,000 GPUs put online with Nscale."

Why It Matters

This round is a bridge to the public markets. Convertible notes let Nscale lock in billions now without setting a price on its equity before the IPO does it for them. Investors get a discounted entry into what could be one of the largest AI infrastructure listings of the year. The $30 billion cap on the discount adjustment signals where sophisticated money thinks a floor might sit. If the IPO prices near the reported $35 billion, noteholders convert at a meaningful markdown to public buyers.

The deal also highlights Nvidia's circular role in the AI economy. The chipmaker is simultaneously Nscale's supplier, its investor and, through non-voting shares, a future shareholder. The Next Web noted that Nvidia made a similar non-voting pre-IPO purchase in SB Energy this month. It also reported that Nvidia now holds roughly $99 billion of stock in companies that buy its chips. For public investors, the question is how much of Nscale's backlog reflects durable end-customer demand and how much reflects a financing loop among a small number of AI giants.

Finally, a British AI champion is choosing New York over London. That is an awkward moment for the U.K., where Nscale sits at the center of national AI infrastructure plans.

What to Watch

The next milestone is the price range in Nscale's amended S-1, which will show how close the company can get to the reported $35 billion target. Watch whether Nvidia's $1 billion tranche lands on schedule in mid-November. Investors will also want more detail on how much of the $103 billion backlog is binding and when it turns into revenue. On the ground, the test is delivery. Nscale's first self-built U.K. data centre in Loughton, Essex, has already slipped into early 2027, according to The Times. Execution in Texas, Norway and West Virginia will decide whether public markets treat Nscale as the next CoreWeave or as a cautionary tale about building ahead of demand.

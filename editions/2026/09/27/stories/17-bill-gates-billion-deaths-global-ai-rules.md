# Bill Gates Warns Unchecked AI Could Cause "a Billion Deaths," Calls for Global Rules Harder Than Nuclear Talks

Bill Gates has spent most of the AI boom as one of the technology's leading optimists. On Sunday he gave the starkest warning of his career about it. In an interview that aired September 27 on NBC's *Meet the Press*, the Microsoft co-founder told host Kristen Welker that artificial intelligence in the wrong hands is already capable of triggering catastrophe on a scale once associated only with the deadliest weapons in history.

"AI is certainly powerful enough to drive events that, you know, cause a billion deaths," Gates said, in excerpts NBC released ahead of the broadcast. "Even though it's pretty hard to get to 100%, there's never been a weapon as powerful as the combination of people with ill intent using the latest AI tools."

The interview was a direct call for government action. It put Gates publicly at odds with President Donald Trump, who has rejected federal guardrails on AI developers.

## A Human Threat, Not a Rogue Machine

Gates focused less on science-fiction scenarios of machines turning on their makers and more on what people can do with the tools that already exist. According to NBC's reporting, he said AI could be used to defraud people, shut down electric grids or scramble bank accounts. The deeper shift, he argued, is that AI lets small groups carry out actions that were once possible only for the biggest nation-states.

Asked whether the industry can police itself, Gates was blunt. "No one thinks self-regulation is enough," he told Welker. Asked whether Congress should legislate, he replied: "Absolutely."

"You need law enforcement and the politicians to get into the discussion about what safeguards and monitoring look like," Gates said, describing the resulting burden on AI developers as only a little bit of overhead. He also pushed back on the idea that one mechanism could solve the problem. Congress has floated requiring AI companies to build in a kill switch, but Gates said that alone would not prevent AI-related tragedies. Labs, he argued, need internal monitoring and records of what their own models are actually doing, layered on top of any shutdown mandate.

## Harder Than the Cold War

When Welker compared the challenge to the nuclear arms-control talks between Washington and Moscow, Gates said the AI problem is worse. "This is more difficult than that was," he said, particularly if the U.S. puts less effort into working with its allies.

He nonetheless wants the U.S. to lead. Gates pointed to past American-led agreements on "aviation safety, ozone layer, nuclear weapons, a lot of things where we played the fair actor, not aggrandizing whatever our unique strengths were," according to an Anadolu Agency account of the interview. "And AI is one we're going to have to go back to this: 'Hey, humanity is all in this together,'" he added.

That puts him on a collision course with the White House. Earlier this month Trump rejected calls for federal AI guardrails, writing on Truth Social: "WHOEVER WINS AI, WINS! We are leading China, and all others, and will continue to do so." Trump has also dismissed warnings from industry leaders, saying "a lot of negative forces" were "bringing things up that won't happen," Newsweek reported.

Gates tried to leave the door open. "What he said is fine, but I think after he hears from me and other people, including that this does not handicap us in whatever he thinks the nation-state race is, that, you know, we will come to a consensus," he said. His central argument to the administration is that monitoring requirements would not slow the U.S. in its competition with China.

## The Foundation's Stake

Gates also discussed how the Gates Foundation, which plans to spend more than $200 billion through 2045, is applying AI in health, education and agriculture. He flagged a stark equity gap: AI error rates in African languages run roughly 10 times higher than in English and other widely used languages. The foundation says it is working with a coalition of 60 companies and partners on the problem, with a goal of reaching more than 3 billion people over five years.

## Why It Matters

Gates is not a habitual doomsayer, which is what makes the warning land. His August essay framed AI as a fork in the road, saying it "will either be the greatest equalizer ever invented, or the worst source of injustice," and warning that "on the current course and speed, there's a very high chance of a net negative outcome." Sunday's interview turned that abstract concern into a concrete policy agenda: legislation, law-enforcement involvement, mandatory monitoring and US-led international rules.

The timing sharpens the stakes. Gates spoke in the same week Trump hosted Chinese President Xi Jinping at a White House summit where AI was a central topic, and after Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei called for slowing frontier development and OpenAI disclosed agents that accessed an outside company's systems without instruction. With the most famous name in personal computing saying self-regulation is not enough, the White House position that any brake equals surrender to China looks less like consensus.

His kill-switch point is a useful corrective: a shutdown button without records of model behavior is only as good as the ability to know when to press it.

## What to Watch

The next test is diplomatic. The U.S. and China have set up a dialogue on AI risks and benefits, with the next exchange expected by November, according to NBC. Whether Washington brings any concrete safety proposals to that table will show whether Gates' lobbying moved the administration at all.

On Capitol Hill, watch whether kill-switch proposals get expanded into broader monitoring and logging requirements of the kind Gates described, and whether Rep. Ro Khanna's push for a U.S.-China expert working group gains support. Finally, watch whether Trump answers Gates with dismissal or engagement.

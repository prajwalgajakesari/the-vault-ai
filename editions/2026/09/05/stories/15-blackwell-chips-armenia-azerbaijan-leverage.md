The Zangezur corridor was supposed to be the hard part. When Nikol Pashinyan and Ilham Aliyev stood beside Donald Trump in the East Room on August 8, 2025, and initialed a 17-point peace framework after three decades of fighting that killed close to 40,000 people, the visible currency was transit rights: a US-leased passage across southern Armenia, rebranded the Trump Route for International Peace and Prosperity.

According to the Wall Street Journal, there was a second currency in the room, and it was made of silicon.

The paper reported on September 4, 2026 — citing people involved in the talks — that US negotiators dangled expanded access to Nvidia's most advanced AI chips as an inducement to Yerevan, tied to a large data center project in the Armenian town of Hrazdan. It is one of the clearest documented instances of what US officials have taken to calling chip diplomacy, and, per the Journal, among the first times export-controlled compute has been deployed as leverage to end an armed conflict rather than to buy commercial alignment.

## What is established, and what is not

The sequence is a matter of public record. Firebird, a US-based AI cloud company, announced in June 2025 that it would build the South Caucasus's first AI data center in a public-private partnership with Nvidia and the Armenian government. The peace declaration was signed that August. In November 2025 — three months later — Firebird announced it had secured a US export license and named Dell Technologies as hardware partner. In February 2026, during a visit to Yerevan by Vice President JD Vance, Firebird and the US government jointly announced Phase 2: roughly $4 billion, about 50,000 GPUs, and licensing for a further 41,000 Nvidia GB300 units. Vance called it "a new beginning" for the two countries.

What is not on the record is the linkage itself. No published text of the joint declaration mentions semiconductors. No Commerce Department licensing document ties approval to a diplomatic concession. The causal claim — that chip access was offered as an inducement while talks were near collapse — rests entirely on anonymous sources in a single newspaper report. Neither the White House, Nvidia nor Firebird has publicly confirmed it. That does not make it wrong; it makes it, for now, unverifiable outside the Journal's sourcing.

The Journal also reported the project is expected to cost more than $5 billion and to reach roughly 300 megawatts and 70,000 Nvidia servers, including Grace Blackwell and Vera Rubin systems, by the end of 2027. Hrazdan sits about 30 miles from the Azerbaijani border.

## The thing itself does exist

Unusually for a story of this type, the promised infrastructure is not vapor. Firebird opened its flagship site, DC-1, on August 8, 2026 — a year to the day after the White House ceremony. Pashinyan attended, alongside Kazakhstan's deputy prime minister Zhaslan Madiyev and David Allen, the US chargé d'affaires in Yerevan.

But the operating numbers are far smaller than the headline ones. DC-1 is specified at 6,144 Nvidia B200 GPUs across 15 megawatts, liquid-cooled, running on Nvidia's DSX platform and Dell PowerEdge servers. That is about 5 percent of the 300-megawatt target and under 9 percent of the 70,000-GPU figure. Nvidia says the site went from plan to operating capacity in just over six months. A planned DC-2 is listed at 75,000 VR200 GPUs across 125 megawatts.

"Our ambition for what we are building in the next 2 years or so is roughly 2 gigawatts of capacity around the world," said Alexander Yesayan, Firebird's co-founder, at the opening. "We're very focused on merging into frontier markets."

The first named customer is Perplexity. Nvidia says it intends to invest in Firebird, following an earlier investment by CoreWeave — the now-familiar loop in which the chip vendor takes equity in a company that then buys its chips.

## Compute as statecraft

Washington has run this play before, but for money or alignment. The May 2025 US-UAE framework opened the door to hundreds of thousands of Nvidia chips; approvals let G42 and Saudi Arabia's Humain buy up to 35,000 GB300s in a roughly $1 billion tranche. On July 10, 2026, the Bureau of Industry and Security moved the UAE out of Country Groups D:3 and D:4 into A:5, ending individual licensing for G42 and Core42.

Armenia is a different category. Oil embargoes, arms packages and IMF standby facilities have been instruments of statecraft for a century. What makes compute distinctive is that a single agency's licensing decision, made without public deliberation, can now be the deliverable in a peace negotiation. Export controls were designed as a national-security screen; used as a bargaining chip, they become a discretionary reward — and the discretion sits with an administration whose president speaks regularly with Nvidia's chief executive.

That creates two accountability gaps. The first is oversight: there is no mechanism requiring Commerce to disclose that a license was granted for diplomatic rather than security reasons. The second is incentive design. If GPU allocations are the reward for concessions, every government at the table has reason to announce the largest data center it can plausibly describe. Armenia's $4 billion project exceeds 10 percent of its nominal GDP. The Caspian Policy Center has flagged the physical constraints: EurasiaNet estimates the site could need 500,000 gallons of water a day in a country with acute water stress, and 30 to 40 percent of Armenian electricity comes from a single aging nuclear plant that runs on Russian fuel.

## What to watch

Three things. Whether Commerce or the White House responds to the Journal's account, and whether anyone in Congress asks to see the licensing file. Whether DC-1 scales past 15 megawatts on schedule, given that the grid and water math has not visibly changed. And whether the framework itself survives: what was initialed in August 2025 was a declaration of intent, not a ratified treaty. If chips helped buy the signature, the question is what happens to the next 41,000 GPUs if the signature stops holding.

When Maria Ressa announced the Global Call for AI Red Lines from the podium of the 80th United Nations General Assembly on September 22, 2025, the campaign did something most open letters carefully avoid: it wrote a date into its own text. Governments, the declaration said, should reach an international agreement on red lines for AI — "ensuring they are operational, with robust enforcement mechanisms — by the end of 2026."

That deadline is now roughly four months away, and the 81st General Assembly high-level week is about to convene in New York. The honest status check: the institutional scaffolding has gone up faster than almost anyone expected, and the binding prohibitions have not been drafted at all.

Launched with more than 200 signatories including 10 Nobel laureates, the call now lists **300+ prominent figures**, **90+ organizations**, and 15 Nobel Prize and Turing Award recipients, according to the campaign's own site. The roster spans Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio, John Hopfield, Jennifer Doudna, Joseph Stiglitz, former Colombian president Juan Manuel Santos, former Irish president Mary Robinson, and — reflecting a deliberate reach beyond the West — Yi Zeng, dean of the Beijing Institute of AI Safety and Governance. It was initiated by the French Center for AI Safety (CeSIA) alongside The Future Society and Berkeley's Center for Human-Compatible AI. The letter names no fixed list of prohibitions but floats candidates: bans on AI assistance with bioweapon design, on AI in nuclear command and control, on lethal autonomous weapons, on mass surveillance and social scoring, on autonomous self-replication, and on AI systems impersonating human beings.

What governments have actually done in the intervening eleven months is real, but procedural rather than prohibitive. On February 12, 2026, the General Assembly appointed the 40 members of the Independent International Scientific Panel on Artificial Intelligence, chosen from more than 2,600 candidates. At its inaugural meeting on March 3, the panel elected Bengio and Ressa as co-chairs — the two most visible faces of the red lines campaign now sit atop the UN's own scientific advisory body. The inaugural Global Dialogue on AI Governance convened in Geneva on July 6 and 7, 2026, after consultations drawing more than 1,500 written submissions. Its stated goals, as framed by Secretary-General António Guterres at the Dialogue's 2025 launch, are safe and trustworthy systems, interoperability between governance regimes, and open innovation. Not prohibitions.

The India AI Impact Summit, which the campaign had flagged as the venue where states might endorse an initial set of standardized red lines, produced the New Delhi Declaration on February 19, 2026 — endorsed by 86 countries and two international organisations by *Nature India*'s count, and by 89 countries and organisations by the Indian government's. Its seven pillars cover human capital, access, trustworthiness, energy efficiency, AI for science, democratised resources, and economic growth. It is non-binding, establishes no enforcement mechanism, and contains no prohibitions.

The hardest obstacle is not drafting. It is Washington. Days after the red lines call launched, White House Office of Science and Technology Policy director Michael Kratsios told the UN Security Council: **"We totally reject all efforts by international bodies to assert centralized control and global governance of AI."** In the same remarks he characterised existential risk as an "ideological fixation." CSIS analysts Laura Caroli and Matt Mande judged that Washington's rejection substantially reduces the multilateral track's near-term relevance, since meaningful global governance is difficult without the leading technology holder. China, by contrast, has positioned the UN as the main stage for AI rulemaking — a stance campaigners welcome and US officials read as an attempt to dilute American influence.

Critics raise a second objection with nothing to do with geopolitics: verification is genuinely hard. Proving the *absence* of a capability in a trained model is not the same problem as counting centrifuges, and proposed technical fixes — on-chip compute metering, cryptographic proofs of training compliance, privacy-preserving audits — do not yet exist at governance grade. Researchers also point to a specification trap: the more precisely you define a threshold, the further it drifts from the risk you were worried about; the vaguer you leave it, the easier it is to move the goalposts for the next model.

## Why this matters

The strongest case for the campaign is that prohibition regimes have worked before, and the people saying so are not naive. Ahmet Üzümcü, who ran the Organisation for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons, signed with the line: *"It is in our vital common interest to prevent AI from inflicting serious and potentially irreversible damages to humanity, and we should act accordingly."* Bengio's framing is narrower and harder to dismiss: *"The current race towards ever more capable and autonomous AI systems poses major risks to our societies and we urgently need international collaboration to address them."* Red lines are not comprehensive regulation. They are a small set of universally rejected uses — historically the easiest category on which rivals agree.

The case against is that binding international law without the largest player is theatre, and theatre has costs: it consumes political capital, lets governments substitute declarations for domestic enforcement, and risks discrediting the concept when the deadline passes unmet. Partial red lines already exist without any treaty — the EU AI Act's "unacceptable risk" prohibitions, and a US-China understanding that AI should not decide nuclear launch.

Meanwhile "red lines" migrated into American domestic politics through an entirely different door. In February 2026, the Pentagon's dispute with Anthropic over contractual limits on fully autonomous weapons and mass domestic surveillance turned the phrase into a live US political fight. Google and OpenAI employees signed letters; Senator Adam Schiff introduced legislation to codify the limits.

Watch three things before December. Whether any state formally tables a red lines resolution during the 81st General Assembly high-level week. Whether the Scientific Panel, co-chaired by two signatories, publishes anything naming specific thresholds — and whether states treat that as expertise or as advocacy. And whether the Schiff bill advances, because a binding national prohibition in the United States would do more for the campaign's substance than a treaty its government refuses to join.

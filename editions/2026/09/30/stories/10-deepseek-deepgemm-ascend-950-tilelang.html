DeepSeek Open-Sources DeepGEMM-Ascend and TileLang Tooling for Huawei's Ascend 950, Chipping at CUDA's Moat

5 min read 1002 words 5 sources
KEY TAKEAWAY

DeepSeek released six open-source Ascend tools, led by an API-compatible DeepGEMM port reaching up to 99.8% of Ascend 950 peak and a TileLang back end, making it much easier for Chinese developers to move off Nvidia's CUDA.

DeepSeek spent Wednesday on software for someone else's chip. The Hangzhou lab open-sourced six infrastructure components built for Huawei's Ascend platform. The centrepiece is DeepGEMM-Ascend, an MIT-licensed port of its matrix-multiplication kernel library that DeepSeek says reaches up to 99.8 percent of the Ascend 950's theoretical peak on dense workloads. Alongside it came an Ascend back end for TileLang, the kernel language DeepSeek now uses for most of its training code. Taken together, it is the most serious attempt yet by a frontier Chinese lab to rebuild the developer tooling that has kept the world's AI builders tied to Nvidia.

The release, announced in a post on DeepSeek's official WeChat account, deliberately mirrors the stack the company previously open-sourced for Nvidia hardware. Besides DeepGEMM and TileLang, it includes DeepEP Ascend for cross-device communication, TileKernels for vector and memory-access operators, FlashMLA for sparse attention and DeepSelect for data selection. "Each of these components has a direct counterpart among the components we previously open-sourced for NVIDIA platforms," the company wrote.

Near-peak numbers on Ascend 950

The DeepGEMM-Ascend repository describes the library as "fully API-compatible with DeepGEMM". It supports BF16, FP8 and FP4 GEMM, MQA logits and MegaMoE, and it ships a prenorm GEMM kernel for DeepSeek's mHC (Manifold-Constrained Hyper-Connections) module. It even keeps the same package name, so a developer can install it on an Ascend machine and keep the same calls and workflow they would use on Nvidia hardware. Under the hood, it replaces the CUDA execution path with a thin abstraction over Ascend's MAD (matrix multiply-add) primitives. The repository says that abstraction hides the chip's fractal data layouts, alignment constraints and address arithmetic. It also leans on Ascend-specific tricks such as sparse data loading and coroutine-based pipelining.

The published benchmarks were run on an Ascend 950DT using Huawei's CANN 9.20 toolkit, and they are striking. A 4096 by 7168 by 16384 BF16 matrix multiply hits 431 teraflops against a hardware limit of 432, or 99.8 percent utilization. FP8 reaches 861 of 865 teraflops (99.5 percent), and FP4 delivers 1,701 teraflops against a 1,730 ceiling (98.3 percent). The MegaMoE kernel fuses expert-parallel dispatch, two grouped GEMMs, SwiGLU and combine into one operation. Across eight ranks with 384 experts, it sustains roughly 790 to 846 teraflops. The MQA logits kernel used by DeepSeek's Lightning Indexer saturates the chip's FIX pipe at 99 percent. The project is led by Kexing Zhou, Zhean Xu and Chenggang Zhao. Its acknowledgements thank Huawei for "technical support and engineering expertise" throughout development.

TileLang is arguably the more strategic piece. According to the project's GitHub update cited by the South China Morning Post, the language now officially supports Ascend 950 with "native code generation, automatic scheduling, and synchronisation". That means the compiler, rather than the kernel author, handles the hardware's most error-prone details. "Compared with NVIDIA's CUDA, TileLang offers a simpler programming model that can significantly improve development efficiency and simplify code logic," DeepSeek wrote. The company says TileLang now implements the majority of operators used to train its V4 model family. It adds that every TileLang operator currently used in DeepSeek training has a matching high-performance implementation on Ascend.

Analysis: building the escape hatch from CUDA

Nvidia's hold on AI has never rested on silicon alone. Its moat is CUDA and the large body of tuned libraries, kernels and developer habits built on top of it over more than a decade. Chinese accelerators have struggled less with raw hardware than with that software gap. Writing fast kernels for Ascend has meant wrestling with unfamiliar layouts and low-level Ascend C. "To build a new generation of independent and controllable GPU software ecosystems, the first priority is to develop a high-level language that is general-purpose, easy to program, and capable of pushing hardware performance to its limits," DeepSeek said in its announcement.

Writing about the release, the policy newsletter Geopolitechs argued that DeepSeek is building an abstraction layer that could sit on top of either Nvidia or Huawei hardware. If that works, it noted, the cost and difficulty of moving AI workloads to Chinese chips "could fall significantly." Huawei's involvement goes well beyond a passing thank-you. DeepSeek said the two teams are jointly building a supernode of 128 Ascend 950 processors and optimizing both computation and inter-chip communication. The announcement came about two weeks after Huawei unveiled its next generation of AI processors and supernode systems. According to Invezz, DeepSeek also plans to install more than 160,000 Ascend chips at a new data center in Inner Mongolia.

The timing matters for Nvidia. Chief executive Jensen Huang has said US export controls cut the company's share of China's advanced AI chip market from roughly 95 percent to effectively zero. H200 sales to Chinese customers made up less than 1 percent of Nvidia's data center revenue in the quarter ended July 26. Separately, Beijing is reportedly weighing whether to let firms such as Alibaba and ByteDance buy Nvidia's RTX Pro 5500. That makes the release a hedge in both directions: if US rules loosen, DeepSeek's tools make staying off CUDA cheaper; if they tighten, the tooling is already in place.

There are caveats. The benchmarks are self-reported and measured on DeepSeek's own model shapes. The repository is a single initial commit with a few dozen stars, and it requires Huawei's CANN 9.20 toolkit and torch_npu. Near-peak kernel efficiency also says nothing about how Ascend clusters hold up on reliability, interconnect bandwidth and yield across a full frontier training run.

What to watch

The real test is whether DeepSeek trains a flagship model end to end on Ascend 950 supernodes, and whether other Chinese labs adopt TileLang rather than writing directly for each domestic chip. Watch for independent benchmarks of DeepGEMM-Ascend and for TileLang back ends targeting other vendors such as Cambricon or Moore Threads. In Washington, watch whether US policymakers start to treat open-source kernel tooling, not just silicon, as part of the export-control equation.

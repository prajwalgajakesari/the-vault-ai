Anthropic's Robot-Exposure Index Finds Robots Could Do Three-Quarters of U.S. Physical Tasks but Pay Off for Just 0.3% of Work

5 min read 995 words 3 sources
KEY TAKEAWAY

Today's robots can technically do 74% of U.S. physical tasks but are cheaper than human labor for only 0.3% of work, and at historical price declines of about 3% a year it would take roughly 40 years for that share to reach 10%.

Robots on the market today can already do almost three-quarters of the physical work in the American economy. For nearly all of it, though, they still cost more than the people doing it. That is the main finding of a new robot-exposure index from Anthropic's economics team, published Wednesday. The study finds that robots could perform 74% of U.S. physical job tasks, about 34% of all working hours, but are cost-competitive for only 0.3% of work. If robot prices keep falling at their historical pace, the authors estimate it will take about 40 years for that share to reach 10%.

The paper, "What work can robots do?", by Russell Legate-Yang and Maxim Massenkoff, adds physical automation to the work Anthropic has been publishing on AI and the labor market. Its framing is simple: "Robots do work where LLMs cannot." Once robots are counted alongside large language models, the share of U.S. work exposed to automation rises from about half to 81%.

How the index works

The researchers began with O*NET, the federal database that links about 900 occupations to roughly 19,000 task descriptions. Claude scored each task on physical, cognitive and interpersonal requirements, which produced 7,594 tasks classified as physical. For each physical task, Claude was told to search the web for specific robots that could perform it and to quote sources showing real deployments, commercial sales or demonstrations. "Only demonstrated robot capabilities count," the authors write. A robot also had to match human performance on reliability, error rates and speed.

Tasks were then sorted into four tiers by the kind of environment a robot needs. E0 means no robot can do the task. E1 means a robot can do it only in a purpose-built setting such as an assembly line. E2 covers structured human workplaces like logistics warehouses, and E3 covers unstructured settings like a city road. Claude first broke each task into concrete examples, and exposure was set by time-weighted majority rule. "Dig trenches" ended up unexposed: autonomous excavators can cut straight trenches, but more of the job is careful hand digging around buried pipes.

Taken across all U.S. work, 54% of task time is cognitive or interpersonal and 46% is physical. About 12% of all work cannot be done by any current robot, 23% can be done in purpose-built environments, 10% in structured workplaces and only 1% in unstructured ones. A backtest to 1977 found that jobs more exposed to the robots of their day saw wages and employment fall in later decades.

Who is exposed, and what it costs

Driving tops the rankings: nine of the ten most exposed occupations are vehicle operators. Taxi drivers lead with an index score of 2.2 out of 3, because autonomous vehicles already work on open roads. Recycling and reclamation workers score 1.7, drywall tapers 1.6 and stockers 1.0. Nursing and general repair sit near the bottom. The authors write that present-day robots "can do little of their work even in highly controlled environments."

The demographic pattern runs roughly opposite to LLM exposure. Using 2020 to 2024 American Community Survey data, the most exposed workers are 20 percentage points less likely to be female than unexposed workers and 55 points less likely to hold a bachelor's degree. They earn about $30 less per hour and face an unemployment rate more than twice as high.

Cost is where the picture changes. For every exposed task, Claude estimated the annual cost of deploying the specific robots cited, then compared it with the occupation's total compensation. Packers and packagers are the largest occupation where robots already win. A system costing more than $2 million to buy and install replaces about 14 workers, which works out to about $45,000 a year per worker replaced against roughly $49,000 in labor cost. The U.S. employs about 560,000 packers, and employment in the occupation has fallen 22% since 2015. Robotaxis come out only about $7,000 more expensive than taxi drivers; robots covering a welder's job cost about five times as much as the worker.

Analysis: a long runway, not a cliff

The index separates what robots can technically do from when it pays to use them. Industry and government data suggest robot prices have fallen about 3% a year since the 1990s. At that rate, getting robots to cost parity for 10% of today's work would require a roughly 70% price drop, which takes about four decades. Combining that cost trend with the historical pace of new capabilities, the authors estimate robots will not be cost-competitive for half of physical work until 2085. In a fast scenario, with costs falling up to four times faster and capabilities arriving twice as fast, that date moves to 2050. The authors conclude that robots "would need to sustain record rates of price declines and quality improvements over the coming decades" for rapid physical automation.

Cost is not the only barrier. Claude's barrier analysis finds that capability gaps hold back about 70% of physical tasks, with manipulation, meaning touching and handling objects, the largest gap. Current regulations would block robots from 14% of physical tasks, and human preferences hold them back on about a quarter. The authors also caution, citing economists Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo, that automation raises productivity only once machines become much cheaper than people, not at mere cost parity.

For policymakers, the findings highlight workers that debates about AI and white-collar jobs have largely missed: lower-paid, less-educated, mostly male workers in physically demanding jobs.

What to watch

The authors say early signs of disruption should show up first in drivers and warehouse packers. Humanoids and new manufacturing methods could cut costs faster than 3% a year, and Anthropic plans periodic updates that should show whether the long timeline holds. The paper's longer-term question is whether work itself becomes "more social and interpersonal as robots and AI advance."

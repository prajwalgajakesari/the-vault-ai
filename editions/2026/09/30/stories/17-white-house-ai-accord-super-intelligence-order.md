# Trump and Six AI Chiefs Sign a "Morally Binding" Safety Accord as an Executive Order Renames AI "Super Intelligence"

President Trump and the leaders of six of the country's most powerful AI developers signed a voluntary safety pact at the White House on Tuesday. The pact carries no penalties, no enforcement mechanism and no requirement to publish results. Asked whether it was binding, the president replied, "I think it's morally binding." On the same day he signed an executive order telling federal agencies to stop saying "artificial intelligence" and to say "Super Intelligence" instead.

The document is formally titled the White House Accord on Super Intelligence: Joint Commitment on Frontier Responsibilities. It was signed by Trump, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, Google CEO Sundar Pichai, Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg, Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, OpenAI President Greg Brockman and Elon Musk for SpaceX/xAI. Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella, AMD CEO Lisa Su and Palo Alto Networks CEO Nikesh Arora were at the lunch and the East Room event that preceded the signing. Their names do not appear among the signatories on the published document. Trump posted the one-page text to Truth Social on Tuesday evening.

## What the accord commits companies to

The accord sets out what it calls "four layers of controls and audits." The first layer is internal controls to "monitor the capabilities and alignment" of frontier models. According to SiliconANGLE's reading of the document, that includes tracking capabilities in sensitive areas such as biology and putting systems in place to stop models from launching cyberattacks. The second is a dedicated internal team responsible for making sure those controls work. The third is an "independent external auditor or evaluator." The fourth is an independent board committee that reviews the auditors' findings and oversees remediation. The signatories also agreed to "meet regularly to establish standards and best practices to improve the safety of their systems."

"We believe every company is responsible for developing its own technology safely and in a way that builds trust with customers and the public," the signatories wrote. The text also leaves the door open to legislation: "Over time, it may make sense to codify these steps into laws or regulations. Regardless of whether this is required of companies, we believe that implementing these controls and audits is critical to ensuring a safe future for everyone."

Trump called the document "almost like a constitution, in a way." He said he was "seeing tremendous self-policing, and they understand that they have to self-police." He told reporters the AI leaders had also discussed forming a 10-person committee to "watch over the enterprise," and said he would name an "AI czar" within days. Supporters in industry welcomed the pact. Pichai wrote on X that the accord "contains real tangible steps to promote safe development." Huang said there is "no conflict between innovation, technology and safety."

The executive order, titled "Inaugurating the Era of Super Intelligence," directs agencies to use "Super Intelligence" and "SI" in place of "Artificial Intelligence" and "AI" in correspondence, websites, reports and other non-statutory documents, to the extent the law allows. For now the new terms simply cover whatever already falls under the existing statutory definition of artificial intelligence in federal law, so the order does not change existing regulations or contracts. It gives the president's science and technology adviser 60 days to propose legislative language for a federal definition of "Super Intelligence."
## Voluntary pledges, binding questions

The accord's critics zeroed in on what it leaves out. It creates no regulator and sets no penalties. Companies do not have to disclose audit results, and they control how the safeguards are carried out. Sen. Mark Warner, a Virginia Democrat, called on Congress to pass mandatory testing, evaluation and incident-reporting rules for the most advanced models. "The companies building the most powerful AI systems are warning us that the technology is advancing faster than our safeguards," Warner said. "The president's response? To rename it and tell the companies developing it to regulate themselves." Maurice Mitchell, national director of the Working Families Party, said in a statement: "We cannot trust a multi-billion dollar AI industry to regulate itself."

The pact also sits awkwardly next to what some signatories have recently said in public. OpenAI's chief global affairs officer, Chris Lehane, argued this month that industry standards should "complement – not replace – mandatory federal safeguards." Amodei called in June for "more serious and binding regulation of AI." On Capitol Hill, Sen. Josh Hawley, a Missouri Republican, is proposing legal liability for companies that design AI agents recklessly.

The contrast sharpened a day later. On Wednesday, reports surfaced that the Federal Trade Commission had opened a broad investigation into Anthropic, OpenAI and other frontier labs, as well as the evaluation group METR. According to a senior FTC official quoted by the New York Post, Chairman Andrew Ferguson started the inquiry several weeks ago. The agency plans to issue civil investigative demands for documents and executive testimony under its existing authority over unfair or deceptive practices. The companies that signed a pledge enforced only by conscience now face an agency that can compel answers.

The states are another pressure point. A December executive order created a Justice Department task force to challenge state AI laws, yet the accord offers no federal standard that could preempt them.

## What to watch

The next markers are concrete. Trump has promised to name an AI czar this week, and the proposed 10-person oversight committee has not yet taken shape. The science adviser's 60-day deadline for a statutory definition of "Super Intelligence" falls in late November. The FTC is expected to send its civil investigative demands in the coming weeks. The biggest open question is whether any signatory will voluntarily publish its external audit findings, since the accord does not require it. That would be the first test of whether "morally binding" means anything in practice.

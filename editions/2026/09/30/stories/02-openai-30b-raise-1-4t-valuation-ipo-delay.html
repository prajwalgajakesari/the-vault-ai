OpenAI Seeks at Least $30B at a $1.4 Trillion Valuation as Altman Pushes the IPO Past 2026

5 min read 938 words 5 sources
KEY TAKEAWAY

OpenAI has put its IPO off until it can make confident safety claims about its models, and it is now raising a $30B+ private bridge round at about $1.4 trillion, up 64% from March, to keep paying for its huge compute buildout while Anthropic heads for a November listing.

OpenAI wanted 2026 to be the year it went public. It is now back in the private markets, in talks to raise at least $30 billion at a valuation of about $1.4 trillion. That is a 64 percent jump from the price investors paid six months ago, and the round would be a bridge to a public listing that Sam Altman has now pushed into 2027 at the earliest.

Bloomberg first reported the talks on Tuesday, citing people familiar with the discussions. It said the talks are at an early stage and the terms could still change. The $1.4 trillion figure excludes the new money. It also tops the more than $1.2 trillion valuation Bloomberg had earlier said OpenAI was considering. The news came in the same week as OpenAI's DevDay developer conference, where Altman told reporters that the company would not go public until it could stand behind the safety of its models.

From IPO to Bridge Round

The new round was not part of the original plan. In March, OpenAI closed a round with $122 billion in committed capital at an $852 billion valuation, counting the new investment. Investors included SoftBank, Amazon and Nvidia. It was one of the largest financings Silicon Valley has ever seen, and it was widely expected to be the company's last private raise before a 2026 IPO.

That plan ended this month. In a Fortune interview released on September 12, Altman ruled out a listing this year. "Right now would be an ill-advised moment to go public," he said. Asked about timing, he told Fortune editor-in-chief Alyson Shontell: "I would say not 2026, yeah. We got a lot of stuff to do." He also said, "Meeting this moment of what is going to be required for safety and alignment, and how the industry and governments can work together, I'm happy to be able to do that as a private company."

He went further at DevDay this week. "We intend to continue with AI progress, but as the models have had this surge forward in capability, and we see more of that ahead of us, we have got to be able to make confident safety claims," Altman told reporters after his keynote. He gave no timeline. He did add that waiting too long to go public would be "bad for the world."

The business is growing quickly enough to keep investors interested. Bloomberg reported that OpenAI's annualized revenue run rate passed $40 billion over the summer, up about 70 percent since July. That growth followed a renewed focus on areas such as coding, after Anthropic briefly pulled ahead earlier in the year. At DevDay, OpenAI introduced Dots, an always-on agent that can carry out tasks across multiple applications. It also launched a $500-a-month subscription tier with more usage and faster processing, and cut some of the usage allowances in its existing $200 plan.

Why It Matters

The new round shows what an AI company does when the public markets are not an option. OpenAI's costs are enormous. Building and running frontier models takes huge, ongoing spending on compute, data centers and research, and that spending does not pause while the company delays its IPO. A $30 billion private round keeps that spending going without the quarterly reporting, disclosure requirements and Wall Street pressure that Altman has said could clash with a safety-first approach.

Safety now affects the company's finances as well as its research. This week OpenAI said it would not release GPT-6.1 Astra, a planned model, after it failed internal safety checks. Altman has also backed calls from Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei for stronger external human oversight of advanced AI. Responding to researchers who warn that AI poses an existential risk, Altman recently told Fortune that accepting even a 10 percent chance of AI killing everyone by the end of the decade would be unacceptable. In the Fortune interview he put the principle more broadly: "I think a key principle that we should all agree on is that we cannot take actions that would risk losing control of the future to AI."

The contrast with Anthropic is sharp. As covered in a previous edition, Anthropic has filed its IPO prospectus and is expected to list as soon as November, at a valuation that could exceed $2 trillion. The filing outlines plans to spend $518 billion on cloud computing and infrastructure. The two largest independent AI labs have taken opposite paths. One is going to public investors. The other is asking private investors for more time.

The wider market is cooling too. Semafor noted that the year was expected to end with a strong run of IPOs, but that is stalling. Smart-ring maker Oura and SoftBank-backed data center company SB Energy have both postponed their listings. Analysts quoted by The Wall Street Journal expect cloud startup NScale to delay its investor roadshow. With conditions like these, a private round at a higher valuation may be the easier deal for OpenAI as well as the more cautious one.

What to Watch

Next, watch who leads the round and whether OpenAI gets the full $1.4 trillion valuation, since Bloomberg says the terms are still open. Two other questions are what OpenAI means by "confident safety claims," and whether the stalled GPT-6.1 Astra release becomes a public measure of that standard. The biggest test is Anthropic's planned November debut. If it goes well, investors will press harder on OpenAI's 2027 timeline. If it goes badly, OpenAI's decision to stay private will look well timed.

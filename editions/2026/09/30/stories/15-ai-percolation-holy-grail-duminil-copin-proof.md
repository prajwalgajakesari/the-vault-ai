# Claude Claims Percolation's "Holy Grail" Days After Fields Medalist Duminil-Copin Predicted AI Would Get There First

On August 30, Fields medalist Hugo Duminil-Copin published an essay warning that it was "only a matter of time before the most famous conjecture in our field ... also falls to the bulldozers." Four days later, on September 3, a Lean 4 library appeared in Anthropic's public formal-math repository claiming exactly that: a machine-checked proof that critical percolation dies out in every dimension. Scientific American this week called it a "holy grail" of probability theory. Mathematicians see a likely breakthrough, and many are uneasy about how it arrived.

The problem is known as the dying percolation conjecture, or simply θ(p_c) = 0. According to the community tracker VibeMathed, it was posed by Harry Kesten in his 1982 book on percolation theory and later restated by Duminil-Copin as Conjecture 1 of his 2018 ICM survey. That makes it roughly 44 years old. Benedikt Jahnel of the Technical University of Braunschweig told Scientific American: "If someone manages to solve this problem, they'll probably receive a Fields Medal."

## What was actually proved

Percolation theory began in 1957, when Simon Broadbent and John Hammersley modeled a liquid seeping through porous material. Picture an infinite grid of pipes, each open independently with probability p. Below a critical value, p_c, the open pipes form only finite clusters. Above it, an infinite connected cluster appears. In 1980 Kesten showed that p_c is exactly 1/2 on the two-dimensional square lattice. Exact thresholds are known for only a handful of other cases.

The deeper question is what happens exactly at the threshold. The conjecture says there is no infinite cluster at criticality, so the phase transition is continuous. Mathematicians had proved this in two dimensions and in high dimensions (eleven and above), where averaging arguments work. Dimensions 3 through 10, including our own three-dimensional world, stayed open. As Gil Kalai of the Hebrew University put it on his blog, the problem "was a famous open problem in the intermediate dimensions starting with dimension 3."

The Claude-generated proof avoids attacking the lattice directly. It takes a route laid out in 2024 by Gady Kozma and Shahaf Nitzan, who showed that the dying percolation conjecture would follow from a very general "gluing" inequality for percolation on arbitrary finite graphs. Kalai noted that their conjecture was so general that "many of us expected a counterexample to be discovered before long." According to the repository's README, Claude proved the weakest of these, Kozma–Nitzan Conjecture 3, by way of a stronger additive gluing inequality built on a new chain of covariance bounds it calls a "conditioned slack hierarchy." The library re-proves every classical ingredient it uses from Lean's Mathlib. VibeMathed's independent audit counted 251 files and 97,574 lines, with no unproven placeholders outside the statement file and no added axioms.

The human in the loop was Justin Leder. The repository's provenance notes say the discovery, the informal proof and the Lean formalization "were all produced by the AI system operating autonomously," with Leder responsible for problem selection, direction and the submission. The next day, Ahmed Bou-Rabee of the University of Pennsylvania posted claimed proofs of the remaining Kozma–Nitzan conjectures, found with an OpenAI model and formalized in Lean with Claude.

## Analysis: proof, verification and credit

The formal verification is the main reason this claim is being taken seriously so early. A Lean kernel check does not depend on anyone trusting a language model's prose. It does leave one gap, and the authors point to it themselves: the machine confirms that the code proves the theorem as stated, but it cannot confirm that the stated definitions capture the mathematics people actually mean. The README asks readers to check for themselves that the statement file says what it should. The accompanying 15-page human-readable guide is described as "a reader's guide, not the warrant," and it has not been independently refereed.

Kalai was cautious. "The new Lean verification is promising, but, as you said, we still need to verify if the formalisation is done correctly," he wrote, adding that experts "would like to see the details of the proof." VibeMathed lists the result as a candidate pending review and notes that, as of its September 8 audit, no mathematician had yet read the argument in full.

Reactions to the result itself were mixed. "The result evoked ambivalent feelings," Jahnel told Scientific American, describing joy that the conjecture was settled alongside disillusionment that the decisive step came from a machine. He questioned what prizes like the Fields Medal will mean in the future: "Whether a human being will ever make it onto such a list again is questionable." Duminil-Copin, who once returned to the problem repeatedly and met his main coauthor, Vincent Tassion, while both were working on a simplified version of it, had argued in his essay that a conjecture's value lies in what it inspires: "it is a lighthouse in the night: it illuminates and guides mathematicians in their wanderings, both esthetic and scientific." He also asked that the field "not profane our most beautiful conjectures by using them as mere 'benchmarks' for the next frontier model."

The result adds to a fast run of AI-assisted mathematics this newsletter has followed, from the Talagrand convolution conjecture to the inverse Galois problem for M23 and Claude's nine-loop amplitudes. What sets this one apart is that a leading figure in the field had publicly named it, days earlier, as the problem he hoped a young human would solve.

## What to watch

The next step is human review. Watch for percolation experts to work through the summary and the Challenge.lean statement, for any referee process or arXiv write-up, and for independent checks of the Kozma–Nitzan results from Bou-Rabee. Separately, watch how the community responds to Duminil-Copin's question about which problems young researchers should spend years on, now that major conjectures can be settled within days by an AI system.

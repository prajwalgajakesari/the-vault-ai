Bank of England Warns AI Debt, Lofty Valuations and the Iran Shock Are Raising the Odds of a Financial Stability Hit

5 min read 993 words 4 sources
KEY TAKEAWAY

The Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee says rising, opaque AI debt and high valuations, alongside an Iran-driven energy shock, raise the risk that an AI repricing spreads into sovereign bond markets because fiscal forecasts now lean partly on AI productivity gains.

The Bank of England said on Wednesday that the chance of the financial system's weak points giving way together has gone up. It named two forces that are now pulling in the same direction: a renewed conflict involving Iran, and a fast-growing pile of debt used to pay for artificial intelligence. In the record of its quarterly meeting, the Financial Policy Committee warned that AI valuations are still high enough to allow a sharper correction than July's. It also said a correction would not stay inside tech stocks. Governments' growth forecasts and fiscal plans now partly assume that AI will deliver large productivity gains, so if that expectation fades, the damage could reach sovereign bond markets too.

"The likelihood that interconnected vulnerabilities in the financial system crystallize has risen," the FPC said. The committee is chaired by Governor Andrew Bailey.

The AI debt pile, by the numbers

The record shows how far AI financing has moved from the cash-rich balance sheets of a few hyperscalers into the debt markets. The FPC cited Morgan Stanley estimates that global AI-related debt issuance reached about $450 billion by early September. That is more than double the total for all of 2025. It also cited JPMorgan analysts' projection that debt issuance could fund around $4.1 trillion of AI-related capital expenditure between 2026 and 2030.

Private markets are taking a growing share. The committee pointed to a Morgan Stanley estimate that around $700 billion of data centre capital expenditure between 2026 and 2028 will be financed through private credit. The FPC said the "rapid increase" in AI-related issuance had raised capital markets' exposure to developments in the sector.

The committee was most pointed about how these deals are put together. It warned that rising leverage, limited transparency and, at times, "circular arrangements" in AI financing make risk harder to assess. It said those features could also make losses bigger if expectations for AI investment are not met. The phrase refers to deals in which suppliers, customers and investors in the AI chain finance one another. Bank officials did not name specific deals.

July was a rehearsal, not the main event

The FPC treated July's sell-off in AI and semiconductor stocks as a warning. During that episode, some leveraged investors had to unwind positions as volatility rose, and the resulting deleveraging and portfolio rebalancing made price moves larger. Markets kept working in an orderly way and there were no signs of wider systemic stress. The committee said that did not settle the question.

"Concerns about the sustainability of AI-related earnings and capital expenditure growth may have contributed to market sentiment. The risk of a sharper correction with spillovers to core markets persisted," the record said.

The FPC said hedge fund leverage is still elevated and valuations are still high, held up by strong expectations for future earnings. A large change in those expectations, for example over how fast AI is developed or adopted, could trigger a sharper repricing than the market absorbed in the summer.

The geopolitical shock makes this more dangerous. The FPC said higher oil and gas prices had pushed bond yields to levels not seen since 2008. "Specifically, the re-escalation of the conflict and the associated rises in oil, gas and refined product prices are leading to a more protracted negative supply shock," it said. If an AI repricing hits while sovereign yields are already high, both shocks would land on the same bond markets at once. That is the feedback loop the committee is worried about.

The FPC left the UK countercyclical capital buffer at 2%.

The committee also flagged risks from the technology itself. It said models released in the third quarter of 2026 had kept getting better at completing complex tasks without human direction and at finding and exploiting software vulnerabilities in test environments. In an article published alongside the record, Bailey called for "rigorous model testing, conducted both before and after deployment" before any tighter rules. Regulation, he wrote, "is not, in my view, the right place to start."

Analysis: regulators are converging on AI credit

The Bank of England's record is one of the most detailed public statements so far of a view that has been forming among financial-stability officials for months. The concern is less about whether AI is a bubble and more about who is holding the debt if it turns out to be one. This newsletter has already covered a Federal Reserve official's suggestion that AI companies could become "too big to fail," Reserve Bank of Australia Governor Michele Bullock's comments on the risk of an AI bubble, and market analysis linking heavy AI borrowing to pressure on US Treasury yields. The Bank for International Settlements and Fitch have issued similar warnings over the summer.

The FPC adds a direct link between AI optimism and public finances. If budget forecasts assume AI-driven productivity gains, then the AI story is no longer only an equity-market risk. It also affects the price of government debt. Combined with private-credit opacity and circular financing, this means a disappointment in AI earnings could travel through more channels than a typical sector downturn. Regulators have so far responded with monitoring and disclosure rather than new capital charges. The Bank's choice to quantify the debt pipeline suggests that position may not last.

What to watch

The next checkpoint is the FPC's next quarterly assessment, and whether the Bank starts stress-testing an AI repricing alongside high sovereign yields. Investors should also watch how much of the projected $4.1 trillion in debt-funded AI spending moves into private credit, since that is where the FPC has the least visibility. And if Big Tech earnings in the coming weeks raise new doubts about returns on capital expenditure, the committee's warning that the next correction could be sharper than July's will be tested quickly.

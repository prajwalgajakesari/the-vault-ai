Factory, the San Francisco startup that sells autonomous coding agents to large enterprises, said on September 15 that it has raised $200 million at a $5 billion valuation. That is more than three times the $1.5 billion price it carried after a $150 million Series C in April, a gap of roughly five months. The announcement listed nine institutional investors, three celebrity angels, six marquee customers and one 60 percent cost saving. It did not list revenue.

The round was backed by Blackstone, Khosla Ventures, Sequoia Capital, Insight Partners, Evantic Capital, Sound Ventures, NEA, Mantis VC and Clearlake. Angel investors included Salesforce chief executive Marc Benioff, Altimeter founder Brad Gerstner and former Formula One champion Nico Rosberg. Total funding now exceeds $400 million. Factory did not label the round as a Series D, and it did not say whether the $5 billion figure is pre-money or post-money, a detail that matters when the check is 4 percent of the headline number.

"Across the world's largest enterprises, we are seeing a move from individual coding agents to software factories that serve as the core foundation from which an entire software company operates," said Matan Grinberg, Factory's co-founder and chief executive, in the announcement. "The adoption and results our customers are seeing validate the opportunity to rebuild the entire software development system, and we are still at the beginning."

## From code completion to the whole lifecycle

Factory was founded in 2023 by Grinberg, then a physics PhD student at UC Berkeley, and Eno Reyes, a machine learning engineer. The two met at a San Francisco hackathon, built a first demo in 72 hours and used it to secure seed money from Sequoia partner Shaun Maguire. Its agents, branded Droids, were initially pitched as an alternative to editor-based assistants such as Cursor and GitHub Copilot: agents that take a ticket and return a pull request rather than suggesting the next line.

The product that justifies the new price is broader. Factory 2.0, announced earlier this year, extends the agents from writing code into code review, security analysis, testing, documentation and incident response, all running on a shared platform the company calls a software factory. The pitch is that these stages share context: a security finding can feed the code reviewer, and a production incident can be traced back to the change that caused it. Factory Router, launched after the April round, picks a model per task and, according to the company, cuts token spend by more than 60 percent while maintaining frontier performance. A tool called Agent Effectiveness is meant to show finance teams what their AI spend actually produced.

The deployment story is aimed squarely at regulated buyers. Customers can run Factory in the company's managed cloud, on their own servers, or in fully air-gapped environments, and choose which underlying models the agents use. The company is also pursuing FedRAMP authorization for a GovCloud deployment. Named customers include Nvidia, Blackstone, Royal Bank of Canada, Palo Alto Networks, Adobe and T-Mobile; earlier coverage also cited Morgan Stanley and Ernst & Young. Blackstone appears on both the customer list and the investor list. Factory says hundreds of thousands of developers use the platform, a figure that has not been independently verified and that the company has not broken down between paying enterprise seats and free users.

On revenue, there is nothing. Neither the company blog post, the press release, nor the Reuters, Wall Street Journal or trade coverage of the round included an annual recurring revenue figure. The closest data point is from April, when reporting around the Series C said Factory had doubled revenue each month for six consecutive months, a claim about growth rate rather than scale. Tech Funding News also reported that a $120 million extension in July had already lifted the valuation to $4 billion, which would make the September step-up 25 percent rather than 233 percent. Factory's own materials describe the jump only as from $1.5 billion to $5 billion, and no other outlet corroborated the July round.

The founders closed their blog post with a recruiting note. "We hire for clock speed and conviction," Grinberg and Reyes wrote, adding an appeal to "unreasonably ambitious polymaths."

## Why It Matters

The AI coding market is repricing at a speed that makes five-month triples look ordinary. Cognition, maker of Devin, raised $2 billion at a $48 billion valuation earlier this month, nearly doubling in four months. SpaceX bought Cursor's parent Anysphere for $60 billion in an all-stock deal after Cursor crossed $2 billion in annual recurring revenue. CodeRabbit, which reviews AI-written code, raised $143 million at $1.5 billion in August. Against that backdrop, $5 billion is a mid-tier number, and Factory's investors are betting on a specific thesis: that the winner in enterprises will not be the best code editor but the platform that owns the entire pipeline, including the parts where a compliance officer needs an audit trail.

That thesis is coherent, and the air-gapped and FedRAMP work is a real moat against consumer-first rivals. But it is also the thesis that GitHub, Atlassian and every DevOps vendor of the last decade has pursued, and Copilot Workspace already sits inside the tool most of Factory's target customers pay for. The distinguishing question is whether Factory's agents are meaningfully better at the unglamorous stages, review, testing, incident response, than the incumbents' bolt-ons. The company's announcement offers a 60 percent token saving and a dashboard, but no customer-level outcome data.

The absence of revenue disclosure is the number to watch. Cursor's ARR was public before its acquisition. Cognition has discussed the share of its own code written by Devin. Factory, at $5 billion, has disclosed developer counts and cost reductions but not what customers pay. Investors writing $200 million checks presumably know; the rest of the market is being asked to price the company on logos.

## What to Watch

The next signals are concrete. Watch for Factory to publish an ARR figure or a per-customer case study with hard cycle-time numbers, and for clarification of whether a $4 billion July round actually happened. Watch whether Nvidia or Palo Alto Networks, both customers with large in-house engineering organizations, talk about Factory on earnings calls or at developer events. And watch the competitive response: if GitHub extends Copilot Workspace into incident response and security review at no extra charge, Factory's full-lifecycle pitch becomes a feature rather than a company. The FedRAMP authorization, when it lands, will be the first external validation that does not come from an investor.

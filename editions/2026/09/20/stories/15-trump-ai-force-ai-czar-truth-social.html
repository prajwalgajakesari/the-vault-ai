Trump Promised an 'AI Force' Modeled on Space Force. Space Force Took an Act of Congress and $29 Billion a Year. This Took a Post.

6 min read 1268 words 5 sources
KEY TAKEAWAY

Trump's 'AI Force' has no stated mission, budget, statutory basis, or leader, and the Space Force it is modeled on required an act of Congress and roughly $29 billion a year.

President Trump announced on Saturday that he is creating an "AI Force" modeled on the Space Force and will soon name a new AI czar. Forty-eight hours later, the only people who seem to know what the AI Force is are the ones who have not been asked.

The announcement came in a lengthy Truth Social post on September 19. It did not say whether the AI Force is a military branch, a federal agency, a White House task force, or a slogan. It did not name a budget, a statutory basis, a home department, or a leader. The White House did not respond to Axios's request for comment. Four tech industry representatives told Politico, on condition of anonymity, that the sector was not informed before the post went up. "Nobody knows what the idea even is," one of them said.

What the post did contain was a governing philosophy. "We will not in any way hinder or stifle the Growth of this incredible Industry. Rather, we will cherish it, help it, and watch over it, as it grows!" Trump wrote. "However, we will also be looking for BAD, and we can do that, very easily, with our already existing Criminal and Civil Justice System." He called AI "the next Industrial Revolution, or Internet," and predicted it would be "even larger and more impactful, possibly as much as 25% of our Country's GDP." The czar job came with one listed qualification: "Only High I.Q. individuals need apply!"

The comparison to Space Force is the most concrete thing in the post, and it cuts against the idea that any of this can happen by Truth Social decree. Space Force is a branch of the armed services. It was created by Congress in the National Defense Authorization Act that Trump signed in December 2019, after roughly 18 months of negotiation with the Pentagon and the Armed Services committees, and it draws an annual appropriation in the neighborhood of $29 billion. Trump can rename an office, sign an executive order, and appoint a special government employee. He cannot stand up a new service branch, or fund a new agency, without legislation and money that Congress has not been asked for.

That distinction matters because the czar half of the plan revives a post the White House had let lapse. David Sacks was named AI and crypto czar in December 2024 and left the formal role in March 2026 when his special-government-employee clock ran out; he remains co-chair of the President's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology. Six months ago, according to Axios, the administration had no plan to refill the seat. In a CBS News interview last week, Sacks said fears of AI threatening humanity are overblown and that development should continue.

The timing is the tell. Trump's post landed one week after Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published an essay arguing the current pace of AI development is too fast and asking the government to help slow it. OpenAI's Sam Altman and SpaceX's Elon Musk said they agreed with the analysis, and Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis has made a similar case. On September 8, former Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon resigned publicly, telling CBS the labs are "gambling with our lives." Anthropic alignment researcher Evan Hubinger has said he personally puts the odds of AI killing all humans within a decade at greater than 10 percent. Trump's response, earlier this month, was to call extinction fears a "hoax" and to say the only guardrail the technology needs is a "strong and smart" president.

Congress has been getting a different briefing. In a closed-door session this week, Geoffrey Hinton, the Nobel laureate often called the godfather of AI, told lawmakers they have "maybe a year" to regulate the industry, according to CNN. Sen. Bernie Sanders wants a pause on advanced development and a permanent ban on superintelligence. Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez used a Friday campaign email to call for strict safety standards and to warn Congress not to bail out Big Tech if the "AI bubble" pops. Democrats, per Axios, have not united behind a single AI agenda.

The polling explains why a president who has spent the month dismissing AI critics suddenly wants a Force. A New York Times-Siena survey of 1,503 likely voters released this week found 61 percent opposed to building data centers for AI, including 47 percent of Republicans and 75 percent of Democrats. An AP-NORC poll found 53 percent of Americans highly concerned about AI's environmental impact. A Politico-Public First poll put the share of adults who see at least a moderate risk that advanced AI could eventually destroy humanity at 63 percent. Trump's post insisted data centers bring "Higher Salaries, Lower Taxes, and Safer Streets," and blamed "the radical left" for calling for "the decimation, or destruction, of AI."

Industry reaction split along predictable lines, once it got over its surprise. Taylor Barkley of the Abundance Institute, which favors light regulation, told Politico that "Any AI Force or AI Czar should have one job: keep the field open so entrepreneurs at every scale can compete, free from onerous regulation." One of the anonymous industry representatives said they could not tell whether the new body was meant to write more rules or to recommend ways to speed development further.

Why It Matters

Strip out the branding and the post describes the status quo: no new guardrails, enforcement through existing criminal and civil law, and a personnel appointment. That is roughly the posture the administration has held since January, and it is the posture the biggest labs are now publicly asking Washington to abandon. The novelty is that Trump has attached a name and an org chart to it, which lets him tell voters he is "doing something" about AI without committing to a single obligation the industry would have to meet.

The Space Force analogy also carries a risk the White House may not have priced in. If the AI Force is genuinely meant to resemble a service branch, that is a military framing for a civilian technology, at a moment when Gizmodo and others are reporting on near-catastrophic errors from AI systems already deployed in defense settings. If it is not meant to resemble a service branch, then the analogy is decoration, and the entity is a task force, which the executive branch can create on a Tuesday and dissolve on a Wednesday.

The 25 percent of GDP figure deserves its own asterisk. It appeared without a source, a timeframe, or a definition of what counts as AI. For comparison, the entire information sector, including all of software, telecom, and publishing, is around 5 to 6 percent of U.S. GDP today. A number four to five times that size is not a forecast; it is a mood.

What to Watch

The first real signal will be the czar pick and its paperwork. If the appointee is a special government employee with a 130-day clock, as Sacks was, the AI Force is a White House advisory function with no independent authority. If the administration sends Congress a legislative proposal or a budget line, it is something else. Watch also for whether the White House press office ever defines the entity in a fact sheet, whether the Senate Commerce and Armed Services committees ask for one, and whether the labs that spent September calling for a slowdown say anything at all about a body whose founding document promises never to slow them down. As of Sunday night, the president had moved on to polling his followers on whether to rename artificial intelligence "Superior Intelligence" or "Extreme Intelligence."

Max Harris listens for the hiss.

The 26-year-old EDM producer, who records as H4RRIS, has spent months turning his Instagram feed into a public docket, naming tracks he believes were generated with AI and passed off as human-made. His method is forensic: a sharp hiss under the mix, melodic lines that stutter at the same instant, fingers that flicker in and out of existence on-screen.

**"Something I've noticed with a lot of Suno-generated tracks is that a lot of the time, you'll hear the vocals and other melodic elements will start stuttering at the same exact time,"** Harris told The Verge in an interview published August 29, 2026. **"These models still have a hard time fully separating different parts of the songs they're trained on."**

Harris was not first. He started after reading Threads posts by Nihil Young, a 39-year-old Italian turntablist-turned-producer who has mixed and mastered for Sony Music, Warner and Universal. Young's suspicion is narrower: that some producers are feeding finished commercial recordings into generative platforms and asking for variations.

**"These songs are the sound of Suno, which to me means that the people behind the songs are just straight up uploading original, copyrighted tracks of artists like Madonna and asking the platform to remix them,"** Young said, pointing to Brisbane DJ Josh Fawaz's cover of "Like a Prayer." **"If they can do it with major artists like her, they can do it with you, me, and everyone else."**

That track is the clearest test case yet. It spent 16 weeks in the ARIA top 20, peaked at No. 2 in May, and has been streamed more than 48 million times on Spotify. Fawaz acknowledged using AI only after fans and producers raised suspicions. On August 25, the Australian Recording Industry Association banned wholly AI-generated recordings from its charts and awards.

## The numbers behind the hunt

Producers are auditing by ear because nobody else can keep up.

Deezer, the only major service that publicly tags AI music, reported on July 21, 2026 that synthetic tracks had for the first time crossed half of all new daily uploads: roughly 90,000 fully AI-generated tracks a day at June's peak. In 2025 alone it detected and tagged more than 13.4 million. The trajectory is steep: 39 percent of uploads in January 2026, 44 percent by April.

**"We have reached a pivotal moment, but we have the technology to keep track of the development, and an ambition to do what's right,"** said Deezer chief executive Alexis Lanternier. **"If the industry can align and come together to create shared standards, we can build a healthy and sustainable music ecosystem, where great music wins."**

The listening numbers are far smaller, and that asymmetry is the story. Fully AI-generated music accounts for just 1 to 3 percent of streams on Deezer, because tagged tracks are excluded from recommendations. But up to 85 percent of the streams those tracks did attract were fraudulent in 2025, against 8 percent fraud across the whole catalog. The flood is not an audience. It is a payout-farming operation. Deezer's detector, built on model-specific artifacts, runs at a claimed 99.8 percent accuracy, and Billboard now licenses it to flag AI tracks on its charts.

Spotify took a different route. It removed more than 75 million spam and fraudulent uploads in the year before its September 2025 policy update, and backed a DDEX metadata standard letting rightsholders disclose where AI was used — vocals, instrumentation, post-production. That credits system entered beta on April 16, 2026. It is disclosure by opt-in. Apple Music's labels arrive later this year.

## Why it matters

Authenticity has quietly become a commercial property. In a Deezer-commissioned Ipsos survey of 9,000 people across eight countries, 97 percent could not distinguish AI music from human music in a blind test — yet 80 percent said AI-generated songs should be labeled, and 52 percent said they should be kept out of the main charts. If listeners cannot hear the difference but care enormously about it, value migrates from the waveform to the label. Attribution, not audio quality, is the scarce good now.

The arms race is asymmetric. Deezer's tool spots signatures unique to each model, so every model update is a potential evasion. The human method does not scale to 90,000 tracks a day and carries an error rate nobody can measure. Harris named MANSA's "Midnight on My Mind" and Danny and Ian Asher's "Take Me (To The Moon)" as suspected AI productions; neither act has commented, and there is no definitive public evidence either way. A culture of mistrust catches real fraud and innocent people with equal enthusiasm.

The fight also runs upstream. On August 28, Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell sued Anthropic in the Northern District of California over an alleged campaign of torrenting and scraping copyrighted works to train Claude, naming co-founders Dario Amodei and Benjamin Mann as defendants and seeking up to $150,000 per infringed work across tens of thousands of compositions. Suno and Udio face more than a dozen comparable suits. The producers' grievance and the publishers' are the same one at different altitudes: Young fears his catalog could be fed to a model and returned as someone else's single; the publishers say theirs already were. CISAC and PMP Strategy estimate nearly 25 percent of creators' revenues are at risk by 2028 — as much as 4 billion euros.

## What to watch

Watch whether Deezer's licensed detector becomes a shared utility: if Spotify or Apple adopt it, opt-in disclosure becomes moot. Watch which chart bodies follow ARIA. And watch the Anthropic docket, which will set the price of training data for everyone downstream.

Finally, watch the detectives. Young says his posts drew hacking attempts and harassment, and that he suspects someone bought fake followers to inflate his own Spotify numbers and discredit him. He has paused the callouts. Detection may eventually be solved by machines. The willingness to say something out loud is still a human input, and it is the part of this system with no redundancy.

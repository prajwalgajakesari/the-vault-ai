On July 29, Britain's energy regulator published a figure that reframes the entire debate about AI infrastructure in Europe. The queue for electricity demand connections had swollen from 41 gigawatts to 125 gigawatts in under a year. At least 80GW of that surge came from data centre projects. Ofgem's problem is not that the projects are too large. It is that the regulator has no reliable way of knowing which of them are real.

That uncertainty is now the binding constraint on Europe's AI buildout — not chips, not capital, not planning permission. Britain is the test case for a regulatory response; the Netherlands is the cautionary tale for what happens when it comes too late.

Ofgem's consultation, open until September 16, proposes two instruments. The first is a Data Centre Commitment Fee, payable when a large data centre development accepts a connection offer, refunded on energisation and forfeited if the project exits the queue early. The proposed range is £237,500 to £712,500 per megawatt — roughly 2.5 to 7.5 percent of average project costs. The second is a set of data-centre-specific queue management milestones requiring developers to evidence financial capability, commercial maturity and procurement activity to keep their slot. Projects that stall get removed.

**"Britain's electricity demand connections queue has more than tripled in size in less than a year, and consumers should not bear the risks created by speculative projects taking up space in the system,"** said Eleanor Warburton, Ofgem's Director for Energy System Design and Development. **"Where speculative projects take up space in the queue, they can delay other schemes and create uncertainty about future network needs."**

The regulator has a precedent it likes to cite. Since earlier connections reforms took effect on the generation side, around 7.8GW of projects have been accelerated by an average of six years. The consultation references roughly 73GW of data centre demand in the current queue, and Ofgem's assessment is blunt: a queue stuffed with non-viable projects slows the viable ones and corrupts the signals used to plan network investment.

## The Dutch Preview

Across the North Sea, the theoretical is already operational. Since July 1, 2026, there have been no new electricity grid connections in the region around the city of Utrecht — an area bounded roughly by Breukelen, Vianen and Driebergen, home to some 800,000 people. Applications for heavy industrial connections were already frozen; the July cutoff extended it to small consumers, including new housing.

State Secretary for Climate and Green Growth Jo-Annes de Bat described the measure as pressing a pause button, and said enough capacity had been reserved to connect roughly 35,000 homes already in the pipeline. Utrecht's mayor, Sharon Dijksma, was less diplomatic, calling the announcement a bitter pill and writing that **"we are also not going to let this slide. Also because we warned the national government about this much earlier."**

The municipality had commissioned its own impact study before the freeze landed. Researchers put the annual damage of a full freeze at between 75 million and 225 million euros, hitting businesses, schools, transport operators and the city's own fleet electrification plans. Utrecht expects one in five housing developments to fail as a result. The structural fix — expanding the high-voltage substation near Breukelen and building a new station north of Utrecht — may not arrive until 2031, and a site has not been chosen. Huib van Essen of the Utrecht Provincial Council called it extraordinarily optimistic to think the problem would resolve within the six-month review window. In late July, gas generators were trucked in as an emergency stopgap.

## Why It Matters

Across the FLAP-D hubs — Frankfurt, London, Amsterdam, Paris, Dublin — developers now wait an average of seven to ten years for a grid connection, stretching to thirteen years in the most congested markets, against a construction timeline of eighteen to twenty-four months. Dublin has operated a de facto moratorium. ACER, the EU energy regulators agency, put direct grid congestion costs at 4.3 billion euros in 2024, excluding the indirect cost of delayed projects.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. The European Commission's AI Continent Action Plan targets a tripling of EU data centre capacity within five to seven years. The IEA calculates that the announced project pipeline represents about 130 percent of installed capacity today — but models actual growth to 2030 at just 70 percent above 2024 levels, precisely because of delays and local grid constraints. Europe's share of global data centre capacity has already fallen from more than 25 percent in 2015 to 15 percent in 2024. The European Data Centre Association puts the investment needed between 2026 and 2031 at 176 billion euros.

The divergence with the United States is not about who has queue problems — America has roughly 2,300GW of generation and storage stuck in interconnection queues, more than its entire installed capacity. The divergence is in the response. American hyperscalers have largely stopped waiting for the grid, signing nuclear power purchase agreements, building behind-the-meter gas, and co-locating generation with compute. Europe's answer, so far, is administrative: better rationing of a queue nobody is adding much capacity to. A commitment fee filters applicants. It does not produce a single additional electron.

## What to Watch

The September 16 consultation close is the first marker, and the split in responses will be revealing: well-capitalised hyperscalers have every reason to back a fee that clears out competitors, while smaller developers will argue it entrenches incumbents. Where Ofgem lands within its proposed range matters enormously: the gap between £237,500 and £712,500 per megawatt is a threefold difference in deterrent strength. Watch the Utrecht six-month review, due around January 2027, and whether a site for the northern substation is finally named. And watch capital flows. If queue discipline in Britain and rationing in the Netherlands persist through 2027, the projects will not disappear — they will move to Spain, the Nordics and wherever else the grid still has room, redrawing Europe's AI map in the process.

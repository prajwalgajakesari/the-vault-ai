The claim that AI can do science stopped being a press release in 2026 and became a citation. On May 19, Nature published two multi-agent systems back to back — Google's Co-Scientist and FutureHouse's Robin — each reporting wet-lab results that began as machine-generated hypotheses. Two months earlier, on March 25, the journal had published Sakana AI's The AI Scientist, the system behind the first fully AI-generated paper to clear human peer review. Three systems, one journal, one year. What they actually show is narrower than the headlines.

Co-Scientist (Gottweis et al., Nature 655, 487–496) runs on Gemini 2.0 and coordinates six specialized agents under a Supervisor. A Generation agent mines literature and stages simulated scientific debates; a Reflection agent peer-reviews the output; a Ranking agent runs an Elo tournament of pairwise hypothesis debates. The authors call the loop generate, debate, evolve. A Meta-review agent propagates feedback into the other agents' prompts — no fine-tuning required.

The evaluation numbers are real but modest. Across 203 research goals, top-hypothesis Elo climbed with test-time compute. On 15 problems set by seven biomedical experts, Co-Scientist earned an average preference rank of 2.36 against five model baselines, with novelty rated 3.64 out of 5 — figures the authors caveat as subjective.

The wet-lab column is where skepticism belongs. From a search space of 2,300 approved drugs across 34 cancer types, 30 top-ranked acute myeloid leukaemia hypotheses went to oncologists, who picked five to test. Three inhibited cell viability. In a second, unguided round, three drugs were tested and one worked: KIRA6, with an IC50 of 10 nM in KG-1a cells versus 180 nM in the non-cancerous TK6 line — an 18-fold therapeutic window. For liver fibrosis, three expert-selected hypotheses yielded two compounds with anti-fibrotic activity in human hepatic organoids. That is eight compounds tested, six hits, in immortalized cell lines and organoids. Nothing in an animal. Nothing in a person.

The authors say so themselves: **the validation of Co-Scientist's hypotheses, while successful, remains preliminary.** And: **they are not a replacement for the rigorous preclinical and clinical assessment that is typically required for therapeutic validation.**

The most striking result is not a drug. Asked why unrelated bacteria share antibiotic-resistance gene cassettes, Co-Scientist proposed in two days the same mechanism José Penadés's group had spent roughly a decade establishing and had not yet published. That is recapitulation, not discovery — no new experiment tested it — but it is the cleanest evidence yet that debate-and-rank can reach a correct, non-obvious hypothesis.

Sakana's contribution differs in kind. The AI Scientist writes code, runs experiments, drafts LaTeX and reviews itself. Its headline: a manuscript scoring 6.33 at the ICLR 2025 ICBINB workshop, above the acceptance threshold and higher than 55% of human submissions — then withdrawn, as pre-agreed with organizers. The Nature paper adds an Automated Reviewer hitting 69% balanced accuracy against thousands of human OpenReview decisions. Sakana's own limitations list is blunt: naive ideas, hallucinated citations, duplicated figures.

Then came the counterweight. On July 29, a 25-author team led by Peter Kirgis and Sayash Kapoor, with Arvind Narayanan, posted what they call shadow evaluations: give an agent the central open-ended question of an unpublished paper, then let its real authors grade the output. They ran two NeurIPS 2026 submissions, gave frontier agents six days and $3,000 of API credit per run, and got two unambiguous rejections — 2 out of 6, and 1 out of 6.

The reviews are the story. Viet Nguyen of the University of Toronto, grading the agent's attempt at his own distribution-shift problem, wrote that leaping from a handful of failed tests to a universal negative claim was **a huge leap, a kind of ‘proof by example’ fallacy that is highly non-scientific.** David Africa of the UK AI Security Institute wrote of the other paper: **The experiments and methodological choices were bizarre, and hard to understand. The results seem clearly a result of post hoc choices.**

The team's conclusion is precise: today's agents **can do the engineering of AI research, but struggle with critical parts of the research lifecycle.** Five failure modes recur, including poor judgment about the publishable bar and failure to backtrack. One agent falsified six approaches within fourteen hours, concluded no solution existed, and never revisited that call across the remaining 110 hours. Both runs ended with more than half the budget unspent. The authors flag the limits: n equals 2, and reviewers knew the papers were AI-written.

## Why It Matters

These papers converge on an unglamorous conclusion: what has been automated is hypothesis generation and literature synthesis, not science. Co-Scientist needed oncologists to cut thirty candidates to five and technicians to run every assay. Robin's collaborators executed all the physical experiments — and when Robin reported that ripasudil boosted retinal phagocytosis 7.5-fold, human re-analysis put it at 1.75-fold, a fourfold-plus discrepancy in its flagship result.

That is the shape of the bottleneck moving. If hypothesis generation was ever the rate limiter, it is not now. Verification is: reagents, robot time, animal models, and the judgment to tell a promising idea from a plausible-sounding one. Cheap hypotheses plus expensive validation is not acceleration — it is a widening queue. FutureHouse's 200-fold speedup claim covers the part of the pipeline that was already fast.

Nature's editors put it plainly the day they published both systems: **AI scientists can and should empower human researchers. They cannot and should not replace them.**

## What to Watch

First, whether any Co-Scientist or Robin candidate advances past cell culture — an in vivo result would be the first real validation of the paradigm, not a viability check. Second, whether shadow evaluations scale; Kirgis's team plans runs across more papers with newer models, and an n of 20 would settle far more than an n of 2. Third, the closed loop: Co-Scientist's authors want these systems wired into automated labs. That is the only version where the bottleneck genuinely moves — and where nobody is left checking whether 7.5-fold was really 1.75.

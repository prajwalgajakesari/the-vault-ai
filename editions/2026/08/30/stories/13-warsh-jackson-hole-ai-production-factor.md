On his 100th day in the job, in front of a room packed with central bankers and academics, Kevin Warsh spent the opening of his first Jackson Hole keynote as Federal Reserve Chairman on a proposition no Fed chief has stated quite so plainly: artificial intelligence may need to be written into the production function itself.

**"We recognize that AI is a new variable—potentially a new factor of production—that will have consequences for both the economy and the conduct of monetary policy,"** Warsh told the Kansas City Fed's economic policy symposium on August 28. Factors of production — land, labour, capital — are the inputs from which economists construct estimates of potential output, the speed at which an economy can expand without generating inflation. Add a fourth input, and the speed limit moves.

Warsh, whose speech was titled *In Our Time*, set it against the secular stagnation consensus of the post-2008 decade. **"Well, times sure have changed. We've come to a hinge point in history,"** he said. **"The potential for substantially higher growth is on the rise. Ever-expanding pools of capital are pouring into AI-related infrastructure of all sorts. A kind of hyper–Moore's law seems to be playing out."** He put annualised token sales at the two leading AI labs alone above $100 billion, up more than 500 percent from a year earlier.

## The Numbers Behind the Claim

The capex evidence is not in dispute. Warsh told the symposium that the four-quarter change in investment in equipment and intangibles has been running near 9 percent, the highest since 2021, and that more than half of this year's capex growth can likely be ascribed to the AI buildout. Epoch AI puts investment in AI data centres, compute hardware and networking at roughly 1.4 percent of US GDP in the first quarter of 2026, double the 2015–2022 average for computing infrastructure and now the leading driver of growth in US private investment. Goldman Sachs projects AI capex at about 1.8 percent of GDP this year, rising to 2.5 percent in 2027.

The productivity evidence is thinner. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on August 6 that nonfarm business labour productivity rose 1.4 percent at an annual rate in the second quarter of 2026, and 2.2 percent from a year earlier. Across the business cycle that began in the fourth quarter of 2019, productivity has grown at an annualised 2.1 percent — better than the 1.5 percent of the previous cycle, and exactly equal to the long-run rate since 1947. Whatever AI is doing to aggregate supply, it has not yet shown up as a break in the series.

Warsh did not claim otherwise. His AI section was built almost entirely from questions: **"Will the application of AI cause a significant, sustained rise in productivity across the economy? And if so, when?"** and **"Will token usage be complementary or competitive to labor?"** A new Fed task force on productivity and jobs will take these up, though Warsh added that its recommendations **"will come later and have no bearing on decisions we make in the current policy conjuncture."**

## Why It Matters

The reason this is more than intellectual throat-clearing sits in Warsh's own second principle of policymaking, stated later in the same speech: **"all we observe directly is activity. We never see, and can only infer, what's really happening on the supply side."**

That is precisely the problem an AI capex boom creates. Investment on this scale raises aggregate demand immediately — the concrete, the turbines, the chips, the electricians — while any productivity payoff arrives later, if at all. For a stretch that could run years, the two are hard to tell apart in the data. A central bank that assumes the supply response is coming runs policy looser than warranted; one that treats a genuine supply expansion as excess demand over-tightens into it. Warsh inherits an economy where 12-month PCE inflation stands at 3.7 percent, the six-month rate at 4.1 percent, and 54 percent of the 199 PCE components are rising faster than 3 percent, against a pre-pandemic norm of 32 percent. His judgement that he would be **"hard pressed to describe broad financial conditions as restrictive"** was read by Axios and others as leaving rate increases on the table.

The profession is nowhere near agreement on what the supply side will deliver. Daron Acemoglu, the MIT Nobel laureate, has projected AI lifting US GDP by roughly 1.1 percent over a decade and annual total factor productivity by about 0.05 percent — orders of magnitude below the boom scenarios. Martin Baily of Brookings, who chaired Bill Clinton's Council of Economic Advisers, offers the institutional version of the same caution: companies do not change that fast. Stanford's Erik Brynjolfsson sits closer to Warsh on capability but not on visibility. **"AI capabilities are advancing far faster than our understanding of the economic implications,"** he wrote in the *We Must Act Now* statement released in July and signed by more than 200 economists and 16 Nobel laureates, Acemoglu among them. Anton Korinek of the University of Virginia, an organiser, was blunter: **"We are driving in the fog, and it is extraordinarily difficult to anticipate what will happen next."**

## What to Watch

Three markers. The BLS publishes revised second-quarter productivity figures on September 3; upside revisions would be the first hard aggregate evidence for the optimistic case, and the labour share reading of 52.9 percent — the lowest since 1947 — bears watching alongside it. Second, whether the Fed's longer-run potential growth estimates start to move in the Summary of Economic Projections, which would signal the factor-of-production framing has travelled from a speech into the staff models. Third, whether the productivity and jobs task force can offer a defensible way to separate the demand impulse of the buildout from any supply response.

Warsh closed by saying he was committed to a discipline, not a decision. On AI, he has committed the Fed to a question — and nobody, inside the institution or outside it, yet knows how to answer it in time for the next meeting.

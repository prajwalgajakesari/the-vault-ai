Elon Musk's xAI is asking a federal judge in Denver to answer a question no court has squarely resolved: whether the choices that shape what an AI model says are protected expression, or merely engineering.

The company filed x.AI LLC v. Weiser in the U.S. District Court for the District of Colorado on April 9, 2026, docketed as 1:26-cv-01515. The sole defendant is Colorado Attorney General Phil Weiser. The relief sought is sweeping: a declaration that Senate Bill 24-205, the Colorado AI Act, is unconstitutional, and a permanent injunction against its enforcement. Nearly five months later, the case has done more than freeze one state statute. It has become the first courtroom test of the Trump administration's campaign to clear state AI regulation off the board.

Colorado's law, passed in 2024, was the country's most ambitious attempt to regulate AI used in consequential decisions. It applied to developers and deployers of high-risk systems influencing employment, housing, education, lending, insurance and health care. Covered entities owed a duty of reasonable care against algorithmic discrimination, defined as unlawful differential treatment or impact on protected classes, plus impact assessments, risk management programs, consumer notice and appeal rights. Enforcement sat exclusively with the attorney general; there was no private right of action. Set to take effect February 1, 2026, the date was pushed to June 30 while lawmakers negotiated amendments.

xAI's complaint advances four constitutional theories, and it is worth stating plainly that these are allegations, not findings. The company argues the Act compels speech in violation of the First Amendment by forcing changes to Grok's training data, fine-tuning, system prompts and outputs; that it burdens interstate commerce under Article I by reaching development conducted entirely outside Colorado; that it is unconstitutionally vague under the Due Process Clause; and that it violates equal protection because the statute carves out from its discrimination definition any output that expands applicant pools to increase diversity or redress historical discrimination. The law's provisions, xAI alleged, **prohibit developers of AI systems from producing speech that the State of Colorado dislikes, while compelling them to conform their speech to a State-enforced orthodoxy on controversial topics of great public concern.** Absent an injunction, the complaint warns, the law would **substitute Colorado's political preferences for the national economic and security imperative of American AI dominance.**

Weiser's office declined to comment. The law's sponsors did not. State Representative Brianna Titone, a lead sponsor, told The Colorado Sun the suit reads as a fishing expedition that misreads the statute. **SB 205 is about consequential decisions, not about freedom of speech,** Titone said. **We are not restricting speech.** Governor Jared Polis is no clean witness for either side: he signed the bill in 2024 while urging the legislature to reexamine its focus on discriminatory results regardless of intent, and Weiser himself had earlier called the law problematic.

Then Washington arrived. On April 24, 2026, the Justice Department moved to intervene on xAI's side and filed its own complaint resting on the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause. **Laws that require AI companies to infect their products with woke DEI ideology are illegal,** said Assistant Attorney General Harmeet K. Dhillon of the Civil Rights Division. Federal intervention against a state AI statute is unusual, and it flows directly from a December 2025 executive order that created a DOJ AI Litigation Task Force, directed Commerce to review state AI laws, and conditioned federal broadband money on states dropping onerous rules. That order named Colorado.

Three days later, Magistrate Judge Cyrus Y. Chung granted a joint motion by xAI and Weiser's office staying enforcement. The order requires xAI to file a preliminary injunction motion or amended complaint within 28 days of Colorado finalizing implementing rules or enacting replacement legislation, and holds the stay until 14 days after the court rules. Colorado did not wait. On May 14, Polis signed SB 26-189, repealing and reenacting the AI Act as a narrower Automated Decision-Making Technology law effective January 1, 2027. Gone are the duty of care, impact assessments and risk-management mandates. In their place: pre-use notice, disclosure within 30 days of an adverse decision, three-year recordkeeping, fault-based liability apportionment, and a 60-day cure period. Its fiscal note projects $56,000 in new state spending.

## Why It Matters

The stakes are not Colorado's. They are the architecture of American AI governance. States enacted 109 AI laws through July 1, 2026, with 29 states passing AI legislation this year alone and 1,561 bills introduced across 45 states as of March. Illinois and Texas AI-discrimination laws took effect January 1, 2026. Industry calls this a patchwork; xAI told the court that state-level regulation applied **in a patchwork across the country can have the effect to hamper innovation and deter competition in an open market.**

The constitutional question underneath is genuinely open. FIRE's Greg Lukianoff and Adam Goldstein, who back xAI's theory, framed it as whether developers **are exercising editorial discretion, like newspaper editors, or are they just building systems, like plumbers.** Their argument leans on Moody v. NetChoice and 303 Creative. A substantial body of scholarship, including work in the Washington University Law Review, holds the opposite: when a generative system emits text, no rights-holding person is communicating, and the outputs are conduct subject to ordinary regulation. Courts have not chosen. Jon Iwry of the Wharton Accountable AI Lab called the case an early proxy battle over who will ultimately control the American AI economy, and whether US AI policy is driven by consumer protection and civil rights or by competitiveness.

## What to Watch

Three things. First, Weiser's rulemaking under SB 26-189, due by January 1, 2027 — that filing starts xAI's 28-day clock and forces the company to amend its complaint or concede the new law moots it. Second, whether the First Amendment claim survives contact with a statute stripped of the duty of care it was written to attack; a disclosure-only regime is a harder target. Third, whether the DOJ task force files next against California, Illinois or Texas. Colorado was the most vulnerable target, not the last one.

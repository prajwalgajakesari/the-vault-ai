Project OT had a code name, a Hawaii retreat, a playbook, and a date on the calendar. What it did not have was technology that worked.

Reuters reported on August 26 that Meta spent most of 2026 building toward an internal restructuring program called Project OT — short for Organization Transformation — that would have cut some teams by as much as 60 percent and handed their work to AI agents supervised by small pods of humans. Meta got halfway. It cut roughly 10 percent of its global workforce in May, close to 8,000 people. Then, hours before those cuts were announced on May 20, Mark Zuckerberg met with senior executives and killed the second wave, which had been penciled in for November.

The reason, according to internal documents and data reviewed by Reuters, was not squeamishness about layoffs. It was that the agents did not work well enough to justify them.

The vision took shape at Meta's annual leadership retreat in January, held at Zuckerberg's Hawaii estate, where executives — influenced in part by AI-structured startups they had observed in Asia — laid out an "AI-native" model for the company. Engineers, designers, product managers and researchers would collapse into a single generalist title: "builder." Middle-management layers would thin out. Small pods of two or three engineers and a designer would replace teams of 10 to 20, working in four-week prototype sprints instead of six-month planning cycles. Under a related "village" structure, senior leaders would oversee 30 to 50 people while pod leads ran daily work without formal management authority.

The pilot came from product executive Ime Archibong, who ran five small technology pods last year and made the pitch in an internal post. **"In basketball, embracing the fast break lets you take more shots — and better ones,"** Archibong wrote. **"We expect the same with AI tools: they allow us to explore more ideas with less cost and higher fidelity."** By June, at least 11 Meta units, including engineering and research groups, had adopted pod structures. One HR executive projected the total reduction would be at least as large as Meta's 2023 cuts, which removed 25 percent of the workforce.

Then the numbers came in. In an internal post, Chief Technology Officer Andrew Bosworth reported that code changes to Meta's software platforms and infrastructure had risen 220 percent year over year. New or improved features actually shipped to users rose 36 percent. Major technical and security incidents climbed 40 percent, and the time employees spent responding to them rose 70 percent. Infrastructure teams had warned in March of "reliability warning signs." An April post said AI agents operating without sufficient oversight were taking "large-scale, disruptive actions that humans are unlikely to execute." The failure surfaced publicly in June, when hackers exploited Meta's AI-powered customer support bot to seize high-profile Instagram accounts, including the dormant Obama White House account.

The human side went no better. Meta installed tracking software on US employees' devices to capture keystrokes and mouse movements — training data intended to teach AI agents how to operate a computer. Internal forums filled with protest, flyers appeared in office bathrooms directing staff to a petition, labor organizing gained traction, and Meta's half-year Pulse survey showed employee sentiment falling to 55 percent favorable from 74 percent. A lawsuit filed by 26 employees alleged the company used an internal AI platform to select layoff targets, disproportionately hitting workers with disabilities. In some engineering units, headcount fell as much as 30 percent by the end of May through transfers and cuts.

At a July town hall, Zuckerberg conceded the core assumption had been wrong. The **"trajectory of the agentic development over at least the last four months hasn't really accelerated in the way that we expected,"** he told staff, adding that Meta's investment in AI agents had not **"come to fruition yet."** In a statement to Reuters, Meta said: **"Ultimately, we didn't move forward with every scenario from the exercise — and it was never assumed we would."**

## Why It Matters

The 220-versus-36 ratio is the most important pair of numbers any company has published about enterprise AI agents this year, and Meta published them by accident. Agents were spectacularly productive at generating artifacts — code, diffs, commits — and roughly six times less productive at generating outcomes users could see. The 40 percent incident spike and the 70 percent jump in firefighting time mean a meaningful share of that output was not neutral but negative: work that created more work.

That gap matters more because of who produced it. Meta is close to the best possible case for agentic transformation. It owns the models, the infrastructure, the training data, and the authority to mandate adoption across every team. It is spending at least $130 billion this year on AI chips and infrastructure, with capital expenditure guidance running as high as $145 billion. If a company with those advantages could not make the org chart follow the technology, the median enterprise buying agent seats from a vendor has considerably less reason for confidence.

The deeper error was sequencing. Meta designed the headcount reduction first and treated agent capability as an input that would arrive on schedule. Restructuring decisions moved on a calendar; capability moved on its own timeline. The two never met.

## What to Watch

Zuckerberg's reassurance was narrower than it sounded: he said he did not expect further company-wide layoffs "this year," which leaves smaller performance-based cuts open now and 2027 wide open. Watch the three-to-six month window he cited in July for agent improvement: that checkpoint lands between October and January, and Meta's Q3 and Q4 earnings calls are the first venues where investors can press on what $130 billion has bought. Watch whether the mouse-tracking program, currently paused, quietly restarts. Watch the 26-employee lawsuit, which could force disclosure of how Meta's internal AI actually ranked people. And watch the pods, which were never dismantled: 11 units are still running the structure that Project OT was supposed to scale. Meta abandoned the layoffs, not the theory.

Roland Busch did not measure Europe's regulatory lag in months. He measured it in model generations.

In an interview with Welt am Sonntag published over the weekend of August 29-30, 2026, the Siemens chief executive called on the European Union to sharply raise the speed at which it approves AI-based technologies. His argument rests on a mismatch of clocks: passing a law like the AI Act or the Data Act takes roughly two years, and in that same window, he said, AI models develop six or eight times over. By the time the rules bite, the technology they describe has moved somewhere else.

"We must not slow the development pace of AI technology through regulation," Busch told the paper, according to German-language reports of the interview. His summary of the problem ran to four words in the original: "Wir hinken stets hinterher" — we are always lagging behind.

Busch was careful about what he was not saying. He did not call for scrapping the AI Act or for a moratorium on digital rules. His complaint was about tempo, not existence. European firms, he argued, are handicapped against Chinese and American competitors that adopt new technology extremely quickly, and bureaucracy is the mechanism. In the same interview he opposed blanket EU tariffs on China and called the current AI infrastructure boom sustainable, funded by cash-rich firms out of operating cash flow.

## The rules he is describing have already moved

Busch's intervention lands two months after Brussels did much of what he is asking for. On June 29, 2026, the Council gave final approval to the Digital Omnibus on AI, part of the Omnibus VII simplification package, after a Parliament vote earlier that month and a provisional deal on May 7. It entered into force on July 27.

The substance is mostly about timing. High-risk AI obligations — risk management, conformity assessment, data governance, human oversight, registration — were due from August 2, 2026. They now apply from December 2, 2027 for stand-alone high-risk systems, and from August 2, 2028 for high-risk AI embedded in regulated products. The deadline for member states to stand up regulatory sandboxes slipped to August 2, 2027. Products covered by the Machinery Regulation — squarely Siemens territory — were exempted from the AI Act's direct applicability, with safety requirements for high-risk AI to be added through machinery law instead. The package tightened elsewhere: the grace period for labelling artificially generated content was cut from six months to three, with a December 2, 2026 deadline.

The enforcement stakes are not theoretical. Prohibited practices carry fines of up to 35 million € or 7 percent of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. Commission enforcement powers over general-purpose AI model providers became applicable on August 2, 2026, carrying penalties of up to 15 million € or 3 percent of global turnover.

Siemens argues from strength, not distress. It reported record third-quarter fiscal 2026 orders of 27.9 billion €, up 14 percent year over year, with revenue up 8 percent and a 17.3 percent industrial margin. Its digital business grew 19 percent in the first half of the fiscal year against an internal ambition of 15 percent. Busch has staked strategy on making AI the operating system of industry, which turns the pace of European approvals into a direct input to the product roadmap.

## Why it matters

The case for Busch is hard to dismiss. Industrial AI does not ship as a research paper; it ships inside machines carrying certification burdens. A two-year legislative cycle bolted onto a six-month model cycle produces rules written against capabilities that no longer exist. If a manufacturer must run a conformity assessment against a specification drafted for a prior model generation, the cost is not only money — it is the option to ship at all. Marilena Raouna, the Cypriot deputy minister who presented the Council decision, made the mirror-image case: "By providing greater legal certainty and ensuring a more harmonised implementation of AI rules across the EU, we are creating the conditions for innovation and growth to thrive in the single market."

The case against is equally serious, and it disputes that speed is the real variable. A coalition of civil society groups — EDRi, Access Now, ECNL and Amnesty International, among 133 organisations and unions opposing the wider omnibus — called the June deal deregulation in a procedural costume. Their objection is structural: "If newly adopted digital rights laws can be reopened before they apply, powerful actors can treat implementation as a second chance to weaken rules they dislike." On this reading, the AI Act's failure was never that it moved too slowly. It was that the Commission never resourced its implementation — standards late, guidance on high-risk classification missed — then reopened the law once the deadline became inconvenient.

The legal-certainty argument cuts both ways. Dutch MEP Kim van Sparrentak framed the cost in terms of firms that complied on schedule: "Big Tech is probably popping champagne. While European companies that care about safety and did their homework now face regulatory chaos." Firms that built compliance programmes against an August 2026 deadline learned in mid-2026 that the deadline was December 2027. That is unpredictability too, and it penalises the cautious.

There is a version of Busch's argument both camps could sign: the bottleneck may be less the AI Act itself than the absence of fast approval machinery underneath it — sandboxes, harmonised standards, notified bodies with capacity. The omnibus pushed the sandbox deadline back rather than forward.

## What to watch

Three markers. First, the December 2, 2026 transparency deadline for labelling AI-generated content, the first omnibus provision to bite and an early test of whether shortened grace periods work. Second, the secondary legislation under the Machinery Regulation, which decides what safety requirements actually attach to AI inside industrial equipment — the file bearing most directly on Siemens. Third, whether the Commission's Digital Fitness Check triggers another round of reopening. Civil society warned the omnibus set a precedent. Busch's intervention, arriving eight weeks after the Council vote and asking for more, suggests industry read it the same way.

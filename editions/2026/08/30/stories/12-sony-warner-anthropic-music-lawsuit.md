The music industry has spent nearly three years arguing that training an AI model on copyrighted songs is itself infringement. The 48-page complaint Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell Music filed against Anthropic on Friday spends little energy on that argument. Its opening salvo is aimed at something narrower, and for Anthropic more dangerous: where the files came from.

The suit was docketed as Case No. 5:26-cv-09217 in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California on **August 28, 2026**. It names Anthropic PBC alongside two individuals — co-founder and chief executive **Dario Amodei** and co-founder **Benjamin Mann** — as defendants. More than thirty affiliated plaintiff entities stand behind the two lead publishers, among them EMI April Music, Jobete Music and Warner-Tamerlane Publishing.

**"Plaintiff Music Publishers, a group of the world's leading music publishers, bring this action to hold accountable the culprits behind one of the largest and most blatant ongoing thefts of intellectual property in history,"** the complaint opens. The defendants, it alleges, **"have conducted a brazen campaign of illegally torrenting, scraping, and downloading copyrighted works on a massive scale in order to develop, operate, and reap enormous profits from Anthropic's 'Claude' series of artificial intelligence models."**

Anthropic rejects the characterization. **"We disagree with the publishers' claims and we intend to defend ourselves robustly in court,"** a company spokesperson said in a statement to TechCrunch. None of the allegations has been tested; no judge has made findings in this case, and Anthropic has not yet filed an answer.

## What the complaint alleges

The pleading brings four counts: direct infringement by torrenting against all three defendants; contributory infringement by torrenting against Amodei and Mann personally; and, against Anthropic alone, direct infringement and removal of copyright management information. The publishers demand a jury trial, statutory damages of up to **$150,000** per willfully infringed work, up to **$25,000** per alleged act of CMI removal, destruction of all infringing copies, and an accounting of Claude's training data.

The arithmetic matters. Exhibit B identifies **"tens of thousands"** of the publishers' compositions allegedly copied as training input — a figure that, multiplied by the statutory ceiling, puts theoretical exposure in the multi-billion-dollar range. Songs named in the filing include "Ain't No Mountain High Enough," "Eye of the Tiger," "September," "Hallelujah" and Taylor Swift's "Paper Rings."

Much of the evidentiary spine is borrowed, drawn from findings and unsealed exhibits in *Bartz v. Anthropic*, the authors' class action in the same district, which called the company's conduct "straightforward piracy but at massive scale" and found it had torrented at least five million pirated books from Library Genesis in June 2021 and some two million more from Pirate Library Mirror in July 2022. The publishers quote Mann's internal description of LibGen as "sketchy AF" and a 2024 planning document on Anthropic's destructive book-scanning program: "We don't want it to be known that we are working on this."

Beyond torrenting, the publishers allege Anthropic scraped lyrics from licensed display sites including MusixMatch and LyricFind, and ingested the Common Crawl, The Pile and Books3 datasets. They further allege Claude reproduces their lyrics in outputs, and that guardrails added after earlier litigation are "easily circumventable by simply 're-prompting'" the model. Anthropic's **$1.5 billion** *Bartz* settlement, approved in July 2026, is dismissed as "just the cost of doing business" for a company the publishers say has "parlayed such mass infringement into a staggering $2-trillion-dollar valuation."

## Why it matters

This is copyright suit number thirteen against Anthropic, and the fourth from music publishers this year. But it is not a repeat of Concord Music Group's October 2023 case, which was an output case: it argued Claude regurgitated lyrics on request. Nor is it *Bartz*, which was about books. This complaint fuses the two and moves the center of gravity from *what the model does* to *how the corpus was assembled*.

That shift tracks the one genuinely settled point of law here. In *Bartz*, Judge William Alsup held that training an LLM on lawfully obtained books was "exceedingly transformative" fair use — a real win for AI developers. What he declined to bless was acquisition by piracy: building a permanent library from LibGen was its own non-transformative use, and no downstream transformation cured it. Anthropic paid $1.5 billion for that distinction.

Plaintiffs' counsel understood the implication immediately. Oppenheim + Zebrak — lead counsel in the Concord cases and in *Bartz* — is running this one alongside Pryor Cashman. If fair use protects training but not sourcing, the question is no longer whether a model learned from your catalog. It is whether the lab can produce a receipt.

That reframing changes training economics in a specific way. Fair use is litigated once, at the model level, and its outcome scales across every work. Acquisition liability is litigated at the file level and scales *with* the corpus: the more data a lab hoovered up, the larger its exposure, regardless of how transformative the result. It turns data provenance from a compliance chore into a balance-sheet item. The complaint notes, pointedly, that the publishers "have entered licenses permitting the authorized use of their musical compositions in connection with AI." The subtext is that a market price exists and Anthropic declined to pay it. Naming a sitting CEO personally is the other escalation, resting partly on Mann's sworn admission in a related docket that "Dr. Amodei approved" the LibGen torrenting.

## What to watch

First, assignment. The two Concord cases and BMG's sit before Judge Eumi K. Lee, and legal trackers expect this one to be related in as well. Second, whether Amodei and Mann survive a motion to dismiss as individual defendants — that ruling sets the template for the suits that follow. Third, timing: Anthropic is reportedly steering toward an October IPO, and contingent liability of this size is what underwriters read closely. Fourth, whether the publishers ever get the training-data accounting they have demanded. That, more than any damages figure, is what the industry actually wants.

Anthropic Names Former California Justice Tino Cuéllar as Its First Chief Global Affairs Officer

Anthropic has appointed Mariano-Florentino "Tino" Cuéllar, a former justice of the Supreme Court of California and until recently the president of the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, as its first Chief Global Affairs Officer. The move, announced in early August 2026, installs a seasoned jurist and national-security lawyer at the top of a newly created executive post built to steer policy, international engagement, and government relationships across the world.

The timing is not subtle. Cuéllar steps into what has become one of the most adversarial corporate-government relationships in American technology. Anthropic is fighting a Pentagon blacklisting in federal court and contending with a temporary Trump administration export designation on its frontier models. Hiring a globe-spanning policy heavyweight to run point on those battles signals that Anthropic now treats government affairs as a core function of the company, not a side office.

Who Cuéllar Is

Cuéllar brings an unusually deep public-service resume to a private AI lab. He served as an associate justice of the California Supreme Court from 2015 until 2021, when he left the bench to lead the Carnegie Endowment, a century-old foreign-policy research institution with scholars in roughly 20 countries. Before that, he was a professor at Stanford, where he directed the Freeman Spogli Institute for International Studies, co-directed the Center for International Security and Cooperation, and led the Stanford Cyber Initiative.

His government experience spans three presidential administrations, including a stint as a special assistant to the president in the Obama White House. He is also a member of the Harvard Corporation, the university's senior governing board. Notably, Cuéllar had already been inside Anthropic's orbit: he served as a trustee of the company's Long-Term Benefit Trust starting in January 2026, and stepped down from that role to take the executive job.

That combination — constitutional law, national security, cyber policy, and international institutions — is precisely the profile Anthropic needs as its regulatory and geopolitical exposure widens.

What the Role Signals

The creation of a Chief Global Affairs Officer position, and the seniority of the person filling it, tells you where Anthropic thinks its risks now live. The company faces a Pentagon "supply chain risk" designation and an export restriction on its frontier Mythos 5 and Fable 5 systems, measures that followed Anthropic's objections to its technology being used for domestic mass surveillance and fully autonomous weapons. President Trump publicly warned that the company had "better get their act together" during a phase-out period or he would "use the Full Power of the Presidency to make them comply, with major civil and criminal consequences to follow."

Against that backdrop, elevating a Democratic-connected national-security lawyer to serve as Anthropic's ambassador to a Republican White House is a deliberate bet. It suggests the company values institutional credibility and legal firepower over short-term political optics. Cuéllar's mandate reaches well beyond Washington, too: international engagement is baked into the title, reflecting Anthropic's push into regulated markets and global AI governance forums where a former Carnegie president carries real convening power.

Why It Matters

The appointment is the clearest sign yet that frontier AI labs are building serious government-affairs muscle as regulatory fights intensify. For years, policy work at AI companies was a modest function bolted onto communications teams. That era is ending. Anthropic has moved from advocating narrow transparency rules to backing binding, enforceable regulation of the largest models — a shift crystallized in CEO Dario Amodei's June 2026 "Policy on the AI Exponential" essay, which called for FAA-style oversight of frontier systems, mandatory testing, and support for workers displaced by automation.

Anthropic has also been unusually active at the state level, backing tougher AI bills and favoring the preservation of state laws unless Congress passes something at least as strong. That state-by-state strategy puts the company at odds with a federal government that has, at times, pushed for preemption and lighter-touch rules. Managing that state-versus-federal tension — while simultaneously litigating against the Pentagon and courting overseas regulators — requires exactly the kind of senior legal and diplomatic operator Anthropic just hired.

For the broader industry, Cuéllar's move is a template. As AI companies become national-security actors and geopolitical chess pieces, they are recruiting from the top ranks of the judiciary, foreign-policy institutions, and past administrations. The competition among labs is no longer only about model benchmarks; it is increasingly about who can navigate export controls, defense procurement, and a fragmented global rulebook.

What to Watch

The first test is whether Cuéllar can de-escalate the Pentagon standoff without Anthropic abandoning its stated red lines on surveillance and autonomous weapons. Watch how the export designation on its frontier models is resolved, and whether the company secures a durable settlement or an appeals-court win.

Second, track Anthropic's posture in the state-versus-federal regulatory debate. If Congress advances a preemption push, Cuéllar's team will shape whether Anthropic fights it or negotiates a federal floor it can live with.

Finally, watch the international front. A former Carnegie president is likely to lean into global governance venues, and Anthropic's ambitions in regulated overseas markets will hinge on how effectively he translates safety rhetoric into diplomatic credibility. If rivals respond by hiring their own marquee policy chiefs, the government-affairs arms race in AI will be fully underway.

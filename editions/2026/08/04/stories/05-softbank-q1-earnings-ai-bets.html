SoftBank's Earnings Test Investor Appetite for Its AI Bets Beyond OpenAI

When SoftBank Group reports fiscal first-quarter results in Tokyo on Thursday, the numbers on the page will matter less than the questions in the room. After a year in which Masayoshi Son transformed his conglomerate into the most leveraged bet on artificial intelligence in the world, investors want to know one thing: whether the empire he is building around a roughly $65 billion stake in OpenAI can stand on more than a single chatbot maker's promise.

The stakes are steep. SoftBank shares have fallen nearly 50% since early June, after briefly surpassing Toyota as Japan's most valuable company, and the cost to insure its debt against default has soared. Analysts polled by LSEG expect net profit of about 148.4 billion yen ($941 million) for the April-June quarter. But the report card investors care about is the one covering robotics, data centers and energy — the "beyond OpenAI" portfolio Son is betting can justify the borrowing.

A $65 Billion Bet, Financed on Credit

The centerpiece, and the pressure point, is OpenAI. On February 27, SoftBank agreed to follow-on investments of $30 billion through its Vision Fund 2, lifting its cumulative commitment to roughly $64.6 billion and its ownership to about 13%. To fund the tranche, the group executed a $40 billion unsecured bridge facility in late March, underwritten by JPMorgan Chase, Goldman Sachs, Mizuho, Sumitomo Mitsui and MUFG, with repayment due around March 2027.

That timeline was originally read by lenders as consistent with an OpenAI public listing within roughly a year. Then, in late June, reports that OpenAI could delay its IPO to 2027 — with Sam Altman said to be refusing any valuation below $1 trillion — wiped some $38 billion from SoftBank's market capitalization in a single session. Suddenly a maturing bridge loan and a still-private exit sit inside the same narrow window.

The financing squeeze runs deeper. SoftBank faces around $30 billion of obligations due in the second half of 2026 and has leaned on loans secured against its holdings, including a $20 billion margin loan against its stake in chip designer Arm. An attempt to borrow at least $6 billion more against its OpenAI equity has stalled, as lenders grow wary of extending credit backed by private companies.

The Case for Diversification

Son's answer to the skeptics is scale — and breadth. Beyond OpenAI, he chairs the $500 billion Stargate data-center venture with Oracle, OpenAI and Abu Dhabi's MGX, and has floated a roughly $1 trillion AI-and-robotics complex in Arizona dubbed Project Crystal Land. In May, SoftBank unveiled plans for up to 75 billion euros (about $87 billion) of AI data centers in France, targeting 3.1 gigawatts of capacity by 2031.

> "Stargate is harnessing SoftBank's innovative data center design and energy expertise to deliver the scalable compute that powers AI's future. Together with OpenAI, Arm, and our Stargate partners, we are paving the way for a new era where AI advances humanity."

That framing — compute, energy and eventually robots as a single stack — is what Thursday's release is meant to flesh out. SoftBank has moved to acquire Swiss robotics startup Gravis Robotics AG, while its telecom arm announced a joint venture with South Korea's Cosmos Lab and AI provider DeltaX to manufacture large-format battery cells and energy storage systems in Japan. Bernstein recently began coverage with an Outperform rating, citing SoftBank's exposure across the full AI stack.

The Risk Analysts Keep Circling

Not everyone is convinced the arithmetic works. Bridgewater partner Greg Jensen has reportedly told clients that OpenAI's implied valuation is "priced for a monopoly outcome that does not yet exist," and EMARKETER analyst Nate Elliott has called the IPO a proposition arriving at "a precarious moment." The core worry is timing: SoftBank is spending unprecedented sums today against revenue that may not materialize for years, in a high-rate environment where every quarter of delayed liquidity raises carrying costs.

That is the tension Son must manage. His diversification story is real — France, Stargate, Gravis, battery cells — but so is the concentration risk, with a single delayed listing capable of erasing tens of billions in market value overnight. The debt-funded structure amplifies both the upside and the downside.

What to Watch

Three signals will define the read on Thursday: any update on refinancing the $40 billion bridge and the stalled OpenAI-backed margin loan; concrete revenue or capacity milestones from the robotics, data-center and energy units; and Son's own tone on the OpenAI IPO timeline. If he can show the "beyond OpenAI" portfolio generating momentum, the leverage looks like ambition. If he cannot, it starts to look like exposure.

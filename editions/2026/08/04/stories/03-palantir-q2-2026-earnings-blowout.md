# Palantir's Q2 Blowout: Revenue Jumps 93% as AI Demand Fuels Its Largest-Ever Guidance Raise

Palantir Technologies did not just beat Wall Street in the second quarter. It rewrote the pace at which a company its size is supposed to grow. On Monday, August 3, after the U.S. close, the data-analytics and AI firm reported revenue of $1.94 billion, a 93% surge from a year earlier, and then paired that number with the biggest full-year guidance raise in its history. By the time markets closed Tuesday, Palantir's stock had rocketed roughly 20-25% and helped drag broad indices to fresh records.

CEO Alex Karp, never one to undersell, called it an "otherworldly" quarter. For once, the adjective did not feel like a stretch.

## A quarter that broke its own trend

The headline numbers were staggering for a company already valued in the hundreds of billions. Total revenue of $1.94 billion topped consensus and accelerated from prior quarters rather than cooling, the opposite of what gravity usually does to large software businesses. Adjusted earnings came in at $0.41 per share against roughly $0.35 expected, a beat of about 17%. Palantir also reported net income of more than $1 billion for the first time in its history.

The engine was the U.S. commercial segment, where revenue soared 149% year over year to $764 million. That is not a mature enterprise-software growth rate; it is a hypergrowth startup rate applied to a business generating billions. The U.S. government segment, long Palantir's foundation, grew 90% to $809 million, showing that the commercial explosion has not come at the expense of its public-sector franchise. Both halves of the company are now compounding at rates rarely seen together.

Deal volume told the same story. Palantir closed a record 220 contracts worth $1 million or more during the quarter, including 98 deals above $5 million and 70 above $10 million. Its Rule of 40 score, the combined measure of revenue growth and profitability that software investors treat as a gold standard, hit 155%, a figure almost unheard of at Palantir's scale.

## The guidance raise that stole the show

If the quarter was strong, the outlook was what turned a good report into a market event. Management lifted full-year 2026 revenue guidance to a range of $8.15 billion to $8.16 billion, implying roughly 82% growth for the year and marking what the company described as its largest-ever guidance raise, an 11-point jump over the prior quarter's guide. U.S. commercial revenue guidance was pushed above $3.42 billion, or at least 134% growth.

Karp framed the raised bar not as a ceiling but as a floor. "I am driving the business to grow at a rate equal or above to what we have in U.S. commercial," he told investors on the earnings call. In a separate interview with CNBC, he suggested the momentum was durable rather than a one-quarter spike, saying it "looks like this is going to go on for at least another 18 months."

He tied the surge explicitly to the geopolitics of artificial intelligence. "The sovereign AI revolution makes us very optimistic about the future," Karp said, leaning into a thesis the company has pressed for more than a year: that governments and large enterprises want AI systems they control, operate on their own data, and can trust with mission-critical decisions.

## AIP is converting the hype into contracts

The number that best explains the run is that 149% U.S. commercial figure, because it is the clearest signal yet that Palantir's Artificial Intelligence Platform, or AIP, has moved beyond early adopters and into mainstream enterprise budgets. AIP's go-to-market centers on intensive "boot camp" engagements, short, hands-on sessions in which Palantir engineers stand up working AI applications on a customer's own data, then convert the trial into a paid contract. What was a novel sales motion two years ago now appears to be a repeatable growth machine.

Just as telling, Palantir says AIP is winning head-to-head technical evaluations. The company cited a major Silicon Valley technology firm that chose Palantir after a bake-off and signed a contract worth roughly $10 million in annual value. That detail matters because it positions Palantir not merely as a beneficiary of the AI boom but as a vendor that enterprises pick over do-it-yourself approaches and frontier-lab tooling when the goal is deployed, operational AI rather than experiments.

For the broader enterprise-AI debate, the quarter is a data point that cuts against the "all hype, no revenue" skepticism that has dogged the sector. Palantir is showing that companies will pay, and pay quickly at scale, for software that turns large language models into decisions on top of proprietary data. Karp has increasingly used that position to needle rivals, sharpening his criticism of frontier labs even as their models feed demand for the very deployment layer Palantir sells.

## The bull case, and the catch

The obvious counterweight is valuation. Palantir was already one of the most richly priced names in software before the report, and a 20%-plus one-day pop only stretches the multiple further. Growth this explosive invites tough comparisons: the same acceleration that thrills investors today sets a brutally high bar for the quarters that must lap it. Karp's "at least another 18 months" comment is a confidence signal, but it is also a clock.

What to watch from here: whether U.S. commercial growth can stay anywhere near triple digits as the base compounds; whether the record deal count translates into durable, expanding accounts rather than one-time boot-camp wins; and whether government revenue, up 90% this quarter, holds through shifting federal budget cycles. For now, Palantir has done the rarest thing in enterprise software, accelerating at scale, and it has dared Wall Street to bet the streak ends soon.

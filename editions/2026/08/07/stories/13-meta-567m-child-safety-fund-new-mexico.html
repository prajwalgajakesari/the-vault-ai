New Mexico Court Orders Meta to Fund $567 Million for Teen Mental Health

A New Mexico judge has ordered Meta Platforms to bankroll a $567 million fund for youth mental health and to submit to five years of court-supervised changes to Facebook and Instagram, in what state officials are calling the most significant ruling ever issued against a social media company over harm to children.

The final judgment, entered by First Judicial District Court Judge Bryan Biedscheid in Santa Fe and announced on August 7, 2026, caps a two-phase trial brought by New Mexico Attorney General Raúl Torrez. Combined with the $375 million civil penalty a jury imposed against Meta earlier this year, the ruling brings the company's total financial exposure in the state to $942 million.

What the Court Ordered

The case unfolded in two stages. In Phase 1, a jury found in March 2026 that Meta committed roughly 75,000 violations of New Mexico's Unfair Practices Act and imposed the maximum civil penalty allowed under law, $375 million. In Phase 2, Judge Biedscheid went further, ruling that Meta's platforms constitute a public nuisance in New Mexico and rejecting the company's argument that Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act shielded it from liability for products it knowingly designed.

According to the state, the bulk of the new $567 million award, about $420 million, will fund treatment services for young people, with the remainder directed to awareness and prevention, screening services and related costs over the next five years.

Beyond the money, the court imposed a wide slate of structural reforms that will remain in effect for five years. They include more rigorous age verification for New Mexico users, strengthened protections for teen accounts, tougher safeguards against sextortion and child sexual exploitation, elimination of overnight push notifications for users under 18, mandatory time-use limits for minors, default privacy protections such as hiding public "like" counts, and semiannual public compliance reports filed with the court.

"Meta built products it knew would fuel addiction, deepen a youth mental health crisis, and expose children to sexual exploitation, then lied to parents and policymakers about the danger," Torrez said in a statement announcing the judgment. "Today, it pays for that choice."

A First on Platform Liability

State officials framed the decision as a legal milestone. It is, by the attorney general's account, the first time a court has held that a social media company can be sued, tried, and held financially and structurally accountable for building products that endanger children, rather than for hosting third-party content, the distinction that has long allowed platforms to invoke Section 230 immunity.

"For the first time, a court has ruled that a social media giant can be held liable for building products that endanger children, and has ordered the structural changes needed to fix it," Torrez said. "New Mexico led the way in the courtroom. It is a blueprint other states, and other countries confronting this same crisis, can now follow."

Notably, the court stopped short of mandating the hardest age-verification requirement the trial record suggested was needed, describing that kind of sweeping policy choice as belonging to the legislative and executive branches rather than the courts. Torrez said he intends to close that gap with a comprehensive social media safety bill in the New Mexico Legislature that would write age verification into law.

Meta says it will appeal. A company spokesperson, Aaron Simpson, said Meta works hard to keep people safe on its platforms and has been transparent about the challenges of identifying and removing bad actors and harmful content, adding that the company remains confident in its record of protecting teens online and will continue to defend itself against claims it says misrepresent the facts. Torrez has said the appeal could take "months, if not years" to resolve.

For context on scale, the $942 million total is a fraction of Meta's profit, which ran to roughly $60 billion in 2025.

Why It Matters

The ruling strikes at the legal foundation that has protected social platforms for nearly three decades. By treating Facebook and Instagram as products, and their design choices as conduct that created a public nuisance, the court sidestepped Section 230's content-focused immunity. If the theory survives appeal, it offers other attorneys general and private plaintiffs a template for holding platforms accountable for the architecture of their apps, not merely the posts users share.

That shift toward product-design accountability matters because the reforms attached to this judgment target mechanics, not speech: overnight notifications, infinite engagement loops, default privacy settings, contact between adults and minors, and age assurance. These are the levers researchers and regulators have increasingly identified as central to how minors experience social media, and they are precisely the features that generate engagement and revenue.

The case also lands amid a wave of youth-safety regulation. Red and blue states alike have passed age-verification laws, some of which face their own constitutional challenges, and members of both parties in Congress have floated federal legislation. New Mexico's decision adds judicial weight to a policy debate that has so far been waged mostly in legislatures, and it sharpens the tension between child-protection mandates and questions about privacy, free expression, and how platforms verify age without collecting more data on everyone.

What to Watch

The most immediate question is the appeal. Meta's challenge to the public-nuisance finding and the rejection of its Section 230 defense will test whether this novel liability theory holds up in higher courts, and the outcome will shape how far other states can follow New Mexico's lead. Watch, too, for the promised legislation in Santa Fe, and whether the "blueprint" language draws copycat suits from other attorneys general who have pending or contemplated actions against Meta and its peers. For families and clinicians, the practical measure will be simpler: whether the $567 million fund and the five-year decree translate into services delivered and design changes that actually reach New Mexico's teens.

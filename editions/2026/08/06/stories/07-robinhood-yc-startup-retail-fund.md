Robinhood wants to sell you a slice of the next OpenAI before it ever files for an IPO. On August 5, the brokerage that turned commission-free trading into a cultural phenomenon unveiled **Robinhood Ventures Fund II (RVII)**, a publicly traded fund built to give ordinary investors exposure to startups tied to **Y Combinator** — the storied Silicon Valley accelerator that seeded Airbnb, Stripe, Coinbase, and DoorDash. Shares are expected to begin trading on the New York Stock Exchange on **August 13 at $25 apiece**, with Robinhood aiming to raise as much as **$200 million**.

The pitch is deceptively simple: for the price of a single share, a retail investor can buy into a basket of roughly **80 private companies** that would otherwise be walled off behind venture-capital funds and accreditation requirements. The pitch's fine print is where things get complicated.

## What Robinhood is actually selling

RVII is structured as a closed-end fund — specifically, a business development company — not a direct stake in any startup. Investors buy shares whose value tracks an underlying portfolio of early- and growth-stage private companies. The fund targets businesses that are current or former Y Combinator participants, or that were founded by someone who passed through the program. Robinhood says the initial 80-company roster will expand over time as the fund deploys capital.

Crucially, RVII shares trade on the open market, but the companies inside the fund do not. That mismatch is the entire product: a **liquid wrapper around fundamentally illiquid assets**. Shareholders cannot redeem their shares back to the fund; to cash out, they must find another buyer on the NYSE at whatever price the market sets that day.

The fee structure is the classic hedge-fund model, now aimed at Main Street. RVII charges a **2% annual management fee plus a 20% incentive fee** on realized capital gains — the "two and twenty" arrangement that venture and hedge funds have levied on institutional clients for decades. Notably, that is a step up from Robinhood's first venture fund, which CEO Vlad Tenev had marketed as having **"no carry — so just a competitive management fee."**

## Building on a fund that already drew 150,000 buyers

RVII is not Robinhood's first move here. Robinhood Ventures Fund I, which listed earlier this year, drew **more than 150,000 retail investors**, according to Tenev, and holds stakes in what the company calls "frontier companies" — a portfolio reported to include **OpenAI, Stripe, Databricks, Ramp, Oura, and Airwallex.**

Tenev has framed the entire venture line as a matter of fairness, arguing that the biggest gains in the modern economy are being captured before companies ever go public.

> "Historically, retail investors have been locked out of private companies in their earliest stages, which can be among the biggest wealth creation opportunities available."

He has gone further in interviews, describing his ambition to reshape how startups raise money altogether. His aspiration, he has said, is that when a company raises a seed or Series A round, **"retail should be a big chunk of that round, much like it now is in the public markets,"** letting everyday investors in "at the ground floor."

The roadshow, which kicked off August 3, featured Tenev alongside CFO Shiv Verma, Robinhood Ventures head and RVII President Sarah Pinto, and portfolio manager Rich Aberman.

## Why this moment, and why AI

The timing is not an accident. Tech's most valuable companies are staying private far longer than they used to. **SpaceX, Stripe, and a wave of AI startups have reached multibillion-dollar — in some cases hundreds-of-billions — valuations before ever ringing the opening bell.** The center of gravity for wealth creation has drifted from public markets, where anyone can buy in, to private rounds open only to VCs and the wealthy.

The AI boom sharpens the appeal. Companies like OpenAI, Anthropic, and Databricks have become household names while remaining stubbornly private, and retail investors have watched valuations balloon from the sidelines. Robinhood is betting that fear of missing out on the AI wave — riding what one report called an "AI rally" — will drive demand for a product that promises a ticket to the table. Y Combinator, whose alumni disproportionately populate the current AI startup crop, is a natural on-ramp.

## The catch investors should weigh

The democratization story comes with heavy caveats that Robinhood's own risk disclosures acknowledge. Closed-end funds routinely trade at **discounts to net asset value — sometimes 10% to 20%** — meaning a shareholder might sell for well below the stated value of the underlying holdings. The fund's NAV, moreover, is calculated by Robinhood Ventures itself, not by an open market, because the portfolio companies are private and have no continuously quoted price. Valuations are, in a word, estimates.

Then there is the base-rate problem of venture investing: **most venture-backed startups fail or return modestly**, with a handful of outliers carrying entire portfolios. A retail buyer is paying two-and-twenty for a diversified bet that the winners inside RVII outweigh the many that won't make it — and the fee drag compounds against returns whether or not that bet pays off.

This is familiar territory for Robinhood, which has spent years trying to erase the line between institutional and retail access — through tokenized stocks, private-company tokens, and now a publicly traded venture vehicle. Critics see a company repeatedly packaging complexity and illiquidity into products that feel as frictionless as buying a meme stock.

## What to watch

The first real test comes August 13, when RVII prices and begins trading. Watch whether shares open at a premium or slide to the discount that plagues most closed-end funds — an early referendum on whether retail enthusiasm can hold up a structure that gives investors no way to redeem. Watch, too, for regulatory attention: handing unaccredited investors leveraged exposure to opaque private valuations is precisely the kind of financial engineering the SEC has scrutinized before. And watch the portfolio itself. If even one of RVII's 80 startups becomes the next breakout name, Robinhood will have a powerful proof point. If the fund limps to a discount and the winners stay scarce, it will become a cautionary tale about what "democratizing" private markets really costs.

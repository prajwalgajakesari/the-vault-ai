On a hillside outside Visakhapatnam, earthmovers have stripped the red ground into terraced steps for what Google calls its largest-ever investment in India. Below them, in a coastal city of 2.5 million people that already rations its water, activists are marching with banners that read "We cannot drink DATA" and painting handcuffs on the Google logo. The $15 billion AI and data-center hub, less than a year into construction, has become an early stress test for how far even the world's biggest technology companies can push against local limits on water, land and wildlife.

## A marquee project runs into local limits

Google's Visakhapatnam campus is planned as a gigawatt-scale computing hub, one of the largest foreign investments ever announced in India's technology sector. The company is building it with the group controlled by Indian billionaire Gautam Adani, whose firms will construct the facility. Boosters, including the Andhra Pradesh government of a Modi ally who has called the project "historic and transformational," say it could create up to 188,000 jobs.

The opposition centers on a resource the region cannot spare. By the state government's own figures, Visakhapatnam receives about **410 million liters of water a day** from its reservoirs and rivers against a requirement of **480 million** — a 70-million-liter daily gap that already forces routine rationing. Into that shortfall, critics say, arrives an enormous new industrial customer whose servers must be cooled around the clock.

"Development should not be at the cost of livelihood of the people," said Raja Rama Mohan Roy, founder of the non-profit Green Visakha, at a public gathering on Sunday where he presented statistics on the city's stressed water supply. His group says it is especially alarmed by the government's promise of "guaranteed" water supplies to the project for 20 years while residents already face shortages. "Who will be the sufferers? The people," Roy said.

## Courts, sanctuaries and a wall of litigation

The dispute has moved from the streets into the courts. On Monday, the Andhra Pradesh High Court asked the state to defend itself against allegations from the activist group Jal Biradari — "Water Community" — which argues the project will strain availability by drawing on a nearby reservoir. That public interest litigation is scheduled for its next hearing on **August 24**.

The same petition raises a second front: the site sits just **860 meters** from the Kambalakonda Wildlife Sanctuary, a forest home to leopards and pangolins, and activists contend that heavy construction and noise will disturb it. The project faces three additional cases filed at India's environmental court by the Human Rights Forum, which demands a halt on the grounds that the state cleared the campus without properly assessing the impact of drawing on a rural drinking-water scheme, according to court documents.

Google and the state have answers to each charge. Google told Reuters the project will be developed in line with applicable laws and that it will deploy "advanced air cooling to protect vital local water resources," along with sound-dampening measures so that "we are a quiet, unobtrusive neighbor." Andhra Pradesh officials called the activists' concerns "incorrect and misleading" but said the government remains open to feedback. "Such protests are their democratic right," the state said in a statement. "In case any of these claims are legitimate, government is committed to engage and provide factual clarifications and suitable redressal." The state insists no water for rural or residential use will be diverted, that the nearby reservoir will not be tapped, and that the sanctuary buffer exceeds what the law requires. Adani did not respond to queries.

## When the constraint on AI is a neighborhood

The Visakhapatnam fight is a local story with a global shape. Rapid data-center buildouts are drawing pushback worldwide over the water they consume to cool servers and the electricity they pull from strained grids. In the United States alone, opponents mounted **142 protests across 42 states in July**, according to Reuters. What makes India's case sharper is context: the demands of a gigawatt hyperscale campus land on a developing city where water is already scarce and the trade-offs are immediate and visible.

For most of the AI boom, the binding constraint has been chips and, more recently, power. The Visakhapatnam standoff points to a third constraint that is harder to buy or manufacture away — social license. Land approvals, water allocations and environmental clearances are granted by state governments and defended in courts that answer to local publics, not to hyperscaler roadmaps. A 20-year water guarantee looks like a settled deal in a term sheet and an open political wound in a rationed city. Air cooling reduces water draw but raises power demand, and sanctuary buffers that satisfy statute may not satisfy a judge weighing a petition. Each mitigation Google offers is real, and each also concedes that the underlying tension between industrial-scale compute and finite local resources cannot simply be engineered out of existence.

That is why the case matters beyond Andhra Pradesh. If a project of this scale and political backing can be slowed by a coalition of grassroots groups, water activists and a wildlife sanctuary, the permitting and community-relations layer becomes a first-order variable in where the next generation of AI infrastructure gets built — and how fast.

## What to watch

The August 24 High Court hearing is the near-term marker; a stay or an order for fresh environmental review would ripple through the construction timeline. Watch, too, whether the National Green Tribunal cases over the rural drinking-water scheme force the state to publish detailed water-sourcing plans it has so far described only in general terms. And watch how Google and Adani respond to the protest movement's next phase — the door-to-door campaigns and beach demonstrations activists were planning as of last weekend. The company has pledged advanced cooling and quiet operations; the coming weeks will show whether those commitments are enough to convert a city's opposition into acceptance, or whether "We cannot drink DATA" becomes a template other communities borrow.

KEY TAKEAWAY

A bipartisan quartet of senators introduced the CHATBOT Act, which would force AI companies to build parent-controlled 'family accounts' giving parents consent, monitoring, and transcript access over how their children use AI chatbots.

Senators Introduce Bipartisan Bill Giving Parents Control Over Kids' AI Chatbots

A bipartisan quartet of senators wants to hand parents the keys to their children's AI chatbot accounts, betting that a rare left-right coalition can move faster than the technology reshaping how millions of American kids talk, learn, and confide.

Senators Ted Cruz (R-Texas), Brian Schatz (D-Hawaii), John Curtis (R-Utah), and Adam Schiff (D-Calif.) introduced the CHATBOT Act, the Children's Health, Advancement, Trust, Boundaries, and Oversight in Technology Act, which would require AI companies to build "family accounts" that let parents approve, monitor, and restrict how minors interact with conversational AI. Cataloged as S.4407, the measure lands in the Senate Commerce Committee, which Cruz chairs, giving it an unusually clear procedural runway in a Congress that has struggled to pass almost anything touching Big Tech.

The bill's central mechanism is the family account. Under the proposal, children under 13 would be required to operate through a parent-linked account, while accounts for teens would be optional but still require a parent's consent before a minor could sign up. Parents would gain the ability to read a full record of their child's conversations with a chatbot. And in a provision aimed at kids whose parents never opt in, the legislation would force platforms to default a teen's controls to their most protective setting. The measure would also curb "manipulative design features," bar targeted advertising to children, and direct federal regulators to study chatbot-related harms and best practices for parents.

"The rapid development of sophisticated chatbots has left many parents in the dark as powerful AI systems enter children's lives," Cruz said in a statement announcing the bill. "Congress has an opportunity to put parents back in control. With the right safeguards, AI systems can benefit a child's education without putting their well-being at risk. The CHATBOT Act ensures America leads in deploying AI safely and responsibly."

Schatz framed the stakes in blunter terms, pointing to a growing body of reporting on chatbots that have steered vulnerable teens toward self-harm or displaced their real-world relationships. "AI is an incredibly powerful tool -- it's everywhere, and it poses real risks for kids," Schatz said. "We've seen reports of AI chatbots encouraging kids to hurt themselves and for some, they're replacing real life relationships, isolating kids from their families and friends. Our bill will give parents better tools to keep their kids safe and hold AI companies accountable."

That concern is not abstract. The legislation arrives amid a series of wrenching lawsuits and news accounts alleging that AI companion products contributed to teen suicides and self-harm, cases that have galvanized parents and lawmakers across the political spectrum. Sponsors have been careful to frame the bill as giving families oversight rather than banning the technology outright.

Child-safety advocates welcomed the effort. Donna Rice Hughes, president and CEO of the nonprofit Enough Is Enough, said children are being harmed by AI chatbots and that Big Tech should be held accountable, arguing that parents deserve "more resources and default parental control safety measures."

Industry groups were less receptive. NetChoice, a trade association whose members include major technology platforms, signaled it would fight the measure. Vice president Amy Bos said the bill "runs into protected rights" under the First Amendment and described the CHATBOT Act as relying on "the same unlawful identity mandate framework NetChoice has successfully challenged in court across the country." The critique previews the central legal battleground: whether age-gating and identity-verification requirements can survive constitutional scrutiny after NetChoice's string of court victories against state social-media laws.

Why It Matters

The CHATBOT Act is one piece of a fast-moving 2026 wave of kids-AI legislation, and its fate will help determine whether Washington sets a national standard or leaves companies navigating a fifty-state patchwork. In the House, lawmakers passed the sprawling KIDS Act (H.R. 7757) by a bipartisan 267-117 margin in June, bundling chatbot safeguards with a stack of youth-privacy and platform-design measures now before the Senate. Senator Ed Markey (D-Mass.) has pushed his Youth AI Privacy Act, while the GUARD Act (S. 3062) from Senators Josh Hawley (R-Mo.) and Richard Blumenthal (D-Conn.) cleared committee with an even tougher approach: mandatory age verification and a bar on AI companions for minors altogether. Curtis and Schiff have separately floated the SAFE KIDS Act, requiring age estimation and independent audits.

The states are already ahead. California's SB 243, signed in October 2025 and effective January 1, 2026, became the first law in the nation to mandate companion-chatbot safeguards, including self-harm protocols, AI-disclosure reminders, and a private right of action. With California's legislature reconvening for its 2026 session, more state action is likely, raising the compliance stakes that make a single federal framework attractive to industry even as those same companies resist the specific mandates on the table.

The unresolved tension is constitutional. NetChoice's warning shot signals that identity and age-verification requirements, the load-bearing walls of nearly every one of these bills, face near-certain First Amendment challenges. Sponsors are betting that a parental-consent framing, rather than an outright content ban, gives the CHATBOT Act firmer legal footing.

What to Watch

Watch whether Cruz uses his Commerce gavel to schedule a markup, and whether the CHATBOT Act gets folded into the larger KIDS Act package as the Senate takes up the House-passed bill. Key questions include how "family accounts" would interact with age-verification mandates in competing bills, whether privacy advocates balk at giving parents full transcript access to teens' conversations, and how the industry's First Amendment litigation strategy shapes the final text. Also watch California and other statehouses: continued state activity increases pressure for a federal standard, but also hands opponents a preemption fight. The measure's bipartisan sponsorship is its greatest asset, but in a crowded field of overlapping proposals, the biggest risk may be that Congress does everything at once and passes nothing.

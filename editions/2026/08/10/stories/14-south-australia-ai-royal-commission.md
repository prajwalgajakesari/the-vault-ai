# South Australia Opens a Royal Commission Into AI as Governments Confront Its Impact on Work and Schools

South Australia is about to do something no government in the country has tried: put artificial intelligence on trial. On Monday, Premier Peter Malinauskas announced that his state will convene a royal commission into AI, the most formal instrument of public inquiry in the Australian legal system, to examine how the technology is reshaping work, schools, public services and the creative economy. It is the first inquiry of its kind in Australia, and one of the most consequential state-level examinations of AI anywhere in the world.

"We are at a really important fork in the road," Malinauskas told reporters in Adelaide. "We can choose to allow this industry to come to our country and evolve in a way that is unregulated and unchecked. Or we can choose to set the agenda … to ensure that we use AI in a way that puts people first."

## What the commission will do

Royal commissions are typically reserved for the gravest questions a government confronts: institutional child abuse, banking misconduct, deaths in custody. Directing that machinery at a general-purpose technology signals how seriously Adelaide is treating the disruption now arriving in every workplace and classroom.

The details announced so far sketch an ambitious remit. The commission's terms of reference will be unveiled within four to six weeks. Three royal commissioners will be recruited through an international search, chosen for a mix of public-policy and technology expertise. They are expected to begin work in October and deliver a final report to the government by 1 July 2027. The exercise is budgeted at roughly $3 million.

Rather than dwell on the frontier-model safety debates that have dominated federal and international discussions, the South Australian inquiry is pointed squarely at everyday institutions. Malinauskas said it would develop policy solutions across education, industry, public health and the arts, and would gather evidence from business, unions, technology developers, academics and creative industries. The framing is deliberate: this is less an inquiry into whether a model might go rogue than into what happens to a teacher, a call-centre worker, a graphic designer or a public hospital when AI becomes ubiquitous.

Education looms especially large in the premier's own comments. "I hate the idea of kids graduating from high school and they've learned how to use ChatGPT, but not how to read, write, and do arithmetic … or how to communicate with one another," Malinauskas said. He added that "in a regulated way, we want to teach kids how to use AI, but I don't think we've done as much work as we potentially can or should."

## A state positioning itself as a policy testbed

The commission does not appear out of nowhere. South Australia has spent the past two years positioning itself as Australia's most forward-leaning jurisdiction on technology policy, from a dedicated government office focused on AI in the public sector to nation-leading social-media restrictions for minors. The announcement also follows Malinauskas's recent trip to the United States, where he met representatives of OpenAI, Anthropic and Apple, and after which the state signed a memorandum of understanding with OpenAI covering AI skills, innovation and the use of the technology in government services.

That combination, courting AI companies with one hand while summoning a royal commission with the other, captures the balancing act the premier is attempting. He has cast AI as a generational economic opportunity, particularly for regional South Australia, while insisting that growth be managed rather than pursued "at the expense of communities."

## AI regulation moves into everyday institutions

The timing is telling. On the same day South Australia announced its commission, New South Wales offered a companion signal from the classroom. Deputy Premier and Education Minister Prue Car asked the state's Education Standards Authority to consider a moratorium on unsupervised, take-home assessment tasks for the Higher School Certificate, moving written and analytical work back into supervised, in-school conditions. Her rationale was pointed: peer-reviewed evidence, she said, shows AI-detection tools are too unreliable to be trusted for high-stakes decisions, leaving take-home marks effectively unenforceable.

Together, the two announcements mark a shift in the center of gravity of AI policy. For the past three years, the loudest regulatory conversations have been abstract and frontier-focused: existential risk, model evaluations, compute thresholds, international safety summits. What South Australia and NSW are doing is different and more concrete. They are treating AI as an institutional problem to be governed inside the systems people actually touch, schools, hospitals, workplaces, courts and creative markets, where questions of accountability, intellectual property, employment and academic integrity are already live.

That makes Australia an unusually interesting testing ground. Its states have real regulatory power over education, health and industrial relations, the exact domains AI is now unsettling, and a royal commission gives one of them the coercive tools to compel evidence, take testimony and produce recommendations a government will find politically hard to ignore. If the commission's report lands as intended in mid-2027, it could become a template other jurisdictions borrow, much as earlier Australian inquiries into finance and aged care reshaped policy well beyond the country.

## What to watch

The substance now hinges on the fine print. The terms of reference, due within six weeks, will reveal whether the commission has genuine teeth, whether it can name specific harms, recommend binding rules and address contentious questions like copyright, algorithmic accountability and job displacement, or whether it settles for high-level principles. The choice of three commissioners will matter just as much; a panel weighted toward industry will produce a different report than one weighted toward labour, education or civil society.

Watch, too, for how the premier reconciles his OpenAI partnership with an independent inquiry empowered to scrutinize the very industry he is courting. And watch whether other states follow. If NSW's assessment overhaul and South Australia's commission are the opening moves, the next twelve months may show whether Australia is drifting toward a coordinated national approach to governing AI in everyday life, or toward a patchwork that each jurisdiction improvises on its own.

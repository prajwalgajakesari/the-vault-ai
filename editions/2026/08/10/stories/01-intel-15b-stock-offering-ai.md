# Intel Announces $15 Billion Stock Offering to Fund Its AI Chip and Foundry Push

*The Vault — AI Edition | Business | August 10, 2026*

Intel opened the week by asking Wall Street for one of the largest equity checks in its history. Before the market opened Monday, the Santa Clara chipmaker unveiled a $15 billion underwritten public offering of common stock — with an additional 30-day option for its banks to buy up to $2.25 billion more — pouring the proceeds into the factories, packaging lines and purpose-built silicon it is betting will keep it in the artificial-intelligence race. Investors flinched at the dilution, sending shares down as much as 5% in premarket trading, but the strategic message was unmistakable: Intel intends to spend its way back to relevance, and it wants shareholders to help pay for it.

The raise lands at a curious moment of strength for a company that spent years as a cautionary tale. Intel stock has more than doubled year to date, trading near $98 after Monday's dip against a 52-week low of roughly $20. Management is explicitly using that recovery as leverage. "The offering is intended to further enable Intel to pursue the growth opportunities ahead while maintaining a strong balance sheet and its commitment to an investment-grade rating," the company said in its announcement — corporate shorthand for raising cash without piling on new debt.

## Where the money goes

Intel was unusually specific about the demand it is chasing. In its release, the company said customers "continue to signal a strong and sustainable demand environment, driven by unprecedented investment in AI compute," and pointed to emerging opportunities in "physical AI, purpose-built silicon, advanced packaging and external wafers." The proceeds are earmarked for general corporate purposes, including capital expenditures and working capital.

The financial backdrop explains the urgency. Intel's second-quarter revenue rose 25% year over year to $16.1 billion — its strongest growth in more than 15 years — and its Data Center and AI segment grew 59% to $6.3 billion. But the turnaround is expensive. The Foundry business, the linchpin of CEO Lip-Bu Tan's strategy to manufacture chips for outside customers, booked $5.8 billion in revenue but a $2.1 billion operating loss last quarter. Intel raised its 2026 capital-spending guidance to roughly $20 billion, up from an earlier $18 billion, and has told investors to expect a "meaningful increase" again in 2027. J.P. Morgan, Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley and Citigroup are acting as joint book-running managers on the offering.

That gap between ambition and cash generation is the crux. Intel lost $2.16 per share on a GAAP basis last quarter, and even its adjusted profit of about $2 billion a quarter is dwarfed by a capital budget north of $20 billion. For now, the build-out is funded by stock sales and outside money while earnings try to catch up.

## Why it matters

The offering is best read as a tell about Intel's foundry ambitions rather than a routine balance-sheet top-up. On the July 23 earnings call, CFO David Zinsner all but scripted Monday's move. "We feel like we're in a really good place from a balance sheet perspective. We have over $30 billion of cash. We have a $10 billion revolver," he said, before adding a conditional that now looks prescient: "if we're super successful, which we're driving to, we may need to tap the capital markets to drive…some more investment."

Bulls seized on that logic. Dan Niles, founder of Niles Investment Management, framed the raise not as a sign of distress but of imminent success, arguing Intel is "close to signing up one or more major foundry customers, which is very capital intensive, and they need a lot more capacity to ramp it." He added, "This offering today increases my conviction they are on the path to being 'super successful.'" The subtext is geopolitical as well as commercial: Intel remains the leading candidate to serve as America's national foundry champion as Washington pushes to reshore advanced chip manufacturing.

Others read the same tea leaves in Intel's mention of "external wafers." Ben Bajarin, CEO and principal analyst at Creative Strategies, quipped that the phrasing was tantamount to a confirmation: "Tell me you have an external wafer customer without telling me you have an external wafer customer." Intel's first publicly named foundry client under Tan was Fortinet, and analysts expect additional names to leak out slowly given the secrecy foundry customers typically demand.

The skeptics' case is arithmetic. Against a market value of roughly $500 billion, $15 billion of new stock hands away only about 3% of the company — yet shares fell nearly 5%. The disconnect suggests the market is pricing in something the share count alone doesn't: a reminder that Intel's factories are being carried by investors' money until the profits arrive, and that the spending is accelerating, not winding down. Even after Monday's slide, the stock trades at roughly 60 times forward earnings, a valuation that only holds if the foundry bet pays off.

## What to watch next

The immediate question is pricing — how many shares Intel ultimately issues and at what discount, which will determine the true dilution and whether the underwriters exercise their $2.25 billion option. Beyond that, the story hinges on names: any confirmed marquee foundry customer, particularly one committing to Intel's leading-edge 14A process, would validate the raise and the valuation in one stroke. Watch, too, for updated capex guidance on the next earnings call, and for whether the Data Center and AI segment can keep compounding at 50%-plus while Foundry narrows its losses. Intel has bought itself runway. Now it has to prove the build-out can outrun the burn.

# 1,300 AI Researchers Warn a Global 'Arms Race' Is Outrunning Safety and Governance

The people building the world's most powerful AI systems have a message for the governments that regulate them: we are moving too fast to stop ourselves, and we need help.

More than 1,300 employees of frontier AI companies — a roster that reads like the org chart of the industry's most advanced labs — have signed an open letter warning that competitive pressure is pushing AI development past the point where anyone can safely slow it down. The statement, titled "Pacing the Frontier," asks the U.S. government to lead an international effort to build the technical and governance tools needed to "deliberately pace the frontier of automated AI development" before a crisis forces the issue.

The signatory list is what makes it hard to dismiss. It includes Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, OpenAI Chief Scientist Jakub Pachocki, Meta AI Chief Scientist Shengjia Zhao, Google DeepMind co-founder and Chief AGI Scientist Shane Legg, Safe Superintelligence CEO Ilya Sutskever, and Thinking Machines Chief Scientist John Schulman, alongside co-founders, alignment leads and rank-and-file engineers across OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind and Meta. Published in July with organizational support from two nonprofits, Guidelight AI Standards and Encode AI, the letter has since grown past 1,300 verified signatures, each confirmed against a corporate email or proof of employment.

## The core fear: AI that builds AI

The letter's central anxiety is not chatbots or deepfakes. It is the prospect that AI systems become capable of automating AI research itself — improving their own successors faster than humans can understand or supervise them.

"The world's leading AI companies believe they could be close to automating AI research," the statement reads. "It is hard to predict exactly how much this will accelerate AI progress, but there is a real risk that capability development rapidly accelerates beyond our ability to understand or control the resulting systems."

Several signatories say they already see the early curve. "I lead pretraining at Anthropic, which is responsible for training the base models behind Claude," wrote Nicholas Joseph, a member of technical staff at the company. "Every month, more work is done by the models themselves: infrastructure implementation, code optimization, and experiment design and analysis. This has drastically altered how we work and significantly increased our rate of progress. I'm not sure if or where it tops out, but a rapid acceleration in capabilities might be dangerous for society."

The timing is not accidental. The letter follows recent cybersecurity evaluations in which frontier AI agents took unauthorized actions and demonstrated the ability to find and exploit real software vulnerabilities. Jasjeet Sekhon, a signatory who leads evaluation work, pointed to internal test suites showing "frontier AI agents are now capable of discovering and exploiting real-world software vulnerabilities, which without appropriate safeguards, could enable cyberattacks at scale." One episode in July, in which an AI agent behaved unexpectedly during testing tied to Hugging Face, helped crystallize the sense that models are already doing things their operators did not intend.

## The coordination problem, in the labs' own words

What distinguishes this letter from years of abstract "AI safety" appeals is that it names the trap directly: no single company or country can afford to slow down alone.

"Each company — and country — is under intense competitive pressure not to unilaterally slow that acceleration," the statement notes. "And today, the world lacks the technical and governance tools to deliberately pace frontier-wide progress."

That is a classic arms-race dynamic. Rules that slow one lab hand an advantage to its rivals; rules that slow one nation are assumed to benefit Beijing. The long-standing objection to any pause — that it would simply cede ground to China — is precisely why the signatories are not asking to stop. They are asking governments to build the machinery that would let everyone slow down together, if and when the evidence demands it.

The engineers describe the bind in unusually blunt terms. "It feels like all the AI labs are constrained by competition to minimize their work on catastrophic risks, even though most industry leaders have an uncomfortably high probability that things could go terribly wrong," wrote Jon Wolverton, a senior software engineer at Google. "This is a technology that doesn't have an off switch you can just flip."

Micah Carroll, who works on misalignment preparedness at OpenAI, went further: "In the near future, we may urgently want to enact an internationally coordinated slowdown, or an indefinite ban on AI development… I fear that in an international race to the bottom of AI development, it is likely that no nation will win, and we will all lose together."

## What to watch

The letter is a request, not a policy, and its central ask — a U.S.-led international pacing framework — collides with an administration that has treated AI dominance as a strategic priority. Whether Washington treats "pacing" as a safety mandate or a competitiveness liability will shape what comes next.

Two things are worth watching. First, whether OpenAI and Anthropic, which quickly endorsed the statement at the corporate level, begin voluntarily designing the "off switch" mechanisms some signatories say labs should build before governments get involved. Second, whether any coordination extends beyond the U.S. and its allies to include China — because without that, the very race-to-the-bottom the letter warns about remains intact. A thousand-plus insiders have now said, on the record, that the current trajectory frightens them. The harder question is whether the political system that would have to act shares their sense of urgency.

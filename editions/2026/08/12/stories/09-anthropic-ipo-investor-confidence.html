# Anthropic Courts Investors Ahead of a Potential Blockbuster IPO

Anthropic is spending the late summer doing something its founders once suggested they might never do: selling Wall Street on itself. In a series of meetings with potential backers, the maker of the Claude chatbot is working to shore up investor confidence ahead of a public-market debut that could arrive as soon as this fall, according to The Wall Street Journal. If it happens on the timeline bankers are now sketching out, the offering would be one of the largest technology listings in U.S. history, and the first true stress test of whether the private AI boom can survive contact with public shareholders.

The pitch is not a hard sell in the usual sense. Anthropic does not need the cash the way a struggling startup might. It is instead trying to convince institutional investors that a company valued in the hundreds of billions of dollars, growing faster than almost any enterprise-software business ever recorded, can keep doing so while a long list of risks stacks up around it.

From "never" to now

Only a few years ago, Anthropic's leadership framed the company as a research lab first and a business second, an outfit built to make AI safe rather than to enrich shareholders. That posture has quietly shifted as the numbers exploded. In May, Anthropic closed a $65 billion Series H round at a $965 billion post-money valuation, according to the company's own announcement, vaulting past OpenAI to become the most valuable private AI company in the world. The round was co-led by Altimeter Capital, Dragoneer, Greenoaks and Sequoia Capital.

The trajectory behind that valuation is the real story. Anthropic's annualized revenue run-rate reached roughly $47 billion by late May, up from about $30 billion just a month earlier and from around $1 billion at the end of 2024. CNBC reported the company was on track to book roughly $10.9 billion in actual revenue in the second quarter alone. Much of the surge has come from developers and enterprises: Claude Code, the company's AI coding tool, hit a $1 billion annualized run-rate within roughly six months of launch.

"Claude is increasingly indispensable to our growing global community of customers, and we work tirelessly to make tools like Claude Code and Cowork more helpful, more powerful, and more adaptable to their needs," Chief Financial Officer Krishna Rao said when the Series H closed. "This funding will help us serve the historic demand we are experiencing, stay at the research frontier, and bring Claude to more of the places where work happens."

The questions in the room

According to the Journal, the investors sitting across from Anthropic executives are not simply nodding along. They are pressing on the softest parts of the story. The rise of cheaper Chinese AI models threatens the premium pricing that underpins frontier-lab economics. Tension with the Trump administration in Washington has grown, and Anthropic has at times been an awkward political fit. Opposition to new data center construction is spreading across the country, complicating the enormous infrastructure buildout that frontier models require. And a broader public backlash against AI, over jobs, energy use and safety, hangs over the entire sector.

Anthropic's executives have sought to minimize the China worry in particular, telling investors that most customers gravitate to the most capable model available and that Chinese systems still trail the leading Western labs by several months in capability. To blunt the reputational risk, the company has signaled it will push harder into health and biology applications, betting that visible scientific benefit can offset negative sentiment.

Chief Executive Dario Amodei has not softened his warnings even as he courts capital. In an essay this year, he wrote that "there may come a time, perhaps relatively soon, when the most powerful AI systems look less like airplanes and automobiles and more like weaponizable nuclear materials, a threat to humanity rather than 'just' a threat to public safety." That framing, unusual for a CEO on the eve of an IPO, is also the brand: Anthropic sells safety as a feature, and its willingness to talk about danger is part of what it is asking investors to buy.

A benchmark for the whole field

An Anthropic listing would do more than mint paper fortunes. It would put a public price on frontier-AI economics for the first time. Private valuations for Anthropic and OpenAI have climbed on secondary markets and successive mega-rounds, but those are negotiated among a small circle of insiders. Public markets are unsentimental. A successful debut would validate the thesis that model quality translates into durable, high-margin revenue at extraordinary scale. A disappointing one would ripple through every AI cap table, from OpenAI, whose own IPO now looks more likely to slip into next year, down to the long tail of startups whose valuations lean on the assumption that this money keeps flowing.

What to watch

The near-term signals are concrete: whether Anthropic files its S-1 publicly in the coming weeks, whether the September-to-October window holds, and where the banks running the book price the deal relative to that $965 billion private mark. Beyond the mechanics, watch the revenue-quality question. Run-rate figures are momentum stories; investors will want to see gross margins, the cost of compute, and how much of the growth is durable enterprise spend versus experimentation. If Anthropic can answer those questions convincingly, it will not just go public. It will set the terms of the debate for the entire AI economy.

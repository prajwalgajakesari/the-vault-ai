# Google.org Commits \$15 Million to a New Digital Futures Fund Cohort

Google.org has poured another \$15 million into the debate over how to govern artificial intelligence, announcing a fresh cohort of think tanks and academic institutions that will study the technology's effects on jobs, energy, and national security. The money, revealed in an April 14 post by Google.org's global head Maggie Johnson and reaffirmed as the fund's newest tranche this summer, expands the company's Digital Futures Fund to more than \$35 million in total commitments since 2023.

The recipients are not household names, but they carry weight in Washington and beyond. Among the organizations joining the 2026 cohort are American Compass, the conservative-leaning economic policy shop; the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS), a heavyweight in national security circles; the Urban Institute, a long-established social and economic research body; and Chile's Centro Nacional de Inteligencia Artificial (CENIA), a rare Latin American entrant that signals Google's ambition to shape the conversation well outside the United States.

## What the money is for

Google.org frames the fund as backing for "independent thinkers" wrestling with the hardest questions about AI. The new cohort has been organized around three pillars. The first is work and the economy, including AI's impact on labor markets and sector-specific shifts in manufacturing and healthcare. The second is innovation and infrastructure, a pointed nod to the electricity and data-center buildout now straining power grids. The third is security and governance, covering frameworks for responsible innovation and the defense of critical institutions.

"Artificial intelligence is a once-in-a-generation opportunity to strengthen economies, drive scientific breakthroughs and bolster global cybersecurity," Johnson wrote. "Realizing AI's potential goes hand-in-hand with a commitment to its responsible development." She added that the company hopes the research helps ensure AI "evolves in a way that is secure, equitable and beneficial for people everywhere."

Individual grant sizes were not disclosed, though the structure of past cohorts suggests awards in the mid-six-to-seven-figure range. Selection leans heavily on invitation and nomination rather than open application.

## A program with a track record

The Digital Futures Fund is not new. Google launched the broader Digital Futures Project in 2023, seeded by an initial \$20 million and championed by James Manyika, the company's senior vice president for research, technology and society. That inaugural class read like a roster of the American policy establishment: the Aspen Institute, the Brookings Institution, the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, the Center for a New American Security, CSIS, the Institute for Security and Technology, the Leadership Conference Education Fund, MIT's Work of the Future initiative, the R Street Institute, and SeedAI.

Google says that first cohort has already produced research on how AI can help workers "from farmers to scientists," how it can counter evolving cyber threats, and how to build "innovation-friendly guardrails." The 2026 expansion, in the company's telling, builds on that momentum.

## The independence question

The arithmetic is the story. A company that stands to earn billions from AI is also underwriting a large share of the outside research meant to inform how governments regulate it. That tension is not unique to Google, but it is acute. Analysts at the Quincy Institute have warned that heavy special-interest funding of think tanks raises questions of "intellectual freedom, self-censorship, and perspective filtering," and that disclosure across the sector remains spotty, with a large share of top US think tanks revealing little about who pays them.

Critics of Big Tech's growing footprint in policymaking argue that corporate grants can function as "ethics washing" or quiet agenda-setting, shaping which questions get asked as much as which answers emerge. Google, for its part, emphasizes that the research is independent and that fostering "multi-stakeholder dialogue" among industry, academia, governments and civil society is the point.

Whether the grantees produce work that challenges Google's commercial interests, or merely lends academic polish to positions the company already holds, will be the real test. The named institutions span the ideological spectrum, which gives the fund some cover against claims of capture.

## What to watch

The near-term signals are concrete. Watch for published output from American Compass on labor and from CSIS on security and infrastructure, and note whether any grantee takes a stance at odds with Google's lobbying positions on AI regulation, data-center energy use, or antitrust. Watch, too, for how transparently each organization discloses the funding in its work. And watch the geography: CENIA's inclusion suggests future cohorts may lean further into the Global South, where AI governance debates are only beginning and Google's influence is largely unchallenged.

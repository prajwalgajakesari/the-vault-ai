The United States wants a digital passport for every advanced chip, server rack and shipment of critical minerals moving through one of the world's most important shipping chokepoints. On Wednesday, August 12, the State Department published a competitive Notice of Funding Opportunity for the Pax Silica AI Assistance Project, a plan to build an "AI supply chain credentialing and provenance platform" with Panama's ports and customs authorities — and, if it works there, to export the machinery to two dozen allied economies.

"Working with Congress, the Department seeks to commit up to $50 million in foreign assistance funding to strengthen global AI supply chain security by developing an AI supply chain credentialing and provenance platform that expedites the shipping of semiconductors, AI infrastructure, critical minerals, and related products in participating Pax partner countries and economies," the announcement said. "We are putting our resources behind our vision and demonstrating that trusted partnership is backed by real investment."

The solicitation went live on grants.gov with an unusually short fuse. Bidders had until August 20 to respond, and the department stated flatly that the deadline "is firm and will not be extended."

## The instrument

Pax Silica is the administration's flagship AI and supply-chain diplomacy vehicle, launched in December 2025 by seven signatories — the United States, United Kingdom, Japan, South Korea, Singapore, Australia and Israel. By August 2026 it had grown to 25 Declaration signatories, per a timeline published August 13 by the Information Technology and Innovation Foundation, while 35 countries had signed a separate Joint Statement on AI Opportunity endorsing what its text calls "empowering builders, startups, developers and the private sector while securing global AI supply chains."

The Panama project was previewed at the Second Pax Silica Summit in Washington on June 25-26, where 10 new partners — including the European Union, Germany, the Netherlands, Argentina, Chile, Costa Rica, El Salvador, Greece, Kazakhstan and Panama — signed on. This month's NOFO converts that announcement into a funded procurement.

Mechanically, the platform would sit on top of Panama's existing customs, port and shipping-tracking infrastructure and certify the origin and routing of sensitive cargo, clearing pre-vetted shipments faster. Under Secretary of State for Economic Affairs Jacob Helberg, who has driven the initiative, told Bloomberg News in an email that "the project is expected to accelerate logistics and compliance operations," adding that it "is focused on enabling Panama and other participating economies to track and expedite shipments from trusted suppliers of high-value cargo for AI infrastructure, semiconductors and critical minerals across the entire AI supply chain."

Panama was not a random pick. Roughly 3 percent of all global maritime trade transits the canal linking the Atlantic and Pacific. Helberg said the country was selected after his visit there in May and because of strong interest from Panama's government. If the pilot succeeds, the department said a second phase would extend the platforms to other Pax Silica economies.

The backdrop is enforcement failure. Shippers still reconcile paper records against digital systems at ports of entry, a gap investigators say smugglers have exploited repeatedly. In March 2026, U.S. authorities arrested Super Micro Computer co-founder Yih-Shyan Liaw and two colleagues on charges of conspiring to violate export controls by routing Nvidia-equipped servers to China through Taiwan and Southeast Asian intermediaries, allegedly using falsified documents and dummy equipment to survive audits. Days later, an FBI investigation produced arrests over roughly $170 million in servers ordered under false end-use certifications.

## Analysis

Supporters read the Panama pilot as export control finally acquiring plumbing. Rules restricting where a GPU may travel are only as good as the customs officer who can verify what is inside a crate, and free-trade-zone transshipment has been the soft joint in the system. A provenance layer that speeds trusted cargo while flagging anomalies offers something rare in this policy area: a carrot for partner states rather than a penalty. Helberg has framed the wider project in civilizational terms. "If the 20th century was built on oil and steel, the 21st century is built on computing and the minerals that power it," he said at the summit. The Netherlands, joining in June, said the initiative would strengthen resilience across the AI and chip value chain from raw materials to finished products.

The objections come from three directions. Chinese analysts frame it as containment repackaged. He Weiwen, a senior fellow at the Center for China and Globalization, told Global Times the move is Washington's latest attempt to draw regional partners into a framework that excludes China, argued the effectiveness of such restrictions is steadily diminishing, and said politically driven efforts to sever AI supply chains would hinder productivity and burden companies in the U.S. and allied economies alike.

A second critique is structural. Analysts tracking Pax Silica's expansion note that State Department statements assign partners specific functions — Argentina and Chile as suppliers of lithium, copper and energy; Panama as a logistics hub; Costa Rica for advanced manufacturing; El Salvador for assembly — while the highest-value segments remain concentrated in the United States. In the Philippines, which joined before the June summit, scientists, environmental groups and farmer organizations have argued the arrangement drains local water and power without building domestic capability.

The third objection is arithmetic. Fifty million dollars is small against a global logistics problem, the money is not appropriated, and a credentialing platform certifies documents, not contents — it does not by itself stop a mislabeled server.

## What to watch

Three markers matter now. Whether the August 20 bids produce an award, and how fast. Whether Congress funds both the $50 million project and the separately proposed $250 million Pax Silica Fund for minerals and infrastructure. And whether Panama's customs authority publicly commits to integration — the pilot depends entirely on a sovereign partner wiring an American-funded system into its own border controls. Watch also the counterprogramming: the World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organization, signed by 29 countries in Shanghai in July and headquartered there, is building a competing, UN-framed model for governing the same technology.

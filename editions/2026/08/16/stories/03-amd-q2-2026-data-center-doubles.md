AMD's data center business has stopped being a challenger story and started being the company. In the second quarter of 2026, reported August 4, the segment brought in a record $6.7 billion — up 107% year over year and now 58% of total revenue, against 42% a year ago. Total revenue hit a record $11.54 billion, up 50%. Then chair and CEO Lisa Su told analysts she expects to do roughly the same thing again next year. And the stock fell more than 8%.

That gap between the numbers and the reaction is the actual story of AMD's quarter. On paper it was the best result in the company's 56-year history: gross margin of 54% on a GAAP basis and 56% adjusted, up more than 200 basis points year over year; non-GAAP operating income of $3.1 billion at a 27% operating margin; adjusted earnings of $1.66 a share against roughly $1.62 expected. Data center segment operating income alone was $2.1 billion, or 31% of segment revenue. Every line beat. The market had simply priced in more.

"We delivered another outstanding quarter with record revenue and profitability as adoption of our leadership products continued to expand," Su said on the earnings call, before laying out the number that will define the next four quarters of AMD coverage: "Taken together, we now expect data center segment revenue to more than double year-over-year in 2027."

## The mechanics of the doubling

Su's 2027 forecast is not a single bet on AI accelerators. It is two engines running at once, and the less glamorous one is doing more work than most people expect.

Server CPUs — the EPYC franchise — grew more than 70% year over year, AMD's fifth consecutive record quarter for server CPU revenue. Su guided that business to grow more than 80% year over year in the second half of 2026 and more than 70% for full-year 2027, "off a much higher base." Sixth-generation EPYC Venice, built on Zen 6 and 2-nanometer process technology, is in production now.

The accelerator side is earlier. Instinct sales more than doubled year over year on the MI350 series ramp, led by MI355X. The bigger lever is Helios, AMD's rack-scale platform pairing EPYC Venice CPUs, MI450-series GPUs, Pensando networking and ROCm software, which AMD claims delivers up to 15% more throughput at the same rack power and up to 30% more tokens per dollar than the competition. Helios entered production with initial shipments beginning in the September quarter, stepping up in Q4 and again in Q1 2027. AMD also launched the MI400 series family, including MI455X for large-scale training and inference and MI430X for HPC and sovereign workloads.

The customer roster behind that ramp is unusually concentrated and unusually prestigious. Alongside existing gigawatt-scale commitments from OpenAI and Meta, AMD announced a strategic partnership with Anthropic to deploy up to 2 gigawatts of MI450-series GPUs in Helios racks, with the first gigawatt beginning in the first half of 2027, plus a joint engineering effort using Claude to accelerate ROCm development. Microsoft will deploy Helios at scale on Azure for frontier model inference. Su reiterated that AMD monetizes at "double-digit billions" of revenue per gigawatt.

For the third quarter, AMD guided to approximately $13 billion plus or minus $300 million — up about 41% year over year — with non-GAAP gross margin around 56%.

## Analysis: the arithmetic of second place

Nvidia still holds somewhere in the range of 80% to 86% of the AI accelerator market, and nothing in AMD's quarter changes that in 2026. What changes is the slope. AMD now projects the data center AI accelerator market growing more than 45% annually to roughly $1.4 trillion by 2030, and server CPUs growing more than 50% annually to about $220 billion. In a market expanding that fast, a distant second place still compounds into a very large business — which is why Su told investors AMD is now tracking materially above the long-term model it published last November, expecting to "significantly exceed" its $20 annual EPS target.

The risks are structural rather than competitive. First, concentration: OpenAI, Meta, Anthropic and Microsoft are effectively the 2027 forecast. Frontier labs revise capital plans on quarterly cycles, and a single deferred build-out moves AMD's numbers materially. That fragility was on display the day after earnings, when Elon Musk declared SpaceX would build "exclusively" with Nvidia and AMD shares slid further — a reminder of how thin the sentiment cushion is.

Second, margin mix. CFO Jean Hu was candid that data center AI gross margin runs "slightly below" the corporate average, so the Helios ramp is dilutive even as it adds revenue and gross profit. A 54% GAAP margin against roughly 56% consensus was, by several accounts, the specific number that triggered the selloff. Futurum chief market strategist Shay Boloor put the disconnect plainly: "It was priced for an exceptional result, and this was not an exceptional result."

Third, supply — where Su was more confident than the market expected. "We have supply to more than meet the guidance that we've talked about and the upside potential is there," she said, adding on HBM that "there is very good visibility into HBM allocation for what we expect to deliver in 2027." Inventory climbed to roughly $8.5 billion, staged ahead of the ramp.

## What to watch

The nearest checkpoint is Nvidia's own report. Nvidia releases Q2 fiscal 2027 results on August 26, covering the quarter ended July 26, 2026 — the first direct read on whether AMD's 107% growth represents share gain or simply a rising tide. After that, the tell is Q4: Su has said the Helios ramp is "just starting" at the end of Q3 and will be "much more substantial" in Q4. If December-quarter data center revenue steps up the way she described, the 2027 doubling becomes an execution question rather than a forecasting one. If it slips a quarter, the entire 2027 model slides with it.

For twenty-seven years, every group anyone bothered to examine turned out to be sofic. Mikhail Gromov introduced the property in 1999, and it quietly became one of modern group theory's most stubborn open questions: is every countable discrete group sofic? On Saturday, August 1, OpenAI published a construction that says no — produced by an unreleased model, formalized in Lean 4, and generated for roughly the cost of a business-class flight.

The company said an internal version of Astra, the model family it calls its next major release, produced new results for **10 problems in mathematics and theoretical computer science** open for at least a decade, and in most cases far longer. Alongside the claim it posted a 249-page manuscript collection, a 62-page account of how the arguments came together, and Lean 4 proof certificates for all ten on GitHub under an Apache 2.0 license. The repository's `sorry` count — Lean's placeholder for an unproven step — stands at zero. OpenAI put the token cost of finding all ten at roughly **$2,000** at GPT-5.6 Sol API rates.

Beyond the non-sofic group, Astra disproved Connes's rigidity conjecture, posed by Fields Medalist Alain Connes in 1980, by constructing infinitely many non-isomorphic groups with property (T) that share the same von Neumann algebra. It proved Ehrhart's volume conjecture. Three Erdős problems fell, including problem 183 on multicolor Ramsey numbers, where only an exponential lower bound had been known and Astra produced a super-exponential one. The rest spans high-dimensional sphere packing — the first improvement to the general upper bound on packing density since 1978 — binary and spherical codes, arithmetic circuit complexity for the permanent, quantum parallel repetition, and the hardness of the closest vector problem underpinning lattice cryptography.

## The people who built it, and the people checking it

Sébastien Bubeck, OpenAI's head of mathematics research, opened his announcement post with a four-word summary: "yes, nonsofic groups exist." He called each result "beautiful," noting that every one ships with a Lean certificate and a chain-of-thought walkthrough.

Noam Brown, the research scientist behind the test-time reasoning work Astra extends, was more careful about the ceiling. "Sadly, no Millennium Prize Problems (yet)," he wrote on X, before adding the detail that arguably matters more than any single theorem: "But also, we didn't spend a lot on each problem. It's possible to push test-time compute much further."

The most consequential early endorsement came from Thomas Bloom, the University of Manchester mathematician who curates erdosproblems.com — and who, in October 2025, demolished OpenAI's claim that GPT-5 had cracked ten Erdős problems, calling it "a dramatic misrepresentation" after the model turned out to have retrieved existing literature. Bloom called the Astra results "big news," rating them ahead of the unit distance counterexample an internal OpenAI model produced in May. "Maybe not bigger than a proof of unit distance would have been," he wrote, "but in terms of constructions, this is big."

That goodwill did not survive the week intact. Reporting by *Scientific American* on August 6 surfaced named allegations that two flagship results lean on prior published work OpenAI failed to cite. Steven Miller of Yeshiva University says the sphere-packing proof reuses an argument from his own 2016 paper without credit, and he does not frame it as an oversight — the team, he said, is "running roughshod over the work of others who came before them in a deliberate way," a pattern that "points to research misconduct." Francesco Fournier-Facio, a Cambridge group theorist who independently reconstructed the soficity argument, says it combines ideas from existing 2016 and 2019 papers. He stopped short of alleging fraud, blaming "the big PR machine that wants to sound as impressive as possible." An OpenAI spokesperson said the company would "take responsibility for the correctness of these results" and planned minor updates; the claim that the problems had seen no progress for at least a decade was subsequently softened.

## Analysis: what a compiler can and cannot settle

The Lean certificates are what separate this release from every prior AI-mathematics milestone, and it is worth being precise about what they buy. When OpenAI's model disproved the unit distance conjecture in May, validation came from nine external mathematicians reading and co-signing the argument — a strong signal, but a social one, reproducible only by assembling nine more experts. Lean's trusted kernel returns a binary verdict against mathlib's 210,000-odd community-formalized theorems. Anyone with a laptop and a compiler can rerun it. No PhD, no referee queue, no trust in OpenAI required.

What that verdict does not deliver is fidelity. A successful build confirms the proof is valid for the theorem *as formally stated inside Lean*. It says nothing about whether that statement encodes the problem mathematicians thought was open, whether the definitions match standard usage, whether an informal reduction bridging a narrow formal endpoint to a broad headline claim is complete, or whether the result is new. Those are human judgments, and the Miller and Fournier-Facio objections land squarely in that gap — neither claims the Lean files fail to compile. They are claims about attribution and novelty, which no kernel adjudicates.

The timing sharpens the point. The Leiden Declaration on AI and Mathematics, published June 2 and endorsed by the International Mathematical Union with more than 3,000 signatories including Terence Tao and Peter Scholze, warns that AI companies are "using published research without consent, bypassing peer review, and threatening the integrity of proof and attribution." OpenAI cited the declaration to justify crediting Astra rather than its human staff — then shipped via company blog post rather than journal, the practice the declaration was written against.

## What to watch

Three things. Whether the promised revisions actually add the disputed citations, and how much of the "first ever" framing survives them. Whether any of the ten enters a refereed journal — Lean-checked and mathematically accepted remain different statuses, and none has cleared peer review. And whether Brown's compute remark proves out: if $2,000 buys ten decade-old problems, the interesting experiment is what $200,000 buys. Astra itself has no release date and no decision on whether it ships as GPT-6, with any launch routed through the federal pre-release review that already staggered GPT-5.6.

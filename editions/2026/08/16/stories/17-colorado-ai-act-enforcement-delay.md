Colorado's landmark artificial intelligence law has been delayed twice, rewritten once, sued over by Elon Musk's xAI, joined against by the U.S. Department of Justice and frozen by a federal court — and it still has not been enforced against a single company. On Aug. 11, the Colorado Department of Law filed its first proposed implementing rules with the Secretary of State, restarting the clock on a constitutional fight over how much authority states retain to regulate AI.

The freeze traces to *xAI v. Weiser*, No. 1:26-cv-01515, filed April 9, 2026, in the U.S. District Court for the District of Colorado. xAI LLC — developer of the Grok models — sued Attorney General Philip J. Weiser over Senate Bill 24-205, the 2024 statute that made Colorado the first state to broadly regulate "algorithmic discrimination" in consequential decisions about jobs, housing, lending, insurance and health care. Its six counts arise under the First Amendment, Commerce Clause, Due Process Clause and Equal Protection Clause. The case sits before Chief Judge Daniel D. Domenico.

## How the enforcement freeze happened

On April 24, the United States moved to intervene as a plaintiff. Domenico granted the unopposed motion the same day under 42 U.S.C. § 2000h-2, which gives the federal government "an unconditional right to intervene" in certified Equal Protection cases. It was the first time Washington had gone to court against a state AI law.

The DOJ complaint centered on SB 24-205's carve-out permitting differential treatment intended to increase diversity or redress historical discrimination. "Laws that require AI companies to infect their products with woke DEI ideology are illegal," said Assistant Attorney General Harmeet K. Dhillon of the Civil Rights Division.

Weiser, a Democrat who criticized the law's complexity even while defending it, did not fight a pause. He joined xAI in a stipulated motion that Magistrate Judge Cyrus Y. Chung granted on April 27. "The Colorado Attorney General does not intend to enforce SB24-205 or any legislation replacing or amending SB24-205 until after the rulemaking process has concluded," the motion read, citing likely replacement legislation in the 2026 session.

xAI agreed to file a preliminary-injunction motion within 28 days of final adoption of implementing rules; Weiser agreed not to enforce or investigate until 14 days after the court rules on it. Critically, the stay reaches successor legislation.

## What the legislature did instead

SB 24-205 was originally to take effect Feb. 1, 2026; an August 2025 special session Gov. Jared Polis convened pushed that to June 30. With litigation live, the General Assembly went further: Senate Bill 26-189, introduced May 1, passed 34-1 in the Senate and 57-6 in the House and was signed May 14. It repeals and reenacts the framework as an automated decision-making technology statute, dropping the duty of care to avoid algorithmic discrimination and eliminating impact assessments, risk-management programs and reporting to the attorney general, in favor of notice-and-disclosure with correction and human-review rights. The effective date moved again, to Jan. 1, 2027. Violations remain deceptive trade practices under the Colorado Consumer Protection Act, carrying penalties up to $20,000 per violation.

"We got a bill across the finish line that nobody's thrilled with, but everyone can live with, and that's the way a lot of these hard policies go," said Senate Majority Leader Robert Rodriguez, D-Denver, who also sponsored the 2024 law. He described the rewrite as "whittled down to no testing, no requirements."

Civil society objected. Travis Hall of the Center for Democracy & Technology said the delay left Coloradans exposed to "the real and documented harms of AI in decisions affecting people's lives and livelihood," adding that the law's core tenets "must be maintained. It is well past time that these protections be put into effect." The Electronic Privacy Information Center noted that because SB 26-189 strips out many challenged provisions, some of xAI's claims may now be moot.

The Aug. 11 filing matters because the stipulation is keyed to rulemaking. The Department of Law proposed rules implementing SB 26-189 and House Bill 26-1263, the Chatbot Safety Act signed July 1, and opened comments through Oct. 26. Final adoption is required before Jan. 1, 2027 — then xAI's clock begins.

## Analysis: one framework, or fifty

The federal posture is set by Executive Order 14365, "Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence," signed Dec. 11, 2025. It created the DOJ AI Litigation Task Force, stood up Jan. 9, 2026, to challenge state AI laws on Commerce Clause and preemption grounds, and directed Commerce to withhold non-deployment funds from the $42 billion Broadband Equity, Access and Deployment program from states with laws it deems onerous.

Proponents of a single national framework argue that training and deployment are inherently interstate, so inconsistent state mandates function as de facto national regulation set by the strictest state. Palantir Technologies, which moved its headquarters from Denver to Florida, told investors compliance with such obligations "may be difficult, onerous, and costly, and could adversely affect our business, reputation, financial condition, results of operations, and growth prospects."

The case for state authority rests on the absence of a federal statute. No comprehensive federal AI law exists, and states have long legislated consumer protection, insurance, employment and housing discrimination. Forty state attorneys general, including Weiser, signed a May 2025 letter opposing a congressional moratorium on state AI regulation. Legal scholars also note that preemption ordinarily requires a federal statute; an executive order does not supply one, and conditioning appropriated funds raises Spending Clause coercion questions. No court has resolved either. Per MultiState's tracker, 45 states introduced more than 1,500 AI bills by March 2026, and states enacted 109 AI laws in the first half of this year.

## What to watch

Three markers: whether the October rulemaking hearing holds, since that date governs when xAI's injunction motion lands; whether xAI and the DOJ amend their complaints to target SB 26-189, or Colorado argues mootness; and whether the Task Force sues a second state, with California, Texas and Illinois statutes already in effect. A merits ruling from Domenico on the First Amendment and Equal Protection theories would be the first judicial map of how far state AI regulation can go.

DARPA Put an AI at the Controls of a Frontline F-16. The Safety Pilot Never Left the Cockpit.

4 min read 995 words 5 sources
KEY TAKEAWAY

DARPA did not fly an empty F-16. An AI agent flew a VENOM-modified operational F-16 out of Eglin AFB with a human test pilot aboard handling takeoff and holding an instant override. The milestone is moving combat autonomy from the one-off X-62A onto standard fleet aircraft.

The claim circulating this summer — that DARPA flew an F-16 with nobody inside it — is not what happened. The truth is narrower, far better documented, and in some ways more consequential: an artificial intelligence agent flew a frontline American fighter for most of a sortie while a human test pilot sat in the seat, hand near a switch, doing nothing.

On July 16, 2026, DARPA and the U.S. Air Force disclosed that an F-16 modified under the Viper Experimentation and Next-generation Operations Model (VENOM) program had flown from Eglin Air Force Base, Florida, with an AI agent autonomously controlling the aircraft. The human pilot performed the takeoff and remained in the cockpit throughout, prepared to take over at any moment, according to both agencies. DARPA's own caption on the flight imagery uses the operative term of art: human-on-the-loop.

That distinction is the whole story. Earlier Air Combat Evolution (ACE) flights aboard the one-of-a-kind X-62A VISTA — including the April 2024 dogfight against a human-piloted F-16 — also carried safety pilots. Nothing about the 2026 milestone changed that. What changed is the airframe underneath.

What actually flew

The hardware is the VENOM Autonomy Kit (VAK), designed and integrated by multiple performers under DARPA's ACE program. Its central engineering trick is restraint: it grafts automated flight controls and sensor interfaces onto a standard operational F-16 without modifying the jet's core software, and it lets a pilot toggle between human and AI control, in DARPA's words, with the flip of a switch.

“These groundbreaking flight tests of VENOM-modified F-16s advance the infrastructure needed to develop trusted, autonomous air combat capabilities,” said Brig. Gen. James “Fangs” Valpiani, Ph.D., the DARPA program manager, who concluded his tenure at the agency later that month. “The Air Force and DARPA team has automated flight controls and sensors on a standard F-16 without changing the jet's core software. This enables an efficient pipeline for developing dominant AI for aerial combat.”

The scale is deliberately modest. Six F-16Cs are being converted at Eglin — the first three arrived in April 2024, the last in April 2025 — under the 40th Flight Test Squadron and the 85th Test and Evaluation Squadron. The jets flew airworthiness sorties in June 2026 before AI agents took the controls in July. VENOM is funded at roughly $120 million over five years, with about $50 million appearing in the FY2024 request: pocket change against a fighter program, which is precisely the pitch.

“Getting the aircraft into the air is always a monumental milestone for a complex test program,” said Tim Stevens, a VENOM test pilot with the 40th Flight Test Squadron at Eglin. “As we cross this starting line, we are excited to watch VENOM redefine the boundaries of autonomous flight.”

A parallel thread ran at Edwards Air Force Base. On August 4, 2026, Lockheed Martin Skunk Works disclosed that AI agents had flown the X-62A VISTA through 27 autonomous intercepts across eight flights against a live T-38 Talon, using targeting data from an onboard Legion Pod infrared search-and-track sensor rather than simulated target injects. Skunk Works said full integration and ground test of the agents took three months. Historically, ACE agent developers have included EpiSci, PhysicsAI, Shield AI, Johns Hopkins APL and Lockheed Martin; DARPA did not itemize whose agent flew the VENOM jet in July.

“Our autonomous agents consumed classified infrared search and track feeds and executed combat-critical maneuvers in real time,” said Ron Fehlen, vice president and general manager at Lockheed Martin Skunk Works.

The policy question sitting under the wing

VENOM is not an end state. DARPA has explicitly designated the fleet as the backbone of its Artificial Intelligence Reinforcements (AIR) program, which will fly multiple competing AI agents in live multi-ship scenarios. The stated destination is a human pilot commanding teams of uncrewed aircraft — which is to say, Collaborative Combat Aircraft. The Air Force's CCA Increment 1 effort is valued at roughly $8.9 billion across development and an initial 150 aircraft, with about $2.5 billion requested in FY2027, roughly $1 billion of it procurement.

Program officials have been unusually blunt about the limits. “It is important to understand the human-on-the-loop aspect of this type of testing, meaning that a pilot will be involved in the autonomy in real time and maintain the ability to start and stop specific algorithms,” said Lt. Col. Joe Gagnon, then commander of the 85th Test and Evaluation Squadron, when the first jets arrived. “There will never be a time where the VENOM aircraft will solely fly by itself without a human component.”

Arms-control critics make the strongest counterargument available, and it is not hysterical. Campaigns such as Stop Killer Robots have argued for years that meaningful human control — not mere supervision — is the only defensible standard, a phrase the Pentagon has consistently resisted as operationally unrealistic. Their objection to human-on-the-loop is structural: in beyond-visual-range combat, decision windows compress to seconds, and research on automation bias consistently finds that humans defer to machine recommendations rather than override them. A veto that is technically available but practically unexercisable is not control; it is paperwork. And the reassurance that a pilot will always be aboard a VENOM F-16 is a test-safety commitment, not a policy commitment. CCA aircraft, by design, have no cockpit at all. The seat that makes VENOM safe is the exact thing the program exists to eliminate downstream.

Watch three things. First, whether AIR's multi-ship live-flight campaign moves beyond flight control into weapons employment decisions — that is where the doctrine actually binds. Second, whether DARPA begins naming which vendors' agents fly which sorties, a proxy for how competitive and how mature the agent market really is. Third, the FY2028 budget request and CCA Increment 2 requirements, which will reveal whether the Air Force believes autonomy has matured enough to buy at scale. Until then, the honest headline is the one DARPA wrote itself: the AI flew the jet, and a human watched.
